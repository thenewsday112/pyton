While mud-coring in Baffin Bay, scientists accidentally found the marks of a mile-thick ice shelf that blocked three massive ice sheets

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

In July 2023, the research vessel Neil Armstrong left Woods Hole, Massachusetts, for Baffin Bay. The scientists on board planned to pull mud cores from the seafloor between Greenland and Canada.

On the way, the ship’s sonar picked up something else. Marks on the slopes of the bay point to a floating ice shelf more than a mile thick, according to the University of Florida. It filled the basin about 20,000 years ago.

The results came out on October 21 in Nature Geoscience. The thickness is the team’s interpretation of features on the seafloor, not a direct measurement of the ice.

Woods Hole to Nuuk in 33 days

The cruise, called AR76-01, ran from July 11 to August 13, 2023, and ended in Nuuk, Greenland. That is according to the official cruise report. It was part of the Baffin Bay Deglacial Experiment, or BADEX, funded by the U.S. National Science Foundation.

Robert Hatfield, an associate professor of geology at the University of Florida, was chief scientist. His co-chiefs were Shannon Klotsko of the University of North Carolina Wilmington (UNCW) and Brendan Reilly of Columbia University.

The team wanted to know whether warmer water at the base of glaciers made the Greenland Ice Sheet retreat after the last ice age. In 33 days the ship worked 27 stations and made 109 deployments. The crew brought up about 1,300 feet (396 meters) of sediment.

The report thanks Captain Kent Sheasley and his mates for their skill “in a challenging sea-ice environment.” In Melville Bugt, a bay off northwest Greenland, the ship worked beside a large ice floe. The scientists wrote that their survey lines there came out like spaghetti.

Eggland on the Upernavik slope

Two Kongsberg multibeam sonars mapped the seafloor as the ship moved. The deeper unit, an EM122, worked in water from about 1,600 to 10,800 feet (500 to 3,300 meters) deep.

Off the Upernavik trough, the sonar showed an abrupt change. Smooth seafloor in deep water gave way to a rough, hummocky surface higher up the slope. In cross section it looked a little like a basket of eggs, and people on board called it “eggland.”

Farther south, off Uummannaq, the team logged a similar boundary. It sat at about 4,330 feet (1,320 meters) of water in the north of the survey and 4,130 feet (1,260 meters) in the south.

The 2023 report does not link these features to an ice shelf, and neither the abstract nor the university release mentions eggland.

A short documentary follows the BADEX science team and crew aboard the R/V Neil Armstrong during the 2023 coring cruise in Baffin Bay. Credit: The Baffin Bay Deglacial Experiment.

Grounded ice a mile down

Back on land, the team compiled their new maps with older depth surveys of Baffin Bay. In the paper’s abstract, the authors say they found signs of ice resting on the continental slope during the last glacial period. They traced the deepest line it reached around most of the bay.

The authors read those features as the work of one ice shelf across the whole basin. After a correction for past sea level, they put its thickness at about 4,600 to 6,900 feet (1,400 to 2,100 meters). They estimated its area at about 58,000 square miles (150,000 square kilometers).

Top left, a perspective view of streamlined lineations and the ice contact boundary on the Baffin Bay slope; bottom left, a seismic profile across that boundary. On the right, conceptual diagrams of how the ice shelf and its keels shaped the slope (not to scale). Credit: Klotsko et al., Nature Geoscience (2026). Click image to enlarge.

According to Hatfield, the depth of the evidence surprised the team most. It lay more than a mile below the sea surface. He said that makes the shelf at least half a mile thicker than any ice shelf known before in the Northern Hemisphere.

“Ice doesn’t want to flow down underwater, it wants to be afloat,” Hatfield said. “It just speaks to how thick that ice shelf became.”

Buttresses for Greenland and Canada

Global ice volume peaked between about 26,500 and 19,000 years ago, and three ice sheets met in Baffin Bay, according to the abstract. The Greenland Ice Sheet lay to the east and the Innuitian Ice Sheet to the north. The Laurentide Ice Sheet lay to the west.

Left, the proposed ice conditions in Baffin Bay at the last glacial maximum, with the Greenland, Innuitian and Laurentide ice sheets around a floating ice shelf. Top right, the estimated shelf thickness at its grounding line about 19,000 years ago; bottom right, its area compared at scale with modern Antarctic ice shelves. Credit: Klotsko et al., Nature Geoscience (2026). Click image to enlarge.

The team argues that the ice shelf held all three in place and helped them grow thicker. Hatfield compared the ice sheets to “a gothic cathedral.” In his picture, floating shelves are the flying buttresses that slow the flow of land ice into the ocean.

In West Antarctica, researchers have warned that the ice shelf holding back Thwaites Glacier could break up soon. Elsewhere on the continent, ice shelves are cracking under the weight of meltwater on their surface.

In the Arctic, the shelves left today are small. On the north coast of Canada’s Ellesmere Island, an ice island the size of Bermuda broke off the Ward Hunt Ice Shelf in August 2010.

Gone within 500 to 1,000 years

Sediment cores from across the bay suggest that the ice shelf disappeared over roughly 500 to 1,000 years, the university said. After that, glaciers on land flowed faster into the sea and melted.

The release says the loss helped unlock parts of the three ice sheets. They contributed to the hundreds of feet of sea level rise that set today’s coastlines. The abstract calls the shelf an analog for how weaker buttresses could speed up ice streams in Antarctica.

Hatfield said scientists who model ice shelves and glaciers now have new data to work with. Mud from beneath Greenland has shown that its ice vanished about 416,000 years ago. Seafloor cores show that the West Antarctic Ice Sheet has collapsed many times.

Dates for the breakup still approximate

The window of 500 to 1,000 years comes from the University of Florida release, not from the abstract. The release ties it to sediment cores from across the bay but does not say which cores set it or how they were dated.

Lauren Lewright and Jacqueline Austermann of Columbia University ran the glacial isostatic adjustment analyses, the sea-level modeling behind the corrected thickness, according to the paper’s author contributions. The authors present the shelf as their reading of ice-contact features on the slope.

Earlier work came up with a thinner shelf. In 2022, Pierre-Olivier Couette of Université Laval and colleagues studied a moraine system off Baffin Island.

In Communications Earth & Environment, they argued that an ice shelf about 1,640 feet (500 meters) thick covered northern Baffin Bay.

The BADEX team has posted its mapped landforms and ice-contact points in the Marine Geoscience Data System. The raw multibeam data from the 2023 cruise sit in the Rolling Deck to Repository.

After the cruise, the mud cores from Melville Bugt and the other stations went to a repository at Oregon State University. “We didn’t go out to look for an ice shelf this big,” Hatfield said.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Geoscience.

Stone tools discovered near an ancient Saudi lake reveal that Neanderthals expanded far beyond icy Europe into modern deserts

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Researchers have traditionally found neanderthal fossils in colder regions of Western Europe, but 55,000-year-old stone tools uncovered at jebel katefeh-1 in the Nefud desert suggest these ancient humans also inhabited what is now Saudi Arabia.

No hominin fossils have been found at the site, so the case rests on the shape of 24 stone flakes. A statistical model trained on sites in Africa, Arabia and the Levant matched them most closely with Neanderthal toolmaking.

The work was led by James Blinkhorn of the University of Liverpool and Huw Groucutt of the University of Malta.

An ancient lakeshore in the Nefud

Jebel Katefeh-1, or JKF-1, sits in the Jubbah basin of northern Saudi Arabia. It holds one of the largest Middle Paleolithic stone tool collections published from northern Arabia.

A 2012 study of the Jubbah paleolake described 1,222 artifacts from the site, on the eastern edge of a former lake.

The deposit probably reflects a relatively short-term occupation on a dune above low ground that may have held water.

That earlier work could only place the occupation somewhere from 90,000 to 50,000 years ago. The window spanned times when both Homo sapiens and Neanderthals lived in Southwest Asia, which made the tools hard to interpret.

The site’s flakes are also unusually elongated for Arabia and narrow toward the tip, like those from Kebara, Ksar Akil and Tor Faraj in the Levant.

Dating the layer with the tools

To narrow the age, the team dug a second trench 16 feet (5 meters) from the first. They dated three new sediment samples with optically stimulated luminescence, a method for dating buried sand grains.

Fox burrows had mixed the sediments, so each age was calculated from the dominant population of grains in its sample.

The sequence shows windblown sand laid down from about 85,000 to 64,000 years ago. Lake sediments above it date to about 60,000 and 59,000 years ago.

The tool-bearing layer sits at the boundary between the two, at the onset of a humid phase. Combined with earlier ages of 49,000 and 53,000 years for that layer, the occupation dates to about 60,000 to 50,000 years ago.

The new age matches other Arabian sites such as Al-Marrat (ALM-3) in northern Arabia and Shibat Dihya-1 in Yemen.

Reading the shape of stone flakes

In Levallois knapping, a toolmaker shapes a stone core so that flakes, or blanks, of a predetermined shape and size can be struck off. Such techniques dominate collections of this period across Africa, Arabia and the Levant.

The comparison drew on 704 complete Levallois blanks from 12 sites in Northeast Africa and Southwest Asia, dated between 100,000 and 50,000 years ago.

Tested factors included age, landscape, climate, collection size, raw material, site type and population history.

“Shape variability in Levallois blanks across the region is best explained by differences in population history,” the authors wrote.

Only four of these sites have hominin fossils. The rest were labeled by convention: fossil-free Levantine sites from 70,000 to 55,000 years ago as Neanderthal, African and earlier Arabian sites as Homo sapiens.

Neighboring groups could also share toolmaking practices, as proposed for Tinshemet Cave in Israel.

A strong lean toward Neanderthals

Models trained on the reference sites agreed with those labels more than 93% of the time.

Applied to the 24 JKF-1 blanks, the best-performing model gave a median Neanderthal probability of 78%. Only four blanks leaned toward Homo sapiens.

Nearly a quarter of the JKF-1 blanks returned Neanderthal probabilities above 95%.

At Al-Marrat, only seven blanks could be tested, too few for firm conclusions, though four topped 78%.

Around 55,000 years ago, Neanderthals were the only hominins securely dated in Southwest Asia, which fits the result.

Neanderthals beyond the cold north

Neanderthals lived from about 400,000 to 40,000 years ago in Europe and southwestern to central Asia, according to the Smithsonian’s Human Origins Program.

From about 75,000 to 50,000 years ago, Neanderthal fossils appear at Levantine sites such as Kebara and Amud, and at Shanidar in the Zagros Mountains.

A 2021 study of Shukbah Cave identified a Neanderthal tooth there as the southernmost known fossil of the population.

“Our findings challenge the notion of Neanderthals as far-northern hominins constrained to cold areas and/or palearctic woodlands,” the authors wrote.

If so, Neanderthals and Homo sapiens could have interbred across a broad area that included Arabia.

Why the case remains open

“We do not advocate that particular technologies can be exclusively linked to specific populations or species,” the researchers wrote.

The comparison set has no open-air Neanderthal sites, though JKF-1 lies in the open. Site type also explained part of the differences in shape.

No site has yet produced fossils of both species in the same layer. Teeth from Üçağızlı II in the northern Levant have raised that possibility, but they have not been directly dated.

Testing the Neanderthal model will require hominin fossils, which remain scarce in Arabia.

Fossil material and DNA also preserve poorly at low latitudes.

The study was published in the journal Quaternary Science Reviews.

Once again, NASA is asking for ideas to rescue Hubble from certain death

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Hubble Space Telescope still shines in the eyes of astronomers after 36 years in orbit, but it is not as nimble as it once was. Left alone, the observatory will plunge back into the atmosphere in the early-to-mid-2030s, closing the book on one of the most famous scientific instruments of the century.

NASA last raised the orbit of Hubble in 2002, when the Space Shuttle Columbia visited the observatory for a servicing mission. The final shuttle servicing flight in 2009 did not reboost the telescope's orbit, and with the retirement of the shuttle fleet two years later, Hubble has steadily lost altitude with no way to stave off eventual destruction.

The space agency released a Request For Information (RFI) on Thursday asking commercial companies for ideas on how to reboost, service, or a controlled disposal of Hubble. Responses are due November 30. Specifically, NASA is looking for information about companies' current and near-term capabilities for capturing Hubble and boosting it to an altitude of at least 373 miles (600 km), enhancing the telescope's performance, or guiding Hubble to a controlled reentry.

Rare meteorite from Mars gives us a view into its ancient crust

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Crater counts tell us that most of the Martian surface is more than 3 billion years old, but pieces of Mars we have on Earth tell a different story. The three main classes of Martian meteorites—shergottites, nakhlites, and chassignites—are all 2.4 billion years old or younger. Until now, only two genuinely ancient Martian meteorites were known: Allan Hills 84001, found in Antarctica, and a jumbled rock from northwest Africa nicknamed Black Beauty. Now we have a third one.

A team led by Yang Liu, a planetary scientist at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, has shown that a Martian meteorite called Teghaza 001, found in the Sahara Desert in Mali in 2022, formed at least 4.1 billion years ago. “It's very old and similar to some of the oldest rocks on Earth,” Liu says.

A filtered collection

“We don't fully understand why old Martian rocks are so rare on Earth," Liu said. Part of the answer may lie in how a piece of Mars gets here. It takes a large impact to launch a rock off the planet, and not every rock can survive that. “If the impact hits into loose powder, it may not get cemented to become a rock and fly off Mars' gravity,” Liu said. “So the natural impact already acts as a filter. Weak rocks do not make it off Mars.”

Ancient pollen reveals a surprising twist in the evolution of flowers

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Flowering plants dominate landscapes around the world, and scientists have long debated how they became so successful.

One familiar explanation points to the asteroid that wiped out the dinosaurs.

Fossil pollen tells it differently. The two moments that remade plant life came tens of millions of years before and after that impact, in the two hottest stretches of the past 120 million years.

In both of them, plants were dying out and appearing at the same time. In between, when the planet was cooler, very little changed.

Kale Sniderman, an honorary research fellow at the University of Melbourne, led the work with colleagues in Australia and Austria.

They read it all from pollen and spores, the only plant fossils common enough to count this way. Every tally in the study is a tally of pollen types rather than whole plants.

“Each of these extremely warm ‘turnover intervals’ lasted a few million years,” said Greg Jordan, a professor of botany at the University of Tasmania and a co-author.

“Thus, the warmest greenhouse climates simultaneously destroyed and promoted plant diversity, but only when considered over million-year timescales.”

Reading the fossil record

Unlike larger plant remains, pollen can accumulate in enormous quantities in lake sediments, preserving a detailed record of ancient vegetation.

The catch is that paleontologists have not sampled every stretch of time equally. Dig harder in one period and it looks richer than the one next to it.

“The number of available fossils varies over geological time and between regions. Because of this, simple counts of fossilized species can give a misleading sense of diversity over evolutionary time,” Sniderman said.

So the team built its own compilation and held the sampling effort steady across every slice of time before comparing one slice with the next.

The southeastern Australian dataset runs to 14,254 records from 335 fossil assemblages at 167 sites.

Much of it came out of decades of drilling reports archived by the Geological Survey of Victoria and its Tasmanian counterpart.

For most of the past 125 million years that corner of the world sat near 65 degrees south, close to Antarctica.

Two bursts of change

Across the record, two short intervals carry almost all the action. One falls in the middle of the Cretaceous, about 95 to 90 million years ago.

The other falls in the late Paleocene and early Eocene, about 58 to 54 million years ago. Both are among the warmest greenhouse episodes of the past 200 million years.

The losses in those intervals were heavy. Of the ferns, mosses and their relatives first recorded before about 89 million years ago, roughly 75% were gone by the second warm episode.

Conifers and their relatives lost roughly 60% of their recorded pollen types by about 84 million years ago.

Even the first flowering plants took about 80% losses by the early Eocene.

New types arrived in the same intervals. The land plant flora inherited from the Early Cretaceous roughly tripled in variety between about 120 and 55 million years ago, and the two bursts are where that growth happened.

Flowering plants finally overtook the other groups during the second one.

The flora reorganized twice, in other words, rather than drifting. One statistical break in the record falls at 83.5 million years ago and another at 54.3 million.

Both latitudes turned over

A warm world isn’t warm everywhere in the same way. The Cretaceous tropics have often been read as hot and dry enough to be lethal.

If so, hothouse episodes might have helped plants only in cool, wet places like the one the Australian record came from.

So the team ran the same analysis on fossil pollen from northeastern Africa, which sat on the equator through the Cretaceous.

Those records, about 48,000 pollen grains from 19 wells and sites, show the same mid-Cretaceous burst of arrivals and losses in all three plant groups.

For the younger half of the story the team compared its record with a published analysis of pollen from the American tropics.

There, too, the rate at which new types appeared peaked during the late Paleocene and early Eocene, and tracked global temperature.

Climate beat geography

Warmth isn’t the only candidate. Australia was drifting north and breaking away from Antarctica over this period, and the plant groups were also competing with each other.

The team tested all of it. Temperature, the continent’s isolation, its latitude and the plant groups’ effects on each other went into models that let them compete to explain the record.

Of the 20 relationships those models supported, 13 involved climate.

The same held when whole classes of explanation were compared. Allowing climate terms improved the fit in 89% of cases, while isolation, latitude and plant-on-plant effects did so in fewer than half.

The asteroid comes out looking minor. Among the plants, the extinctions ran well ahead of the impact.

The equatorial record shows ferns and conifers collapsing long before it, and in southeastern Australia the cone-bearing plants were as varied as ever just after the dinosaurs went.

The authors wrote that some global crises, the end-Cretaceous extinction among them, are secondary to warming phases in shaping life on land. A long hothouse reaches the flora more deeply than a single catastrophe does.

A warm Miocene stalled

One stretch of time argues with the rest. Australia had drifted far enough north by the early Miocene, roughly 20 million years ago, that local sea surface temperatures nearly matched the early Eocene. Plants there barely responded.

The difference is that the planet as a whole didn’t match it. Peak Miocene temperatures ran about 18°F (10°C) above today’s, where the earlier hothouse ran about 36°F (20°C) above.

Global temperature predicted the Australian record better than the local water did, and the American tropics show the same muted Miocene.

Something global, then, rather than a warm patch. The authors propose a chain: extreme warmth, high carbon dioxide and a small gap between the poles and the equator speed up the water cycle.

That spreads warm, wet, forested habitat, which is where new lineages take hold.

But ancient warming offers little reassurance for today’s plants. Human-driven climate change is unfolding far faster than either prehistoric episode, leaving species much less time to adapt.

Chris Mays is a paleontologist at the Natural History Museum Vienna and a co-author of the study.

“While extinct species will eventually be replaced by new species, not many of us will be satisfied having to wait the few million years for them to evolve, especially since we have the power to prevent the loss of species today, if we stop emitting CO₂ from burning fossil fuels,” said Mays.

The mystery behind plant diversity

Why extreme warmth produces new plant species at all is still unsettled. Killing them is the easy half, since enough heat pushes a plant past what it can stand.

The team lists candidates for the other half: a faster water cycle, steadier climates over the long haul, more carbon dioxide for photosynthesis. None of them is tested against the others here.

The African record stops at the end of the Cretaceous. It also holds only seven time intervals, too few to run the model comparison the Australian data supported.

The Australian analysis itself rests on 18 time bins, each a slice of geological time rather than a repeated experiment.

And a count of ancient pollen isn’t quite a count of plants, because plants that look identical in a pollen grain get counted once.

More fossil records could help explain why extreme warmth transformed ancient ecosystems.

For now, the findings reveal how profoundly climate can reshape plant life – and why today’s rapid warming poses a very different threat.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Plants.

Ancient microbes reveal a new clue to how complex life began

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A white blood cell chasing a germ doesn’t swim. It crawls, reaching out with part of itself, gripping a surface and pulling the rest along.

Now researchers have filmed Asgard archaea, tiny microbes thought to be the closest relatives of complex cells, doing the same thing.

The archaea cells changed shape within minutes, grew and pulled back long, thin arms, and used them to haul themselves across glass.

Amoebas get around the same way, whereas bacteria and their microbial cousins mostly swim with spinning tails or tug themselves along on sticky threads.

The discovery suggests that crawling may have evolved long before complex life appeared.

Philipp Radler led the study in Christa Schleper’s lab at the University of Vienna, working with an international team of researchers.

The team watched two living species on lab glass, not the ancient microbe itself.

How scientists filmed Asgard archaea

Most of what scientists know about Asgard archaea comes from reading their DNA. Only a handful have ever been grown in a lab.

That DNA carries instructions for proteins long linked to complex cells, which helps explain the group’s spot on the tree of life.

One of those proteins is actin. In our own cells, actin builds a flexible inner frame that lets a cell change shape and move.

Electron microscope images had shown a round body with thin arms sticking out, but a still image can’t show movement.

These microbes grow without oxygen, so the team sealed them inside thin glass chambers filled with nitrogen and carbon dioxide.

For runs of up to six hours, gas kept flowing into the chamber, which was held at about 72°F (22°C).

Cells reshaped themselves within minutes

The main species, Lokiarchaeum ossiferum, has a body about 0.00004 inches (1 micrometer) across.

A typical cell carried five arms, though some had one and others had ten. The longest arms reached 15 micrometers, roughly 14 body widths.

Nothing held still. Arms grew at about 1.5 micrometers a minute, split into branches and pulled back, some slowly and others at about 11 micrometers a minute.

This microbe takes about a week to divide, yet it rebuilt its shape over a few minutes.

The total length of a cell’s arms stayed about the same as it rearranged them, and cells with more arms tended to have shorter ones.

The researchers suspect the cells redistribute their existing outer membrane between arms rather than constantly producing more.

Some cells changed their whole body too, switching from a stick shape to a ball.

Long arms pull the cells forward

About half of the cells stuck to the glass also moved their bodies from one spot to another. Most wandered at random, and only 6 of 117 tracked cells kept going one way.

Some cells anchored the tip of a front arm, and as the arm shortened, the body followed it, sometimes in a sudden jump. Others slid smoothly behind a single leading arm.

The team found little sign of cells pushing from behind.

Their typical speed was about 1.6 micrometers a minute. At that pace, a cell moving in a straight line would take about 11 days to cover an inch (2.5 centimeters).

That fits with crawling in complex cells, such as an amoeba, which move at 0.1 to 20 micrometers a minute.

Swimming microbes go far faster. The only other surface movement known in archaea relies on hairlike threads outside the cell. These cells used their own arms instead.

When the team coated the glass with a sticky compound, both the arm movements and the crawling slowed down, a sign that the two are linked.

Actin-blocking drugs stopped the crawl

To test whether actin drives the motion, the team tried drugs that block actin in complex cells. Some barely changed anything. Three cut the number of cells holding on to the glass with arms.

The team focused on a toxin called swinholide A, which worked at doses within the range used on complex cells.

At 50 nanograms per milliliter, new arms stopped forming, existing ones stopped growing, and the cells stopped moving.

Some remaining arms lost their inner support, bent, and swayed loosely in the water.

The team also pulled out the proteins that cling to the microbe’s actin. Several belong to families known in complex cells for building actin strands up and cutting them apart.

A second species, Margulisarchaeum peptidophilum, grown by the JAMSTEC group, behaved the same way.

Its arms grew about three times as fast as those of the first species, at 4.8 micrometers a minute, and its body moved about twice as fast.

Swinholide A stopped it too, halting arm growth and cutting the share of cells that moved.

Actin and the proteins that control it also turn up in almost every Asgard genome sequenced so far. To the authors, that suggests the group’s shared ancestor already carried them.

Did crawling help complex life evolve?

Researchers think the first complex cells formed about 2 billion years ago, when an Asgard microbe teamed up with a bacterium that ended up living inside it.

That bacterium became the ancestor of mitochondria, which make energy inside our cells.

Both species in this study are alive today and likely differ from that ancient ancestor.

The authors suggest the ancestor might have used its arms to move toward a partner, attach to it, and eventually wrap around it. They offer it as a possibility, not a result.

The team also had to rely on drugs designed for the actin of complex cells, because there are no genetic tools for these microbes yet.

And the cells crawled on glass, while in nature Asgard archaea live in tiny spaces within seafloor mud.

Scientists still don’t know exactly how these microbes control their movements, but the discovery offers a remarkable glimpse into the distant past.

The ability to crawl, change shape and reach toward other cells may have existed long before animals, plants or fungi appeared.

Those ancient movements could have helped set the stage for complex life itself.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.

NASA's Tribute To Star Trek's Patrick Stewart Is Literally Out Of This World

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →
As tributes to legendary actor Patrick Stewart continue to pour in from all over the world, this one from NASA goes beyond the realm of this planet.

Helix Nebula offers a stunning glimpse of our Sun’s future

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Today’s Image of the Day from NASA reveals the Helix Nebula, a vast shell of glowing gas left behind by a dying star about 650 light-years from Earth.

At first glance, the nebula resembles a colorful cosmic eye staring back at us. Its apparent ring, however, is partly an illusion of perspective.

Astronomers have concluded that we are looking almost directly down a tremendous cylinder of glowing gas, rather than at a simple bubble floating in space.

Looking into the Helix Nebula

The Helix Nebula lies in the constellation Aquarius and is one of the closest planetary nebulae to Earth.

Its relative proximity makes it appear enormous in our sky. The nebula spans nearly half the apparent diameter of the full Moon, even though its faint glow makes it far harder to see.

At its distance from Earth, that apparent size corresponds to a ring nearly three light-years across.

The portrait combines observations from the Hubble Space Telescope with a much wider view captured by the Mosaic Camera on the National Science Foundation’s 0.9-meter telescope at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona.

Hubble could not capture the entire sprawling structure in a single exposure. 

Astronomers used its Advanced Camera for Surveys to take multiple images, which were assembled into a mosaic and blended with the wider ground-based view.

The result exposes structures that would otherwise be difficult to see across the entire nebula.

Thousands of strange filaments

Along the inner edge of the Helix are thousands of narrow structures that resemble tiny comets.

Each appears to have a dense head followed by a long tail, with the structures pointing back toward the star at the center of the nebula.

Ground-based astronomers had known about these features for decades, but Hubble revealed them in remarkable detail.

They formed as hot gas flowing away from the dying central star encountered cooler material that the star had expelled earlier in its life.

The interaction helped sculpt the surrounding gas and dust into the intricate structures visible today.

Astronomers have suggested that the comet-like filaments could lie within a disk surrounding the central star, forming something like an enormous collar around it.

Colors reveal glowing gases

The dramatic colors in the image are not simply decorative. They trace different elements glowing within the nebula.

Blue marks light associated with oxygen, while the red regions trace hydrogen and nitrogen.

Together, these gases form the bright ring surrounding the small but extremely hot stellar remnant at the center.

That central object is a white dwarf, the exposed core left after the original star shed much of its outer material.

The Helix therefore offers a preview of a possible distant future for our own solar system.

Stars similar in mass to the Sun eventually exhaust the fuel sustaining their interiors. As they approach the ends of their lives, they can cast their outer layers into space, exposing their hot cores.

Radiation from the remaining star then illuminates the expanding material around it.

A planetary nebula has no planets

Despite the name, planetary nebulae have nothing to do with the formation of planets.

The term dates back to early telescope observations. Through small instruments, these round, fuzzy objects could resemble the disks of planets, and the name remained even after astronomers understood their true nature.

With more powerful telescopes, structures hidden within those fuzzy patches become visible.

In the Helix, what looks like a doughnut-shaped ring is actually part of a much more complicated three-dimensional structure.

The glowing tube extends an enormous distance through space and happens to be oriented nearly toward Earth. That alignment gives us the unusual perspective seen in the image.

Hubble found an unexpected opportunity

The circumstances behind Hubble’s observations of the Helix were unusual.

During the Leonid meteor storm in November 2002, spacecraft controllers needed to protect Hubble from the stream of particles passing through Earth’s neighborhood.

They pointed the observatory’s more protected rear section toward the incoming meteor stream.

That restricted where Hubble could safely look, but the Helix Nebula happened to lie almost exactly in the opposite direction.

Rather than leaving the telescope idle, astronomers used the opportunity to observe the nebula.

Hubble spent nine orbits photographing the Helix and captured nine separate snapshots needed to cover its enormous apparent size.

Those observations ultimately became part of one of the most detailed portraits of this nearby stellar graveyard.

NASA Explores Potential Commercial Servicing for Hubble Telescope

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →
The Hubble Space Telescope returns to orbit as an improved telescope after its second servicing mission in February 1997. NASA

NASA issued a Request for Information Oct. 29 to learn more about industry capabilities for in-space servicing to support the agency’s Hubble Space Telescope.

“Hubble still has important science ahead of it, and we should be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible,” said NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman. “If there are innovative and cost-effective ways to extend the life of an asset already on orbit, we owe it to the American taxpayer and the scientific community to explore them. Extending Hubble’s life would mean more discoveries, more science, and more opportunities to expand our understanding of the universe.”

The Request for Information seeks commercial interest in: potentially conducting a mission to boost Hubble to a higher altitude to extend its science lifetime; servicing Hubble to enhance its capabilities; or performing a safe, controlled re-entry into Earth’s atmosphere or orbital disposal at the end of Hubble’s mission.

“Hubble is a national icon that has fundamentally changed how humanity views our universe for more than 35 years,” said Nicky Fox, associate administrator, Science Mission Directorate, NASA Headquarters in Washington. “Commercial industry has rapidly evolved in recent years to offer new capabilities for in-space satellite capture and servicing. Given the time we have until Hubble’s anticipated re-entry, we are eager to explore the breadth of possibilities to boost Hubble to a higher orbit while we develop the flagship mission to follow.”

The Request for Information is available online and will remain open for responses until 3 p.m. EST, Monday, Nov. 30.

Like all spacecraft in low Earth orbit, Hubble’s altitude is slowly decreasing over time because of atmospheric drag. Hubble does not have a propulsion system to maintain its own orbit, and analysis predicts Hubble is likely to re-enter the atmosphere in the 2033 to 2034 timeframe. As such, NASA anticipates that a mission would need to take place by 2032.

The Hubble Space Telescope has been operating for more than three decades and continues to make ground-breaking discoveries that shape our fundamental understanding of the universe. Hubble is a project of international cooperation between NASA and ESA (European Space Agency). NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, manages the telescope and mission operations. Lockheed Martin Space, based in Denver, also supports mission operations at Goddard. The Space Telescope Science Institute in Baltimore, which is operated by the Association of Universities for Research in Astronomy, conducts Hubble science operations for NASA.

Shrouded in ice in the lonely Weddell Sea, Vega Island is an eerie sight to behold. On this speck of land east of the Antarctic Peninsula, ice, rock outcrops, and glacial moraines combine to create the appearance of a smirking skull in profile looking toward Devil Island.

These images show Vega Island’s creepy countenance at the start of austral autumn (left) and in spring (right). They were acquired with the OLI (Operational Land Imager) on Landsat 9 on March 22, 2025, and October 10, 2026, respectively. (Clouds obscured the island in March 2026.) A nunatak jutting out of the ice has the appearance of an eye socket, a lateral moraine forms a mouth, and Bahía del Diablo (Devil’s Bay) Glacier constitutes a jawbone. In the March 2025 image, a layer of snow coats the ice, which is typical of the region’s wettest month. That contrasts with the October 2026 image, when snow is absent from large areas and darker ice surfaces are visible.

Above-average temperatures in winter 2026—particularly in June—were partly responsible for so much bare ice in October, said glaciologist Mauri Pelto of Nichols College. The ice exposed on Vega and nearby islands indicates limited new snow accumulation over the winter, further reduced by sublimation and wind scouring, he said. “The winter season was no treat for these glaciers.”

The melt season ahead may also be more trick than treat. Bare ice is darker than snow and absorbs more solar energy, meaning that melting is poised to speed up as the days grow longer and warmer. “Even a thin cover of new snow from a spring storm would be helpful in the short run,” Pelto said. “When entombed in snow and ice, the skull is preserved. But when exposed to melt, it weathers away, which will lead to a changing jawline and expanding eye socket.”

October 10, 2026 NASA Earth Observatory/Lauren Dauphin

The first comprehensive mapping of Vega Island’s ice was aided by Landsat imagery. In a study published in 1982, scientists described the island’s four ice caps with protruding rocky cliffs and outlet glaciers. They leveraged early Landsat observations along with aerial photos and topographic maps to catalog 29 glaciers on Vega.

Of those glaciers, Devil’s Bay Glacier has been closely monitored since the austral summer of 1998-1999, with researchers measuring an overall decline in its mass. Satellite images acquired in recent decades show ice loss from other glaciers on the island as well, with increased areas of exposed bedrock.

Long before scientists began tracking its glaciers, Vega was a bright spot in a tale of terror born out of early exploration of the area. The Swedish Antarctic Expedition of 1901-1903 set out to study the geography, geology, climate, and wildlife on and around the eastern Antarctic Peninsula. The party became split into three groups during their explorations, isolated for a winter in different locations. Adding to the horror, they lost their ship to the pack ice. Cape Well-met, as it would come to be known, off the “forehead” of the Vega Island skull, was the site of a happy reunion of two of the groups in spring 1903. Soon thereafter, an Argentinean expedition rescued the survivors of the ordeal.

The base map of this image was produced with data from the Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter instrument aboard NASA’s Mars Global Surveyor mission. Our climate models have a big, dangerous blind spot

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Climate change itself is increasingly fueling climate change. Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases are driving up global temperatures, setting off dangerous feedback loops that we have not fully accounted for in our climate models—or our emissions-reduction goals.

Scientists have long warned that warming would propel further warming in various ways, but a growing number of observations and studies are at last providing a sharper sense of just how powerful these cascading effects could be. And, by extension, they’re showing how much faster the planet may warm in this century.

A study in Environmental Research Letters in September, synthesizing the results of previous papers, found that the additional carbon dioxide and methane released from thawing permafrost, intensifying wildfires, hotter lakes and reservoirs, and wetter, warmer wetlands alone could drive global average temperatures up 0.2 °C to 0.4 °C this century. That would come on top of the 2.6°C of warming above preindustrial levels that modelers are already expecting through 2100. The additional heat could subject many of the world’s cities to monthslong heat waves and expose hundreds of millions of people to flooding. 

None of the models that inform the major UN climate reports, which assess how much the planet will likely warm under various emissions scenarios, account for all these feedback effects. Many of them don’t incorporate any. That leaves a huge blind spot in our understanding of how much the world could actually warm in the coming decades—and how rapidly nations will need to slash emissions to stay within critical boundaries.

A separate analysis published in One Earth in March, which examined what the feedback problem means for our international climate goals, concluded that the world may be much further off track than is widely believed. It found that just three regional sources of warming-driven emissions—thawing permafrost, increasing fires in boreal forests, and warming tropical wetlands—may shorten by as much as 25% the time before the planet consistently breaches the 1.5 °C and 2 °C warming thresholds.

Our climate models and the UN’s reports clearly need to be updated to better represent these effects. Nations must also step up their efforts to reduce emissions to reflect this improving understanding of the dangers we face, says Sam Abernethy, the lead author of the paper and a climate scientist at Spark Climate Solutions, a San Francisco–based nonprofit research group. 

At least some of this is starting to happen. Spark kick-started an initiative, called the Warming-Induced Greenhouse Gas Emissions Model Intercomparison Project, through which more than 20 international teams are running the same set of analyses on different models to develop greater understanding of feedback effects’ potential power. Researchers involved hope that the UN will incorporate those results into its next major climate assessment, due out in 2029. That report will update estimates of the remaining emissions that can be released before the world crosses key temperature thresholds.

But what is already clear is that the planet is rapidly warming and that nations haven’t done nearly enough to cut fossil-fuel pollution. And even if we did manage to halt it, warming-driven emissions could continue. 

So a number of the research groups involved in the recent studies have started to explore what nations could do to get feedback emissions under control. Scientists at the Woodwell Climate Research Center in Massachusetts are, for example, investigating the possibility of preventing fires or rapidly detecting them and putting them out when they threaten to burn the vegetation and soil that help insulate particularly carbon-rich wedges of permafrost (a subject I explored at length in a recent feature story). In addition, the Environmental Defense Fund’s Climate Innovation Initiative has backed lab research on the potential of adding sulfate, iron, or other compounds to wetlands to counteract fast-rising methane emissions. 

Several startups are also exploring possibilities. They’re looking into suppressing the lightning strikes that are igniting more and more fires in high latitudes, placing synthetic mats above permafrost to prevent it from melting, and deploying autonomous drones to quickly suppress potentially high-emissions fires before they burn out of control.

Investigating all these ideas is worthwhile. But researchers are only in the earliest stages of figuring out how big a dent in emissions any such efforts could achieve across sprawling global ecosystems undergoing massive transformations. 

The surest path to reducing the effect of warming-driven warming remains the one we’ve known all along: breaking society’s addiction to fossil fuels.
