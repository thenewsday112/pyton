Ancient microbes reveal a new clue to how complex life began

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A white blood cell chasing a germ doesn&#8217;t swim. It crawls, reaching out with part of itself, gripping a surface and pulling the rest along. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Now researchers have filmed Asgard archaea, tiny microbes thought to be the closest relatives of complex cells, doing the same thing.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The archaea cells changed shape within minutes, grew and pulled back long, thin arms, and used them to haul themselves across glass. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Amoebas get around the same way, whereas bacteria and their microbial cousins mostly swim with spinning tails or tug themselves along on sticky threads.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The discovery suggests that crawling may have evolved long before complex life appeared.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Philipp Radler led the study in Christa Schleper&#8217;s lab at the <a href="https://www.univie.ac.at/en/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">University of Vienna</a>, working with an international team of researchers.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team watched two living species on lab glass, not the ancient microbe itself.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-how-scientists-filmed-asgard-archaea">How scientists filmed Asgard archaea</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most of what scientists know about Asgard archaea comes from reading their DNA. Only a handful have ever been grown in a lab.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That DNA carries instructions for proteins long linked to complex cells, which helps explain the group&#8217;s spot on the <a href="https://www.earth.com/science/humans-and-all-life-on-earth-may-have-spawned-from-asgard-archaea-eukaryotes/">tree of life</a>. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of those proteins is actin. In our own cells, actin builds a flexible inner frame that lets a cell change shape and move.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Electron microscope images had shown a round body with thin arms sticking out, but a still image can&#8217;t show movement.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">These microbes grow without oxygen, so the team sealed them inside thin glass chambers filled with nitrogen and carbon dioxide. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For runs of up to six hours, gas kept flowing into the chamber, which was held at about 72°F (22°C).</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-cells-reshaped-themselves-within-minutes">Cells reshaped themselves within minutes</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The main species, <em>Lokiarchaeum ossiferum</em>, has a body about 0.00004 inches (1 micrometer) across. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">A typical cell carried five arms, though some had one and others had ten. The longest arms reached 15 micrometers, roughly 14 body widths.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nothing held still. Arms grew at about 1.5 micrometers a minute, split into branches and pulled back, some slowly and others at about 11 micrometers a minute. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">This microbe takes about a week to divide, yet it rebuilt its shape over a few minutes.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The total length of a cell&#8217;s arms stayed about the same as it rearranged them, and cells with more arms tended to have shorter ones. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The researchers suspect the cells redistribute their existing outer membrane between arms rather than constantly producing more.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some cells changed their whole body too, switching from a stick shape to a ball.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-long-arms-pull-the-cells-forward">Long arms pull the cells forward</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">About half of the cells stuck to the glass also moved their bodies from one spot to another. Most wandered at random, and only 6 of 117 tracked cells kept going one way.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some cells anchored the tip of a front arm, and as the arm shortened, the body followed it, sometimes in a sudden jump. Others slid smoothly behind a single leading arm. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team found little sign of cells pushing from behind.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Their typical speed was about 1.6 micrometers a minute. At that pace, a cell moving in a straight line would take about 11 days to cover an inch (2.5 centimeters). </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That fits with crawling in complex cells, such as an <a href="https://www.earth.com/animals/this-microscopic-hunter-knows-which-bacteria-are-worth-chasing-before-it-learns-anything/">amoeba</a>, which move at 0.1 to 20 micrometers a minute. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.earth.com/animals/how-dead-sea-microbes-swim-through-some-of-earths-saltiest-water/">Swimming microbes</a> go far faster. These cells used their own arms instead.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">When the team coated the glass with a sticky compound, both the arm movements and the crawling slowed down, a sign that the two are linked.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-actin-blocking-drugs-stopped-the-crawl">Actin-blocking drugs stopped the crawl</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">To test whether actin drives the motion, the team tried drugs that block actin in complex cells. Some barely changed anything. Three cut the number of cells holding on to the glass with arms.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team focused on a toxin called swinholide A, which worked at doses within the range used on complex cells. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">At 50 nanograms per milliliter, new arms stopped forming, existing ones stopped growing, and the cells stopped moving. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some remaining arms lost their inner support, bent, and swayed loosely in the water.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team also pulled out the proteins that cling to the microbe&#8217;s actin. Several belong to families known in complex cells for building actin strands up and cutting them apart.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">A second species, <em>Margulisarchaeum peptidophilum</em>, grown by the JAMSTEC group, behaved the same way.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Its arms grew about three times as fast as those of the first species, at 4.8 micrometers a minute, and its body moved about twice as fast. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Swinholide A stopped it too, halting arm growth and cutting the share of cells that moved.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Actin and the proteins that control it also turn up in almost every Asgard genome sequenced so far. To the authors, that suggests the group&#8217;s shared ancestor already carried them.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-did-crawling-help-complex-life-evolve">Did crawling help complex life evolve?</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Researchers think the <a href="https://www.earth.com/earth-science/complex-life-emerged-long-before-oxygen-flooded-earth/">first complex cells</a> formed about 2 billion years ago, when an Asgard microbe teamed up with a bacterium that ended up living inside it. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That bacterium became the ancestor of mitochondria, which make energy inside our cells.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Both species in this study are alive today and likely differ from that <a href="https://www.earth.com/science/microbe-asgard-archaea-from-2-billion-years-ago-could-be-the-ancestor-of-us-all/">ancient ancestor</a>. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The authors suggest the ancestor might have used its arms to move toward a partner, attach to it, and eventually wrap around it. They offer it as a possibility, not a result.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team also had to rely on drugs designed for the actin of complex cells, because there are no genetic tools for these microbes yet. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">And the cells crawled on glass, while in nature Asgard archaea live in tiny spaces within seafloor mud.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Scientists still don&#8217;t know exactly how these microbes control their movements, but the discovery offers a remarkable glimpse into the distant past.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The ability to crawl, change shape and reach toward other cells may have existed long before animals, plants or fungi appeared. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Those ancient movements could have helped set the stage for complex life itself.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The full study was published in the journal <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11063-9" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank"><em>Nature</em></a>.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8212;&#8211;</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Like what you read? <a href="https://www.earth.com/subscribe/">Subscribe to our newsletter</a> for engaging articles, exclusive content, and the latest updates.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Check us out on <a href="https://www.earth.com/earthsnap/">EarthSnap</a>, a free app brought to you by <a href="https://www.earth.com/author/eralls/">Eric Ralls</a> and Earth.com.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8212;&#8211;</p>
