Octoverse: Commits, pushes, reach new highs in 2026 as developers build with agents

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Developers pushed code to GitHub 17.9 billion times in the past 12 months, more than in 2024 and 2025 combined.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Behind that number are developers building what interests them and what their communities need. In Nepal, one developer <a href="https://github.com/nirajbhusal/rasuwa-flood-bulletin">built a flood bulletin on GitHub Pages</a> with verified information on roads, rescue, weather, and power. It has since grown into <a href="https://github.com/nirajbhusal/gati-alerts">Gati</a>, a Nepali-first relay of official disaster alerts that runs on GitHub Pages and Actions. Elsewhere, developers at NASA&rsquo;s Jet Propulsion Laboratory are creating <a href="https://github.com/nasa/spacewasm">software designed to run programs aboard spacecraft</a>, and fans <a href="https://github.com/crosspoint-reader/crosspoint-reader">built open-source firmware that lets people customize their e-readers</a>. Curiosity, aimed in every direction, at a scale GitHub hasn&rsquo;t seen before.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That growth happened quickly. Partway through the year, activity on GitHub bent sharply upward, just as agents went from something developers were trying out to something they were working alongside. Developers picked from more models than ever and built in industries we are highlighting in this report for the first time, from health care to agriculture.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Octoverse is our chance to step back and look at the whole picture. This year, that picture is bigger than ever. Here is what stood out most:</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li><strong>More AI, more of everything.</strong> Among repositories created this year, the average repo with an agent configuration file logged eight times as many commits as the average new repo without one.</li> <li><strong>As of September, developers start more than 40% of pull requests with an agent.</strong> In March, it was 17%. For a growing share of work, the first move is now directing an agent, and review is how developers shape what comes back.</li> <li><strong>Developers move fast on model choice.</strong> In September, 61% of GitHub Copilot users used more than one model, and requests spread across general purpose, deep reasoning and planning, and fast and lightweight models</li> <li><strong>Builders are showing up from every field.</strong> This year, for the first time, we&rsquo;re sharing our view of public repositories by industry. Repos serving mining, construction, health care, and agriculture have each grown several times over since 2022.</li> </ul> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Underneath it all, AI models and the harnesses built around them have advanced over the past year, and developers have more to choose from. Growth is moving quickly: one year into last year&rsquo;s five-year developer growth projections, every country we projected has already closed at least a fifth of its distance to 2030, and our own forecasting models no longer agree on where it will go next.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-year-developers-set-a-new-pace">The year developers set a new pace</h2> <figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img alt="Bar chart titled &ldquo;The year of momentum&rdquo; showing growth from 2024 to 2026 in GitHub activity. Developers pushed code changes to GitHub 17.9 billion times in the past 12 months, more than 2024 and 2025 combined. Commits across all repo types (public and private) climbed even faster to 34.3 billion, more than three times where they stood two years ago. Merged pull requests more than tripled to 1.3 billion over the same period, an average of 3.5 million a day.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Agents were one of the tools developers leaned on hardest this year, pulling them deeper into daily work. A repository has quietly become part of the paper trail behind published research.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-developers-are-reshaping-their-workflow">Developers are reshaping their workflow</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">More developers now begin a pull request by specifying what they want and orchestrating the agents that carry it out. By September 2026, more than 40% of pull requests on GitHub started with an agent&rsquo;s first draft, up from 17% in March. By September, agent pull requests were at 90%, while the comparison group held at about 86%.</p> <figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img alt="Table compares agent-authored pull requests with pull requests without detected agent involvement. For pull requests changing multiple files, the figures are 87% and 79%, respectively. For pull requests merged within two weeks, agent-authored rates increased from 83% in February 2026 to 90% in September 2026, while the comparison group was about 86% in both periods." class="wp-image-99770" height="576" src="https://github.blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Octoverse2026_BlogSpotlight_4.jpg?resize=1024%2C576" width="1024" /></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Among GitHub Copilot&rsquo;s monthly active users, 55% now use agentic features, up from 35% a year ago. The steepest growth is in the most hands-off mode. Copilot cloud agent, where a developer assigns a task and an agent works on it independently, grew 230% year over year, against 85% for agent chat. Developers still ask agents questions, and increasingly they hand over whole tasks.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The willingness to delegate is reflected by the kinds of tasks developers are attempting. Public repositories mentioning interoperability or reverse engineering increased more than 300% year over year, from about 30,000 created in 2025 to more than 130,000 in 2026.</p> <figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img alt="300%&rdquo; year-over-year growth in public repositories mentioning interoperability or reverse engineering." class="wp-image-99771" height="576" src="https://github.blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Octoverse2026_BlogSpotlight_7.jpg?resize=1024%2C576" width="1024" /></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Review is where developers shape what comes back, and more tooling is being built for it. <a href="https://docs.github.com/pull-requests/how-tos/stacked-pull-requests?utm_source=octoverse-pr-stacks-docs&amp;utm_medium=blog&amp;utm_campaign=universe-2026">Stacked pull requests</a>, which split related work into smaller changes that are easier to review in sequence, launched as a preview on July 30, and more than 264,000 developers had used them by September. Usage of AI-assisted code review and GitHub Advanced Security both grew more than 90% year over year. The platform also helps secure that work: push protection, which screens for exposed secrets such as API keys and passwords, ran 5.8 billion scans this year, nearly four times 2025&rsquo;s pace. Developers are investing in how they review code.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-model-choice-moves-at-developer-speed">Model choice moves at developer speed</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Developers match models to the work they hand to their agents. In September 2026, 61% of GitHub Copilot users who selected a model themselves used more than one model across their sessions, up from 50% four months earlier.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The lineup is growing too: developers on paid GitHub Copilot plans used 29 distinct models in May and 47 in September, and no single model carried more than 25% of requests that month. Some model decisions come down to cost and familiarity. Developers weigh price and functionality against need and may lean toward providers they already trust.</p> <figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img alt="Three panels show GitHub Copilot usage in September 2026: 61% of users used more than one model; paying users used 47 distinct models; and the top model accounted for 25% of requests." class="wp-image-99772" height="256" src="https://github.blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Octoverse2026_BlogSpotlight_5.jpg?resize=1024%2C256" width="1024" /></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">To see how developer choices spread, we grouped the models used in GitHub Copilot by what they are built for: deep reasoning and planning, general purpose, and fast and lightweight. We then looked at where requests went, counting only activity where a developer explicitly selected a model. That leaves out cases where GitHub Copilot picks the model (<a href="https://docs.github.com/copilot/concepts/models/auto-model-selection?utm_source=octoverse-auto-model-selection-docs&amp;utm_medium=blog&amp;utm_campaign=universe-2026">auto model selection</a>), GitHub and Microsoft staff usage, background utility models, duplicate requests used for monitoring and testing, and requests made with a developer&rsquo;s own API key.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">We found that out of every 100 developer-selected requests, 38 went to general purpose models, 37 to deep reasoning and planning models, and 25 to fast and lightweight models. Developers spread their choices across models and functionality, and about 25% of requests go to a fast model.</p> <figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img alt="Grid of 100 developer-selected GitHub Copilot requests, excluding the auto model: 37 use deep reasoning and planning models (pink), 38 use general-purpose models (gray), and 25 use fast, lightweight models (green). Percentages are rounded." class="wp-image-99773" height="576" src="https://github.blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Octoverse2026_BlogVisual_2.jpg?resize=1024%2C576" width="1024" /></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">To see how those choices shift, we looked at them as a time series over the past three months, tracking request share alongside how many developers tried each model and how many kept coming back. Three patterns stand out:</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li><strong>New releases are used heavily before most developers have tried them.</strong> In their first weeks, most new models take a larger share of requests than the portion of developers who have tried them. Early adopters use a model heavily before the rest follow.</li> <li><strong>Developers keep a model in rotation after leaning on it less.</strong> When a model&rsquo;s share of requests falls, the share of developers still using it falls more slowly</li> <li><strong>Developers blend kinds of models.</strong> Requests spread across all three groups, and few developers stay with any one kind for long.</li> </ul> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Taken together, the spread across kinds of models points to a lot of choosing: a lineup that keeps growing, a best fit that shifts from one task to the next, and all three kinds of models held in rotation. Many developers also let GitHub Copilot do some of it. In September 2026, 63% of paying GitHub Copilot developers used <a href="https://github.blog/changelog/2026-09-14-configure-cost-and-quality-in-copilot-auto-model-selection/">auto model selection</a> at least once, letting Copilot pick the model for the task.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Planning, implementing, and checking a change each place different demands on a model, in capability, speed, and cost. <a href="https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/project-hydrafusion-frontier-quality-via-multi-model-orchestration/">Project HydraFusion</a>, began as a research preview of model orchestration, matching models to each step of that work. In the CLI and the GitHub Copilot app, it routed almost 60,000 sessions. That work is now generally available as <a href="https://docs.github.com/copilot/concepts/models/copilot-fusion?utm_source=octoverse-fusion-docs&amp;utm_medium=blog&amp;utm_campaign=universe-2026">GitHub Copilot Fusion</a>, which brings model orchestration together with auto model selection and chooses the right model for each step behind the scenes.</p> <figure class="wp-block-image size-large is-resized"><img alt="63% of paying GitHub Copilot users used Auto model selection at least once in September 2026." class="wp-image-99775" height="576" src="https://github.blog/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Octoverse2026_BlogSpotlight_6.jpg?resize=1024%2C576" style="width: 905px; height: auto;" width="1024" /></figure> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-language-choice-has-new-influence">Language choice has new influence</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Language choice has long reflected what developers want to build, what their teams know, and what existing systems require. This year&rsquo;s data raises the question of whether a fourth influence is emerging: the language an agent picks when a developer doesn&rsquo;t specify one.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Last year, we reported that TypeScript had overtaken Python and JavaScript to become the most used language on GitHub by contributors. Unless noted, metrics include public and private repositories. Industry, open-source contribution, and open-source project figures are public only. Prior years: 2024 and 2025 figures are recalculated on the same definitions and window, so they may differ from earlier reports, which covered public activity and a September 1 to August 31 window.</li> <li><strong>Pushes, commits, and contributions.</strong> A push sends one or more commits to GitHub. Contributions count each push once, so commit totals can exceed contribution totals. Actions are counted in jobs, not minutes. Open-source contributions (2.5 billion) are public.</li> <li><strong>Filtering.</strong> Contributor and contribution counts exclude spam-flagged accounts, staff, and bots where identifiable.</li> <li><strong>Geography and projections.</strong> Developers are mapped to countries by self-reported location, with a 100-developer publication threshold. Progress is net developers added from September 2025 to September 2026 as a share of the distance from each country&rsquo;s published 2025 count to its 2030 projection.</li> <li><strong>Languages.</strong> Contributors are distinct accounts each month that took part through pushes, pull requests, issues, reviews, or comments, attributed to each repository&rsquo;s primary language (Linguist). An account can count in several languages. This follows the Octoverse 2025 method. Forecasts cover the top 10 languages by average monthly participation and project to September 2027 (mean shown).</li> <li>The <strong>language bubble chart</strong> compares growth in monthly participating accounts between September 2025 and September 2026 with the share of merged pull requests with detected AI involvement. Bubble size reflects community size.</li> <li><strong>Top countries by pushes to public repositories, adjusted by profile location.</strong> Pushes from each country were summed across the Octoverse year. To adjust for biases caused by VPN usage, LLM classification was used to extract the ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 code from the profiles of GitHub users who contributed pushes during this period, which were then used to construct ratios of ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 codes based on profile location vs. ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 codes based on IP address. These ratios were then used to weight the pushes from each country.</li> <li><strong>Industry.</strong> Public repositories created 2022 to 2026 were sorted into NAICS sectors by a fine-tuned multilingual embedding model using name, description, topics, and README, from random samples per year. AI mentions and agent-opened pull requests (detectable agents only) were flagged as separate signals. Sector and AI-mention shares can be related because both read the same text. Shares are estimates.</li> <li><strong>Open-source rankings.</strong> Repositories are classed as community or single-vendor by who holds strategic control. &ldquo;Fastest-growing&rdquo; ranks by the absolute year-over-year increase in distinct contributing accounts. &ldquo;Most engaged&rdquo; blends a repository&rsquo;s rankings for stars, forks, and distinct pull request and issue creators. Forked repositories are excluded from both lists.</li> <li><strong>Model choice.</strong> Figures cover GitHub Copilot and count developer-selected requests only. They exclude auto model selection, staff usage, duplicate monitoring requests, background utility models, and requests made with a developer&rsquo;s own API key. Each model needs at least 1,000 weekly active users to count. Models are grouped by what they are built for, with examples listed alphabetically by provider:</li> <li><strong>Deep reasoning and planning:</strong> Claude Opus 5.5, GPT 5.5.</li> <li><strong>General purpose:</strong> Claude Sonnet 5, Grok 4.7, GPT 6.1 Sol.</li> <li><strong>Fast and lightweight:</strong> Claude Haiku 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash, GPT 6 Luna.</li> <li>Figures describe observed use, not model quality. Trend observations use weekly data from July 14 to October 13, 2026.</li> <li><strong>Request share</strong> is the share of all qualifying requests per model group in a 7-day period ending October 13, 2026.</li> <li><strong>Interoperability.</strong> Counts public repositories created each year whose name, description, topics, or README mention interoperability or reverse engineering, or related terms, such as clean room implementation, drop-in replacement, or protocol re-implementation.</li> <li><strong>Forecasts.</strong> We ran 43 time-series and regression models on historical GitHub data, sign-ups, product usage, and market sizing, projecting developers on GitHub to September 2031 relative to September 2026. Outcomes group as 3x or more (21 models), 2x to under 3x (19), and under 2x (3). Octoverse 2025 used five models. No forecast is perfectly accurate.</li> </ul> <p>The post <a href="https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-commits-pushes-reach-new-highs-in-2026-as-developers-build-with-agents/">Octoverse: Commits, pushes, reach new highs in 2026 as developers build with agents</a> appeared first on <a href="https://github.blog">The GitHub Blog</a>.</p>

Helicopters and other VTOLs have a less orderly life than fixed-wing planes do. They must hover, sidestep, turn in place, settle, climb, and hold themselves in the air by main force. This is one reason why so many electric aircraft designs appear to have sprouted propellers wherever an engineer found a place to put one.</p><p>There is a downside to using multiple smaller motors: If one of the motors quits, the rotor it’s driving has little stored energy compared to the inertia in a conventional helicopter’s one large rotor. And you say, ‘You can actually live here and have a high-paying job in a mission-driven business, doing something that you care about with great people.’ ” The company currently employs about 1,500 people, most of them in South Burlington, Vt., and is actively hiring for its engineering, manufacturing, software, flight operations, and commercial teams.</p><p>As befits an aviation company, Beta offers flying lessons for all employees—a perk that is intended to give engineers, software developers, pilots, and mechanics a common language. The company also operates a health clinic for employees and their families and is building a day care center. Those programs have an idealistic Vermont flavor that is shaped by Clark and his wife, <a href="https://investors.beta.team/governance/leadership-team" target="_blank">Katie Clark</a>, who is Beta’s head of brand, creative, culture and spaces. But they also serve a practical purpose: attracting and retaining the best people in a small labor market. <em>—T.C.</em></p><p>Electric-aircraft makers compensate with redundant motors and electrical channels, but most leading designs also have wings to allow gliding. In Beta’s A250, that means that if all else fails, the pilot still has a shot at reaching a suitable landing spot.</p><p>That helps explain the convergence in today’s eVTOL designs. <a href="https://www.jobyaviation.com/aircraft/" target="_blank">Joby Aviation</a>’s S4 uses six propellers that tilt between lift and cruise. <a href="https://www.archer.com/aircraft" target="_blank">Archer Aviation’s Midnight</a> has six tilting propellers and six dedicated lift props. Beta’s A250 keeps its four lift propellers horizontal and uses a separate pusher motor for forward flight.</p><h2>Beta’s Approach to Batteries</h2><p><span><span>In Beta’s home state of Vermont, the electricity produced in the state comes almost entirely from renewable sources. Of course, electric aviation’s climate advantage depends not only on how clean the power grid is, but on how efficiently the aircraft uses energy. The turbines in conventional jet engines are marvelous machines, but they throw away a lot of energy as heat. Plus they need to carry the hardware to manage that heat—inlets, ducts, radiators, oil coolers—and the drag that comes with pulling air through all of it. </span></span></p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-rebelmouse-image rm-float-left rm-resized-container rm-resized-container-25" style="float: left;"> <img alt="A photo showing a bearded white man wearing a helmet and piloting an aircraft" class="rm-shortcode" id="c7d60" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/a-photo-showing-a-bearded-white-man-wearing-a-helmet-and-piloting-an-aircraft.jpg?id=68518211&amp;width=980" /></p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-rebelmouse-image rm-float-left rm-resized-container rm-resized-container-25" style="float: left;"> <img alt=" A photo showing a small white plane flying above a shoreline" class="rm-shortcode" id="514ad" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/a-photo-showing-a-small-white-plane-flying-above-a-shoreline.jpg?id=68518209&amp;width=980" /><small class="image-media media-caption">Kyle Clark [in cockpit], founder and CEO of Beta Technologies, pilots the company’s Alia CX300, an electric plane that Beta expects to receive FAA certification in 2027. For now, the hardest limit on electric aviation is not imagination, software, or even motors. It is the stubborn weight of stored energy.</p><p>“One thing you notice about [electric] airplanes is that they are all small,” says Cecutta, the aviation analyst. “They are not small because someone has done a market study and decided that is the optimal size. They are small because you cannot make them big. This limitation is from the batteries.”</p><p>In a conventional aircraft, fuel accounts for a significant percentage of the takeoff weight. But that weight gradually disappears during flight. In an electric plane, the battery packs also account for a lot of the weight; in Beta’s CX300, they’re close to half the aircraft’s mass. But a drained battery at landing weighs the same as a fully charged one at takeoff. That means the rest of the aircraft must be light enough to lift a power source that never gets lighter, yet strong enough to endure vibration, turbulence, hard landings, and years of fatigue. The more engineers push to save weight, Datta notes, the more flexible the airframe becomes, making the wings and other structures more prone to bending, twisting, and vibration. In that sense, the battery problem becomes a structural problem, too.</p><p>Beta’s CX300 carries five liquid-cooled <a href="https://beta.team/battery" target="_blank">battery packs</a> of about 45 kilowatt-hours each, giving the aircraft roughly 225 kWh of rated capacity, though not all of that may be usable in flight. Altogether, about 14,000 cells sit beneath the occupied cabin, each one a tiny storehouse of energy, heat, and potential trouble.</p><p>The burden of the battery explains much of what I see elsewhere in Beta’s aircraft: the 15-meter wingspan, the composite structure, and the almost doctrinal effort to leave out every piece of hardware and creature comfort that’s not essential to the mission. Cells become modules, modules become subpacks, and eleven subpacks become a 45-kWh pack. Cooling plates manage heat during normal operation, while passive barriers and electrical isolation are designed to contain the ugly edge cases that Beta’s testing process intentionally triggers.</p><p>The modular design of the packs will give Beta’s planes a path to better batteries as the cells and battery chemistry improve. Caputo tells me that the company is already evaluating higher-density cells at its battery testing facility in nearby St. Albans, Vt. More watt-hours per kilogram could eventually mean more range, more payload, or both.</p><h2>Beta Technologies’ Electric Ecosystem</h2><p>What sets Beta Technologies apart from its competitors is the breadth of its ambitions: It’s not just building electric aircraft.
