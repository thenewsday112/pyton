Voyager 1 gives controllers the silent treatment

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

<p>Voyager 1 gave controllers the silent treatment for a few days this month before resuming communication weeks before the spacecraft reaches the one-light-day milestone.</p><p>The <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/voyager/2026/10/29/nasas-voyager-1-conserves-power-resolves-brief-communications-pause/" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">incident</a> happened on October 18: NASA's Deep Space Network (DSN) couldn't detect a signal from the probe. On October 19, on the next scheduled communication attempt, there was still nothing. Was this it? Had the decades-long galactic venture finally ended? </p><p>Things are operating on a knife's edge for the two veteran spacecraft, which will reach their half-century next year. <a href="https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2023/07/31/nasa-mistakenly-severs-communication-to-voyager-2/772623" target="_blank">Voyager 2 suffered an antenna pointing issue</a> in 2023, and a low power condition caused Voyager 1 to <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/voyager/2024/10/28/after-pause-nasas-voyager-1-communicating-with-mission-team/" target="_blank">unexpectedly turn off</a> one of its two radio transmitters.</p><p>The good news is that on October 20, the signal reappeared, leaving engineers wondering why Voyager 1 temporarily went silent.</p><p>Thus far, it seems a group of thrusters went offline and their backups were turned on. This could indicate onboard computers detected a problem and put the spacecraft into a safe mode.</p><p>Kareem Badaruddin, Voyager mission manager at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, said: "This is the first time we've seen the spacecraft do something like this, but we are also at a point in the mission where we have to expect the unexpected.</p><p>"We're relieved to be back in contact with Voyager 1 and are eager to understand what happened."</p><p><span class="italic m-italic ">The Register</span> <a href="https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2024/11/28/whats-it-like-to-be-an-engineer-on-the-voyager-project/727819" target="_blank">spoke to Badaruddin in 2024</a>, where he described the challenges of working with the veteran spacecraft, but he reckoned the work was the "best job at JPL."</p><p>The twin Voyager spacecraft have recently been through a procedure dubbed the "Big Bang," where controllers turned off onboard devices and switched to lower power alternatives. The result will extend the lifetime of the spacecraft by a few more years and postpone further instrument deactivation. It was completed first on Voyager 2, and then Voyager 1, six weeks later in September.</p><p>However, the brief lapse in communication highlights that despite the ingenuity of the team that keep the spacecraft running, the project must eventually come to an end. In 2026, Suzanne Dodd, Project Manager for the Voyager project, told <span class="italic m-italic ">The Register</span> <a href="https://www.theregister.com/science/2026/08/12/how-voyager-engineers-found-two-more-years-in-a-50-year-old-power-budget/5284906" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">what the end would look like</a>. </p><p>She said, "We're just going to one day not hear the signal from the DSN, and we're going to rush around and make sure that it's not something on the ground."</p><p>Months of listening will follow, before engineers throw in the towel and declare the mission over. And then? Maybe a farewell message sent to the last known location.</p><p>Thankfully, in this case, the signal returned, so no need for a goodbye transmission just yet.</p><p>In a few weeks, Voyager 1 will be so far away that it will take a full 24 hours for commands to reach it and another 24 hours for a response to reach Earth.</p><p>Communications blip notwithstanding, there is every chance both Voyager spacecraft will still be operational as they pass their half-century milestone next year and, technology and funding permitting, continue sending data to Earth for a good few years yet. ®</p>

NASA ponders a Hubble servicing mission or a controlled deorbit

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

<p>NASA has posted a contract opportunity notice, also known as a request for information, from commercial space orgs who can service the Hubble Space Telescope.</p><p>Hubble was always looked after by NASA astronauts flying on the Space Shuttle, yet the <a href="https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2024/05/14/it-is-15-years-since-the-last-hubble-servicing-mission/1403259" target="_blank">last Hubble Space Telescope (HST) servicing mission</a> was the STS-125 Space Shuttle flight, launched on May 11, 2009. At the time, scientists hoped the mission would add another five years of operational life to the venerable spacecraft. Almost 17 years on, the HST (aka Hubble) is still delivering science.</p><p>However, time is running out for the observatory. NASA reckons there is a 10 percent chance it could reenter the Earth's atmosphere by September 2032, rising to 50 percent by December 2033. <a href="https://www.theregister.com/science/2026/10/26/swift-operations-end-2004-spacecraft-headed-for-fiery-reentry/5305346" target="_blank">As the end of the Swift mission</a> showed, increased solar activity can cause greater-than-anticipated atmospheric drag.</p><p>Also, like Swift, Hubble has no propulsion system, and so when it reenters (as it will, without intervention), the reentry will be uncontrolled. Hubble is sufficiently massive that there is a remote chance somebody on the ground could have a very bad day when that happens.</p><p>As such, one mission option suggested by NASA is a vehicle to grapple the observatory and perform a controlled reentry and disposal.</p><p>However, there are other options. Something to boost the telescope's orbit up to at least 600 km, or another to "enhance its ongoing performance and increase its resilience and expected on-orbit lifetime."</p><p>Both are intriguing options, and important since, as NASA acknowledges, no replacement for Hubble is expected before the 2040s, when the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) could become operational. Despite the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the Nancy Roman Space Telescope, Hubble remains in high demand, with NASA reporting a 7:1 oversubscription.</p><p>Before becoming NASA Administrator, Jared Isaacman <a href="https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2022/09/30/nasa-spacex-study-how-to-boost-hubble-to-higher-orbit/1235860" target="_blank">advocated</a> a SpaceX mission to boost the Hubble, but the idea was ultimately rejected. Similar plans could now be dusted off. NASA's requirement is for a mission to occur by 2032, and not harm the Hubble.</p><p>A soft-capture mechanism was fitted to Hubble during the STS-125 mission, which will mean docking with the spacecraft will be easier than the grappling planned for Swift. Then, the question is what to do with it. A controlled reentry is one option. The other options – boosting to a higher altitude or "enhance its ongoing performance and increase its resilience" are intriguing. <a href="https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2019/10/09/mission-extension-vehicle-1-launches-to-save-space-from-zombie-satellites/691459" target="_blank">Northrop Grumman's Mission Extension Vehicle (MEV)</a>, for example, is capable of on-orbit servicing and extending the lifetime of satellites. The Hubble is an order of magnitude more challenging, but the technological hurdles can be overcome with time and funding.</p><p>Last year, <span class="italic m-italic ">The Register</span> <a href="https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2025/07/21/nasa-veteran-warns-hubble-faces-death-by-a-hundred-cuts/1311407" target="_blank">spoke</a> with Dr John Grunsfeld, one of the astronauts (now retired) who worked on Hubble during the STS-125 mission. Grunsfeld told us that the veteran spacecraft faced death by a thousand cuts as its budget was whittled away. The observatory itself <a href="https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2024/06/05/official-hubble-telescope-to-transition-to-single-gyro-mode/1056157" target="_blank">recently switched</a> from three to one gyro mode (the telescope uses gyros to control where it is pointed) to eke out a few more years of operational life.</p><p>At this point, <a href="https://sam.gov/workspace/contract/opp/2ecd9dbf4a3946b4968cc0ebaa75d0b3/view" rel="nofollow" target="_blank">NASA is looking for information and comment</a> – this isn't a request for proposals.
