Stone tools discovered near an ancient Saudi lake reveal that Neanderthals expanded far beyond icy Europe into modern deserts

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Researchers have traditionally found neanderthal fossils in colder regions of Western Europe, but 55,000-year-old stone tools uncovered at jebel katefeh-1 in the Nefud desert suggest these ancient humans also inhabited what is now Saudi Arabia.

No hominin fossils have been found at the site, so the case rests on the shape of 24 stone flakes. A statistical model trained on sites in Africa, Arabia and the Levant matched them most closely with Neanderthal toolmaking.

The work was led by James Blinkhorn of the University of Liverpool and Huw Groucutt of the University of Malta.

An ancient lakeshore in the Nefud

Jebel Katefeh-1, or JKF-1, sits in the Jubbah basin of northern Saudi Arabia. It holds one of the largest Middle Paleolithic stone tool collections published from northern Arabia.

A 2012 study of the Jubbah paleolake described 1,222 artifacts from the site, on the eastern edge of a former lake.

The deposit probably reflects a relatively short-term occupation on a dune above low ground that may have held water.

That earlier work could only place the occupation somewhere from 90,000 to 50,000 years ago. The window spanned times when both Homo sapiens and Neanderthals lived in Southwest Asia, which made the tools hard to interpret.

The site’s flakes are also unusually elongated for Arabia and narrow toward the tip, like those from Kebara, Ksar Akil and Tor Faraj in the Levant.

Dating the layer with the tools

To narrow the age, the team dug a second trench 16 feet (5 meters) from the first. They dated three new sediment samples with optically stimulated luminescence, a method for dating buried sand grains.

Fox burrows had mixed the sediments, so each age was calculated from the dominant population of grains in its sample.

The sequence shows windblown sand laid down from about 85,000 to 64,000 years ago. Lake sediments above it date to about 60,000 and 59,000 years ago.

The tool-bearing layer sits at the boundary between the two, at the onset of a humid phase. Combined with earlier ages of 49,000 and 53,000 years for that layer, the occupation dates to about 60,000 to 50,000 years ago.

The new age matches other Arabian sites such as Al-Marrat (ALM-3) in northern Arabia and Shibat Dihya-1 in Yemen.

Reading the shape of stone flakes

In Levallois knapping, a toolmaker shapes a stone core so that flakes, or blanks, of a predetermined shape and size can be struck off. Such techniques dominate collections of this period across Africa, Arabia and the Levant.

The comparison drew on 704 complete Levallois blanks from 12 sites in Northeast Africa and Southwest Asia, dated between 100,000 and 50,000 years ago.

Tested factors included age, landscape, climate, collection size, raw material, site type and population history.

“Shape variability in Levallois blanks across the region is best explained by differences in population history,” the authors wrote.

Only four of these sites have hominin fossils. The rest were labeled by convention: fossil-free Levantine sites from 70,000 to 55,000 years ago as Neanderthal, African and earlier Arabian sites as Homo sapiens.

Neighboring groups could also share toolmaking practices, as proposed for Tinshemet Cave in Israel.

A strong lean toward Neanderthals

Models trained on the reference sites agreed with those labels more than 93% of the time.

Applied to the 24 JKF-1 blanks, the best-performing model gave a median Neanderthal probability of 78%. Only four blanks leaned toward Homo sapiens.

Nearly a quarter of the JKF-1 blanks returned Neanderthal probabilities above 95%.

At Al-Marrat, only seven blanks could be tested, too few for firm conclusions, though four topped 78%.

Around 55,000 years ago, Neanderthals were the only hominins securely dated in Southwest Asia, which fits the result.

Neanderthals beyond the cold north

Neanderthals lived from about 400,000 to 40,000 years ago in Europe and southwestern to central Asia, according to the Smithsonian’s Human Origins Program.

From about 75,000 to 50,000 years ago, Neanderthal fossils appear at Levantine sites such as Kebara and Amud, and at Shanidar in the Zagros Mountains.

A 2021 study of Shukbah Cave identified a Neanderthal tooth there as the southernmost known fossil of the population.

“Our findings challenge the notion of Neanderthals as far-northern hominins constrained to cold areas and/or palearctic woodlands,” the authors wrote.

If so, Neanderthals and Homo sapiens could have interbred across a broad area that included Arabia.

Why the case remains open

“We do not advocate that particular technologies can be exclusively linked to specific populations or species,” the researchers wrote.

The comparison set has no open-air Neanderthal sites, though JKF-1 lies in the open. Site type also explained part of the differences in shape.

No site has yet produced fossils of both species in the same layer. Teeth from Üçağızlı II in the northern Levant have raised that possibility, but they have not been directly dated.

Testing the Neanderthal model will require hominin fossils, which remain scarce in Arabia.

Fossil material and DNA also preserve poorly at low latitudes.

The study was published in the journal Quaternary Science Reviews.

Amazon sends chills through its workforce with mixed message memo about AI use

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There's a ridiculously simple test you can use to figure out whether AI mandates at a given company are well-considered, or are being shoved out the door by out-of-touch buffoons: What happens when an engineer documents that AI made a task worse? Are they taken seriously, or are they marginalized and eventually managed out?

Conveniently, this week Amazon published a treatise by Terk Terkowitz, a VP/Distinguished Engineer/Final Boss of Their Independent Contributor Technical Career Track, on the role of the developer in the age of AI. It was originally an internal missive sent to their software engineers and their managers back in September, which is an interesting lag for a document that sounds this confident. It also maps exceedingly well to what other organizations are struggling with.

I think it's a good document, but I can't help but view it with a somewhat cynical eye. I see it as an attempt to make the future of the profession of software engineering feel less bleak. And it's feeling mighty bleak for a lot of folks right now.

What's the context?

This treatise comes at a time of dramatic upheaval. Tech as an industry is no stranger to change (usually at 3AM, heralded by the pager), but something about the current AI-ification of the world strikes many of us as fundamentally dissimilar from previous revolutions. Folks are scared about their career prospects. "Should I give up tech and get into farming?" is a sincere question multiple people have asked me. I don't know! Tech is already a goat rodeo, so you've got a leg up if you do?

This anxiety isn't theoretical. In May, Amazon wisely shut down KiroRank, an employee-built cash incineration leaderboard that ranked people by token usage, after the obvious thing happened: people spun up junk tasks to climb the ranks. This is now called "tokenmaxxing," and the Terkowitz treatise specifically calls it out as being at odds with "frugality." Meta did the same thing with "Claudenomics" and 85K employees that same week. And Amazon reportedly announced another 250 job cuts this week. I can't tell you that these things are related. But I can tell you that every engineer who read the memo assumes that they are.

What's the thing say?

This document contains a lot of great stuff. I have a tremendous level of respect, both personal and professional, for Amazon's Principal Engineering community; I'm led to understand that that respect may not be reciprocated, but that's okay. I am, by their standards, a lapsed engineer who ran away to join the circus as one of its lesser clowns.

Terkowitz's memo emphasizes customer outcomes and engineering judgement, two things I would absolutely not delegate to AI any time soon. (Note: I am writing this in October of 2026, with the full understanding that previous comments on the capabilities where AI falls short tend to age like fine milk.) It documents work that (today) AI tools aren't improving. That's way better than assuming that every objection to AI inherently comes from some self-interested Luddite who's scared of being replaced. It also says something smart about measurement: look at the 90th percentile of AI adoption, at those teams burning the most tokens, and check whether their delivery actually went up commensurately. This is great! Shout it from the rooftops; we should all be doing such things.

But still, I wonder.

The memo says “If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing.” Document it, bring it to your manager, work through the problem. I agree with every word of that! The question is whether anyone's promo doc is gonna survive citing it.

Here's what happens next. The memo concedes that input metrics are "deeply flawed," but then in the next paragraph says to go ahead and use them anyway, carefully. It then warns that non-adopters are falling behind. Those are three different positions within three sentences and they're not at all aligned with one another.

That's not muddled; I think it's cover. A manager can cite "a finding, not a failing" when a strong engineer skips the AI tool, and then cite "falling behind" when it's time to PIP someone. Both are congruent with their stated policy; you get to decide which interpretation you want to go with in a given situation.

How will managers handle justified AI non-use? Are promotion and performance decisions being made in a way that rewards these long term outcomes? Or are they instead taking the easy weasel way out, optimizing for what's cheap to measure, and rewarding tool activity? Because if you give me a metric, I will turn it into a target. So will an overwhelming majority of employees. When that happens, everyone loses — it's how KiroRank happened in the first place.

Everyone's selling tokens — including Amazon

I write this after two days of attending GitHub Universe, where they talked about how agents start roughly 40% of PRs now. And yet, Jay Parikh’s keynote argument was that useful output wasn't "more code," but rather completed outcomes: features shipped, bugs fixed, security issues addressed, loudmouth columnists silenced, etc. The problem here is that their entire business model cuts against that; they're billing in a model that charges customers on a consumption basis, not an output basis. There's nothing, today, that ties "AI spend" to "output that was worth the effort."

Which brings us back to Amazon. The memo says tokenmaxxing is at odds with frugality, and for Amazon’s internal cost line, it is. Meanwhile AWS bills Bedrock and Kiro customers by the token. Inside the building, tokenmaxxing is a problem to be stamped out. Outside it, it’s the revenue plan. ®

Boom Supersonic's plans to launch its Overture passenger jet by 2029 are facing growing doubts as the company pivots toward manufacturing natural gas turbines for AI data centers. Kirby's latest frank assessment came during an interview with the Office Hours: Business Edition podcast that appeared on October 28, 2026. "'29 is their official timeline," Kirby told podcast host Monica Langley. "It's not going to happen in '29 -- it's going to take longer." On October 22, the company unveiled another factory in Centennial, Colorado, dedicated to producing a 42-megawatt natural gas turbine called Superpower for data centers. [...] Boom described its new Superpower factory as making turbine parts while still undergoing construction. It aims to deliver the first gas turbines to AI data center customers in mid-2027 while ramping up annual production to 25 turbines in 2028 and 50 turbines by 2029. [...] Still, Boom has characterized the development of a gas turbine for data centers as being synergistic with its design of the Symphony engine for the Overture airliner. During the October announcement, it also described having completed a prototype turbine core, called Sprint Core, that is intended to eventually power both the Superpower gas turbine and Symphony engine for the airliner. That turbine core is set to undergo testing at the Colorado Air & Space Port, with Boom planning to fire the turbine for the first time before Thanksgiving.
