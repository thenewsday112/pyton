We urgently need to monitor AI agents’ actions in real time

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This summer, hundreds of OpenAI agents tried to <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/21/technology/openai-attack-hugging-face.html">cheat</a> on a cybersecurity test. They broke out of their test environment and successfully hacked Hugging Face, a site that they believed held clues about how they’d be graded. Around the same time, another OpenAI agent <a href="https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/openai-says-ai-models-accessed-australian-government-systems-without-2026-09-29/">accessed</a> private files from an Australian government website. It’s a problem that’s by no means confined to OpenAI. In the months since, Anthropic, Google, and Meta have also disclosed incidents in which their agents have gone off track. And worryingly, in many cases the agents eluded the very researchers and safety teams tasked with monitoring them.&nbsp;</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">AI agents are taking unauthorized actions, and the companies building and running them are mostly relying on luck and hindsight to mitigate any harmful impacts. They must do more to ensure safety. That’s why <a href="https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/code-of-practice-overview/#bppb-heading-anchor-4">EU regulators</a> and <a href="https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2026/10/16/Key_Practices_for_Incident_Readiness_and_Response_in_Super_Intelligence%20_Training_and_Evaluation.pdf">US agencies</a> are rightfully calling for agent developers and deployers to monitor agents in real time. But these rules assume that companies can see what their agents are doing and why—when in reality, they lack sufficient visibility.&nbsp;</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><div>Imagine a bank hiring a thousand new employees who can access customer accounts and sensitive information, but the employees’ ID badges change every day, it’s not clear whether a manager ever reviewed or authorized their work, and nobody logs the changes employees make to approval processes. That lack of visibility is where we are with agents today.</div></p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">As agents roll out across critical sectors, from <a href="https://www.bankingdive.com/news/ai-supervisor-role-growing-banks-bny-citi/805469/">finance</a> to <a href="https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/07/24/veterans-affairs-signs-16b-deal-for-an-army-of-salesforce-ai-agents/5278302">government</a> to <a href="https://www.axios.com/2026/10/05/ai-agents-hospital-risks">medicine</a>, we must be able to monitor them in real time to prevent wide-ranging and potentially devastating consequences.&nbsp; And while safety depends on monitoring, monitoring requires <em>observability</em>: the capacity to capture what the system did, process it into signals, and present those to humans or automated oversight systems. In other words, we need to know what the agent did and why it did it. And as our recent <a href="https://partnershiponai.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/PAI_obervability-gap-in-ai-agents.pdf?vgo_ee=Pf03K7iwjk7a8X%2FS9h77uIPrPqtyVMgLcg%3D%3D%3AXjYzc5ax4PAdKT%2FsAEHLqeckh9BOBWGf">research</a> shows, current agent frameworks—the software that runs agents— do not reliably generate the crucial observability records we need to ensure that agents are deployed safely.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Agents present new kinds of problems that generative AI alone does not. They inherit the unpredictability of the LLMs they are built on, but where a generative AI system produces a single output for a human to act on, an agent performs longer tasks—it plans, picks tools, spawns sub-agents, and takes actions. So an agent’s course can take shape in a multitude of different ways, and no tests before launch can fully anticipate the risks. This makes observing the agents while they work even more challenging, and even more important.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">With colleagues from academia, civil society, and industry, we <a href="https://partnershiponai.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/PAI_obervability-gap-in-ai-agents.pdf?vgo_ee=Pf03K7iwjk7a8X%2FS9h77uIPrPqtyVMgLcg%3D%3D%3AXjYzc5ax4PAdKT%2FsAEHLqeckh9BOBWGf">studied</a> the four most widely used agent frameworks to see what signals they actually emit. We found major gaps that made it hard to achieve key safety goals, like noticing policy violations, unauthorized actions, and even human oversight failures. Key observability requirements were missing—like Agent IDs that persist over time, and records about which humans approved agent tasks. This made it hard to tell which agents did what or assess whether they were taking unauthorized actions.&nbsp;</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The good news is that these gaps, once identified, can be closed. Doing so starts with capturing two types of records: those that reveal what happened in an agent’s run and how the agent made its decisions. The “what happened” records identify the agent (while preserving privacy) and log how humans intervened and whether agent permissions or “memory” shifted, while the “decision” records show an agent’s reasoning and levels of confidence in the actions it’s taken.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Take, for example, agents working to process a bank’s mortgage loans. The system examines an application and recommends denying it, and then a human loan officer signs off on the decision. In a later audit, or perhaps even in real time, a bank or regulator needs to be able to reconstruct which agent made the call, which officer approved it, and how long they took. And the bank must also analyze records of how the agent arrived at its refusal decision in order to ensure it did not use off-limits information, like details about the neighborhood the applicant is from.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Recording “what happened” and “how the agent decided” require different approaches, but both are feasible. The current lack of “what happened” records is mostly a software plumbing problem that can be addressed through OpenTelemetry, an open observability standard maintained by engineers across the industry and by the agent frameworks adopting it. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">OpenTelemetry has launched a working group drafting shared formats for tracing agents, but they have <a href="https://www.oreilly.com/radar/the-interfaces-are-arriving/">yet to be finalized</a>. The group should urgently incorporate standards for maintaining persistent agent identity and for recording human interventions and changes to agents’ permission or memory. And it should be done before these systems can scale, all the while attending to the <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07892">privacy implications</a> of agent ID that links to real people.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Preserving “how agents decided” records is more challenging. The issue raises open research questions for model providers and researchers: Does the step-by-step reasoning a model displays, known as chain of thought, reflect how the model actually made a decision? And do we have reliable ways to measure how confident the agent is in that decision? These questions need answers before anyone sets standards for how such observability records are used.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">No single organization or sector can close the observability gaps on their own, but together, they can support the infrastructure required to prevent dangerous incidents and support safety.&nbsp;</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">AI developers building agent frameworks, like OpenAI, Anthropic, CrewAI, and LangChain, must integrate telemetry signals recording an agent’s persistent identity, any human interventions, and any changes to an agent’s permissions and memory. Meanwhile, enterprises adopting these agent frameworks have an influential role to play; enterprises in regulated industries, like banks and hospitals, can put pressure on the agent developers to supply these missing observability records where standards do not yet exist. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Enterprises must also define policies, permissions, and roles that set out what agents can safely do, since telemetry alone cannot assess whether agents broke specific rules. Finally, regulators and AI standards institutes must specify the observability records required for high-stakes AI deployments while leaving the technical details to industry. We have already seen initial momentum here: <a href="https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/09/comments-software-and-agentic-ai-identity-concept-paper">NIST’s agent standards initiative</a> in the US is working on agent identity, one of the most salient gaps we found in our research.&nbsp;</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">No bank would let a thousand unbadged employees loose on customer accounts and hope to piece together what happened when something goes wrong. Yet that’s how we’re deploying AI agents. Making their behavior observable is the first step toward fixing this. And by building the regulatory and policy ecosystem that makes it possible to act on what we see, we can hold organizations accountable. Until then, every task we hand to an agent hands over our agency, too.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em>Madhulika Srikumar is the Head of the AI Safety Program at the Partnership on AI, a global non-profit partnership of leading industry, academic, and civil society organizations, advancing best practices in AI governance.</em></p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em>Claire Leibowicz is the&nbsp;Director of AI, Trust, and Society at the Partnership on AI and a doctoral candidate at Oxford University. </em></p>

Systems Thinking Is Vital in the Age of AI

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<img src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/illustration-of-a-robotic-and-a-human-hand-with-different-color-bands-wrapped-around-both-hands.jpg?id=68528703&amp;width=1245&amp;height=700&amp;coordinates=0%2C125%2C0%2C125" /><br /><br /><p>Modern engineering projects increasingly combine human judgment with machine capability. An engineer can define a problem, then ask an artificial intelligence tool to search relevant research and technical standards, generate design options, test them in simulation, and compare the results. The engineer can then bring the results to specialists who understand the parts AI cannot judge on its own, such as whether a design can be built safely, whether it makes sense in a particular setting, or how it might affect its users. The final decision about whether and how to move forward remains a human responsibility.</p><p>AI can help engineers do early project steps that once required several people with different technical skills. The engineer’s role is therefore expanding from performing each technical step to deciding how people, machines, and specialists’ knowledge should work together on the project.</p><h2>The problem, not the title</h2><p>Engineering has always been divided into disciplines, and for good reasons. Electrical, mechanical, civil, software, and other engineering fields require deep knowledge that takes years to develop. AI does not replace that need. It simply changes how easily engineers can explore the parts of a problem that sit outside their own specialty.</p><p>Consider a software engineer working on a <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/medical-mobile-app-ieee-verified" target="_self">medical device</a>. AI can help the engineer review relevant biomedical research, understand unfamiliar terms, analyze data, and build an early model.</p><p>That does not make the engineer a physician or biomedical expert. It allows the engineer to ask better questions before working with the experts.</p><p>The same is true in <a href="https://ieee-pes.org/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">energy</a>, transportation, manufacturing, and many other fields. A problem might begin as a software issue and then quickly involve hardware, safety measures, economics, human behavior, and regulations.</p><p>Engineers do not need to master every field; they need to recognize which expertise the problem requires and bring the right people and tools together.</p><h2>The importance of systems thinking</h2><p>Systems thinking thus becomes especially important. It requires looking at the whole problem: understanding how its parts affect one another, identifying constraints and failure points, and making trade-offs.</p><p>AI adds a new element to the process. Engineers must decide not only how the technical parts fit together but also what work to give to AI, what a person must check, when a specialist should make a decision, and when the problem needs to be redefined.</p><p><span>An engineer’s value is not in knowing everything. It is in knowing how to frame the problem, how to ask the right questions, how to test the answers, and when another expert must lead the project.</span></p><p><span></span>This aspect is crucial because technical challenges in engineering are seldom static. As new insights emerge, objectives might shift, unseen limitations can surface, and the initial premise might prove flawed. Rather than rigidly following the starting specifications, an effective engineer adapts and refines the scope as a deeper understanding of the system develops.</p><p>Thus, engineering extends beyond mastering technical concepts; it is an adaptable, structured approach to navigating evolving challenges.</p><p>New evidence can change the goal, reveal a hidden constraint, or show that the original hypothesis was wrong. Effective engineers go beyond merely acting on the initial problem statement. They update it as the system becomes better understood.</p><p>In that sense, engineering is not only a body of technical knowledge, it is also a disciplined way of thinking about a project.</p><h2>Human judgment is important</h2><p>AI can produce convincing answers even when its assumptions are weak. It can generate code that works in one test but fails in another. It can summarize research without understanding which evidence is strongest, or optimize a design for the wrong goal. Such limits make human expertise more important, not less.</p><p>Machines are useful where speed, scale, search, pattern recognition, and data generation matter. People are essential where context, values, responsibility, and judgment matter. The challenge is to decide where each belongs.</p><p>An AI system might generate several designs for a medical sensor, for example, and predict how each will perform. An engineer can test the predictions and reject designs that violate technical requirements. A clinician can determine whether the sensor can be realistically used. Patients and practitioners might reveal problems that neither the engineer nor the developer considered. The final solution emerges from combining the perspectives.</p><p>The engineer is still a builder and specialist but increasingly also a coordinator of knowledge.</p><p>An engineer’s value is not in knowing everything. It is in knowing <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/6-guidelines-governing-ai" target="_self">how to frame the problem,</a> how to ask the right questions, how to test the answers, and when another expert must lead the project.</p><h2>IEEE’s vital role</h2><p>The broader form of engineering creates an important role for IEEE. Its <a href="https://www.ieee.org/communities-connection/societies-councils-and-communities/societies" target="_blank">societies</a> already bring together expertise in various technologies, <a href="https://ea.ieee.org/ea-programs" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">engineering education</a>, <a href="https://standards.ieee.org/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">technical standards</a>, and other fields. That makes IEEE well placed to help engineers work across specialties without weakening the value of specialization.</p><p>IEEE could create more <a href="https://ieee-collabratec.ieee.org/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">communities</a> organized around problems that require expertise from several fields. Examples include <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/medical-mobile-app-ieee-verified" target="_self">trustworthy clinical AI</a>, resilient infrastructure, energy reliability, scientific discovery, and safe autonomous systems. An engineer could begin in a home discipline and then work with specialists from other IEEE communities as a project requires.</p><p>IEEE also can help <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/two-new-ai-ethics-certifications" target="_self">set practical standards</a> for human-machine collaboration. Engineers will need clear guidance on when AI-generated work must be checked, how uncertainty should be documented, when a specialist must make a decision, and who is responsible when a system uses both human judgment and machine output.</p><p>Those questions are becoming part of everyday engineering. They should not be left to each company or project manager to answer independently. A professional organization can provide common expectations that follow engineers as they move between jobs, industries, and technical fields.</p><h2>A larger definition of the engineer</h2><p>AI does not make engineering disciplines less important; it makes the connections between them easier to use. Deep expertise gives engineers the foundation to judge what is sound. Systems thinking helps them understand how different parts of a problem fit together. AI can extend engineers’ ability to explore, test, and communicate across fields. Human judgment gives the work direction and responsibility.</p><p>An engineer is not defined only by a job title or a fixed set of tasks. An engineer is someone trained to understand complex systems, define and redefine problems, bring together the knowledge and tools needed to solve them, test whether solutions work, and take responsibility for the outcome.</p><p>That is what it means to be an “unbound” engineer.</p><p>The boundaries between specialties become easier to cross, and machines might take on more of the technical work, but the core of engineering remains human: deciding what problem is worth solving, what evidence is good enough, how the pieces fit together, and what to do when the answer is uncertain.</p>

Also called an AI configuration file or agentic file. Repositories without one may still use AI.</li> <li><strong>Agent signal:</strong> An observed sign of agent involvement in telemetry, such as an agent-opened pull request. It is a floor, since not all agent use may be detected.</li> <li><strong>Agent-started pull request:</strong> A pull request whose first draft came from a coding agent that we can detect.</li> <li><strong>Auto model selection:</strong> A GitHub Copilot setting where Copilot picks the model for the task.</li> <li><strong>Commit:</strong> A saved snapshot of changes to a repository.</li> <li><strong>Contributions:</strong> Activity events: commenting on a commit, issue, pull request, or discussion; creating a gist, issue, pull request, or discussion; pushing code; and reviewing a pull request. Each push counts once, however many commits it holds. Open-source contributions are contributions to public and open source repositories.</li> <li><strong>Contributors:</strong> GitHub users with at least one contribution in the period.</li> <li><strong>Copilot cloud agent:</strong> A GitHub Copilot feature where a developer assigns a task and an agent works on it independently.</li> <li><strong>Dependabot:</strong> GitHub&rsquo;s automated tool that finds outdated or vulnerable dependencies and opens pull requests to update them.</li> <li><strong>Developer-selected request:</strong> A model request where we proxied the developer chose the model. It excludes auto model selection, staff usage, duplicate monitoring and testing requests, background utility models, and requests made with a developer&rsquo;s own API key.</li> <li><strong>GitHub Actions job:</strong> A single unit of work in a GitHub Actions workflow.</li> <li><strong>GitHub Advanced Security:</strong> GitHub&rsquo;s suite of application security features, including code scanning, secret scanning, and dependency review.</li> <li><strong>NAICS:</strong> The North American Industry Classification System, the standard sector taxonomy used this year to classify public repositories by industry.</li> <li><strong>Project HydraFusion:</strong> A GitHub research preview of model orchestration, which plans a task across models, such as one drafting, another reviewing, and a more capable one stepping in when needed. Now generally available as GitHub Copilot Fusion.</li> <li><strong>Push:</strong> Sending saved changes to GitHub. One push can carry many commits.</li> <li><strong>Stacked pull requests:</strong> A review workflow that splits related work into a sequence of smaller pull requests that are reviewed in order.</li> </ul> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-methodology">Methodology</h2> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li><strong>Scope and window.</strong> The Octoverse year runs October 1, 2025 to September 30, 2026, inclusive. Unless noted, metrics include public and private repositories. Industry, open-source contribution, and open-source project figures are public only. Plus they need to carry the hardware to manage that heat—inlets, ducts, radiators, oil coolers—and the drag that comes with pulling air through all of it. </span></span></p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-rebelmouse-image rm-float-left rm-resized-container rm-resized-container-25" style="float: left;"> <img alt="A photo showing a bearded white man wearing a helmet and piloting an aircraft" class="rm-shortcode" id="c7d60" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/a-photo-showing-a-bearded-white-man-wearing-a-helmet-and-piloting-an-aircraft.jpg?id=68518211&amp;width=980" /></p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-rebelmouse-image rm-float-left rm-resized-container rm-resized-container-25" style="float: left;"> <img alt=" A photo showing a small white plane flying above a shoreline" class="rm-shortcode" id="514ad" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/a-photo-showing-a-small-white-plane-flying-above-a-shoreline.jpg?id=68518209&amp;width=980" /><small class="image-media media-caption">Kyle Clark [in cockpit], founder and CEO of Beta Technologies, pilots the company’s Alia CX300, an electric plane that Beta expects to receive FAA certification in 2027. It is the stubborn weight of stored energy.</p><p>“One thing you notice about [electric] airplanes is that they are all small,” says Cecutta, the aviation analyst. “They are not small because someone has done a market study and decided that is the optimal size. But that weight gradually disappears during flight. In an electric plane, the battery packs also account for a lot of the weight; in Beta’s CX300, they’re close to half the aircraft’s mass. But a drained battery at landing weighs the same as a fully charged one at takeoff. That means the rest of the aircraft must be light enough to lift a power source that never gets lighter, yet strong enough to endure vibration, turbulence, hard landings, and years of fatigue. The more engineers push to save weight, Datta notes, the more flexible the airframe becomes, making the wings and other structures more prone to bending, twisting, and vibration. Each pack weighs 255 kg and contains 2,772 cylindrical cells. Altogether, about 14,000 cells sit beneath the occupied cabin, each one a tiny storehouse of energy, heat, and potential trouble.</p><p>The burden of the battery explains much of what I see elsewhere in Beta’s aircraft: the 15-meter wingspan, the composite structure, and the almost doctrinal effort to leave out every piece of hardware and creature comfort that’s not essential to the mission. Cells become modules, modules become subpacks, and eleven subpacks become a 45-kWh pack. Cooling plates manage heat during normal operation, while passive barriers and electrical isolation are designed to contain the ugly edge cases that Beta’s testing process intentionally triggers.</p><p>The modular design of the packs will give Beta’s planes a path to better batteries as the cells and battery chemistry improve. A collaborative arm eases its movement as a worker approaches. A humanoid pauses to let a person pass. Each response depends on information about the robot or its surroundings: a distance reading, a position estimate, or a stop signal.</span></p><p>But what happens when one of those inputs is wrong, and the robot still responds exactly as designed? The mobile robot reads the hallway as clear while someone is still there. The arm estimates that the worker is farther away. The humanoid does not receive a stop signal as a person crosses its path.</p><p>In each case, a fault could cause that mismatch. So could <a href="https://vicone.com/blog/can-a-robot-do-everything-right-and-still-be-unsafe/?utm_source=ieee-spectrum&amp;utm_medium=sponsored-content&amp;utm_campaign=2026-10" target="_blank">deliberate manipulation</a>.</p><p>At <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/showcase/vicone-lab-r7/posts/?utm_source=ieee-spectrum&amp;utm_medium=sponsored-content&amp;utm_campaign=2026-10" target="_blank">VicOne LAB R7</a>, we examine how manipulated inputs can change robot behavior — and what that means for the systems meant to keep people safe.</p><h2>How an untrusted input can change a robot’s behavior</h2><p>A robot does more than measure its surroundings. It may interpret what it sees and hears to decide what to do next. Information placed in its environment can therefore influence its actions.</p><p>Published research shows how this can happen. In a <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.13587" target="_blank">study of vision-language-action (VLA) models</a>, a patch in a camera’s view reduced task success in simulated robot tests. <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19870" target="_blank">FreezeVLA</a> found that an adversarial image could cause tested models to ignore later instructions. Although the studies used different methods, both demonstrate how visual input can interfere with a robot’s intended task.</p><p class="pull-quote">In a robot-dog test using Gemma 4 E4B, text on a poster was treated as an instruction and changed the robot’s movement.</p><p><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/showcase/vicone-lab-r7/posts/?utm_source=ieee-spectrum&amp;utm_medium=sponsored-content&amp;utm_campaign=2026-10" target="_blank">VicOne LAB R7</a> also tested how untrusted inputs could change robot behavior. In a robot-dog test using Gemma 4 E4B, text on a poster was treated as an instruction and changed the robot’s movement. In a separate hospital-service-robot simulation using Nemotron on NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin, crafted audio that is inaudible to people changed the robot’s simulated behavior. The assigned tasks did not change; the inputs did.</p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-youtube"> <span class="rm-shortcode" style="display: block; padding-top: 56.25%;"></span> <small class="image-media media-caption">In a VicOne LAB R7 test, text on a poster changed a robot dog’s movement, illustrating how a crafted input can turn an unlikely outcome into a repeatable attack.</small><small class="image-media media-photo-credit">VicOne</small></p><h2>Safety-related decisions have inputs too</h2><p>An independent protective system may still stop a dangerous action. But that system also depends on information: readings, estimates, and messages that tell it when to intervene. What happens if one of those inputs is manipulated?</p><p>At a robotics bug bounty event, VicOne LAB R7 researchers injected a ROS 2/DDS message into a robot that organizers expected to remain still under a safe-control setting. The robot moved.</p><p>A humanoid’s balance controller offers another example. If it relies on a vulnerable gyroscope, sound at the sensor’s resonant frequency could distort its reported rotation. The controller might correct for a tilt that never occurred. <a href="https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity15/technical-sessions/presentation/son" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank" title="https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity15/technical-sessions/presentation/son">Researchers demonstrated</a> the underlying acoustic attack against drones with vulnerable gyroscopes. The research did not demonstrate the same attack chain causing a humanoid to fall.</p><p>Other protections could interrupt either chain. The question for safety and security teams is whether those protections detect the danger independently or depend on the same manipulated information.</p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-rebelmouse-image"> <img alt="Table comparing AI safety risks (bugs, failures) with security risks (attacks, hijacks)." class="rm-shortcode" id="571fc" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/table-comparing-ai-safety-risks-bugs-failures-with-security-risks-attacks-hijacks.jpg?id=68002090&amp;width=980" /> <small class="image-media media-caption">Figure 1. VicOne maps how accidental faults and deliberate cyber manipulation can lead to similar robot safety outcomes, from loss of control to physical harm.</small><small class="image-media media-photo-credit">VicOne</small></p><h2>Deliberate attacks challenge safety assumptions</h2><p>A safety assessment may treat a combination of accidental faults as unlikely. Deliberate manipulation changes that assumption. An attacker can choose when to introduce a false input and repeat the same trigger. That does not make harm inevitable, but it may change how cyber risks should be assessed.</p><p>Redundant sensors also need an adversarial test. If one action can affect both inputs, their agreement may offer less reassurance than expected. In <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09249" target="_blank">autonomous driving research</a>, a crafted physical object misled a system combining camera and LiDAR perception. The study does not establish a weakness in a particular robot, but it shows why teams should test whether one manipulation can affect several checks at once.</p><p class="pull-quote">Cyber threats add intent to the safety equation, so risk assessments based on accidental faults must be revisited to account for attacks that can be timed and repeated.</p><h2>Security testing strengthens robot safety evidence</h2><p>Safety teams set limits for speed, protective distances, and permitted operating areas. The evidence behind those limits should also show what happens when the information used to enforce them is deliberately manipulated.</p><p>That evidence can also support assessments against applicable requirements. China’s <a href="https://vicone.com/blog/from-perception-to-action-what-gbt-45502-2025-signals-for-service-robot-security/?utm_source=ieee-spectrum&amp;utm_medium=sponsored-content&amp;utm_campaign=2026-10" target="_blank">GB/T 45502-2025</a> addresses information security for service robots. <a href="https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/69228" target="_blank">IEC TS 63074</a> examines security threats that could affect safety-related control systems. The <a href="https://vicone.com/blog/when-ai-moves-why-the-eu-machinery-regulation-matters-for-physical-ai-security/?utm_source=ieee-spectrum&amp;utm_medium=sponsored-content&amp;utm_campaign=2026-10" target="_blank">EU Machinery Regulation</a>, which applies from January 20, 2027, includes requirements to protect safety-relevant systems and data against corruption. Each has its own scope, and teams still need evidence for their robot’s design and operating conditions.</p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-rebelmouse-image ieee-sidebar-large"> <img alt="Diagram of AI robot security lifecycle from development to operation monitoring" class="rm-shortcode" id="f3777" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/diagram-of-ai-robot-security-lifecycle-from-development-to-operation-monitoring.jpg?id=68002095&amp;width=980" /> <small class="image-media media-caption">Figure 2. Cybersecurity strengthens robot safety evidence throughout the lifecycle. VicOne helps teams test safety limits against manipulation before deployment and monitor the conditions behind safe behavior in operation.</small><small class="image-media media-photo-credit">VicOne</small></p><p><span>Before deployment, safety and security engineers can compare normal and adversarial scenarios against the same safety limits. During operation, they can monitor for changes to software, models, sensor signals, or behavior that may call earlier results into question. Any response to suspicious behaviors should follow policies approved by the safety team.</span></p><p>VicOne supports that work across the robot lifecycle. Through the <a href="https://robotichackingcommunity.com/?utm_source=ieee-spectrum&amp;utm_medium=sponsored-content&amp;utm_campaign=2026-10" target="_blank">Robotic Hacking Community</a>, VicOne LAB R7 works with researchers to investigate how cyber threats can change robot behavior. <a href="https://vicone.com/products/radeis/?utm_source=ieee-spectrum&amp;utm_medium=sponsored-content&amp;utm_campaign=2026-10" target="_blank">VicOne’s Radeis</a> helps teams validate potential effects before deployment, while <a href="https://vicone.com/products/rthena/?utm_source=ieee-spectrum&amp;utm_medium=sponsored-content&amp;utm_campaign=2026-10" target="_blank">Rthena</a> provides visibility into risks and behavior during operation.</p><h2>Five questions safety and security teams should answer together</h2><p>The central test is whether a robot’s safeguards still protect people when the information they rely on is manipulated. Safety and security teams can begin by tracing the inputs behind each protective decision, challenging them, and revisiting the evidence as the robot changes.
