The growing AI landscape is giving enterprises more choice in how they build, customize, and deploy increasingly capable models. Open-weight models are an important part of that landscape. They give enterprises access to model weights and greater flexibility to inspect, evaluate, modify, and run models within their own environments.

While that flexibility can be valuable, it also affects how enterprises evaluate models, where they implement safeguards, and who is responsible for maintaining those safeguards over time.

As enterprises choose which models to deploy, the relevant question is broader than whether a model is open-weight or closed-weight. Enterprises need to understand what they are using the model for, what could go wrong in that context, and what evidence and controls they need to manage risks.

Today, Microsoft and NVIDIA are publishing Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise, the first in a planned series of joint resources intended to help enterprises address those questions. The paper introduces a shared risk taxonomy and an approach to managing model risks grounded in established standards and frameworks, including International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standards and the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework.

The paper reflects a core idea: weight openness alone does not determine an AI system’s risk. Risk depends on the model, use case, deployment, and safeguards.

Read the whitepaper: Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise
Understanding risk in the deployment context
Open- and closed-weight models can share many of the same underlying risks, but how those risks materialize depends on the deployment. A model used to help an employee draft a document presents different considerations than the same model used in a customer-facing workflow, just as a standalone inference endpoint differs from a system connected to external data or tools.

The architecture matters too. A risk may originate in the model, while the most effective control may sit in the inference environment, application, or surrounding operational processes. The joint guidance looks beyond the model label to factors including provenance, intended use, users, deployment conditions, and surrounding controls. This first paper focuses primarily on model-layer risks. Evaluating a model alone does not establish the safety of an agent or application: teams must also assess its permissions, data access, tools, runtime, and operational controls.

Establishing a common language
Managing these risks consistently also requires a common vocabulary across engineering, security, legal, safety, and business teams.

The initial Microsoft and NVIDIA taxonomy organizes risks into five broad categories: Cybersecurity, Data Protection, Content, Human-AI Interaction, and Bias. It is not a policy or deployment checklist, but instead it should be used by enterprises to adapt it to their own systems, use cases, and risk criteria, then connect relevant risks to evaluations, mitigations, and accountable owners.

Using evaluations as risk evidence
Evaluations help turn the taxonomy into something enterprises can use. The paper maps evaluations to a selection of risks across the five categories, giving teams a way to start with a risk that matters for their deployment and identify evaluations that can produce relevant evidence about it.

Microsoft Foundry helps enterprises assess quality, groundedness, and safety with built-in and custom evaluations and automated red teaming. Its configurable content-safety controls help detect and mitigate harmful inputs and outputs and prompt attacks. Teams should validate control availability for their model and deployment, including preview status, and combine these capabilities with application-specific testing, monitoring, and human oversight.

Evaluations still need to be interpreted in the context of a particular deployment and the goal is to make it easier for teams to connect the risks they identify with useful evidence, while building a broader set of evaluation resources that can evolve alongside models, deployment patterns, and emerging risks. Microsoft and NVIDIA plan to use the joint repository to share additional evaluations and point to existing resources that can help enterprises assess specific risks.

Accounting for changes in control and responsibility
Open-weight access gives enterprises greater control over how a model is evaluated, modified, and deployed. Model access and deployment are separate choices: open-weight models can also be used through managed platforms. Responsibility for safeguards depends on the service and deployment configuration; enterprises that operate their own model deployments may take on controls otherwise provided by a managed service.

Those safeguards can operate at different layers, and (depending on the risk) enterprises might use model changes, access controls, input and output filtering, monitoring, or human review. The important step is connecting each identified risk to controls that can effectively address it within the deployed system. Those decisions need to be revisited as models and deployments change.

Building on this work
Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise focuses primarily on the model layer. Future work from Microsoft and NVIDIA is intended to extend this approach across the runtime, harness, and application layers, with additional technical guidance on evaluation and mitigation.

We also expect the resources supporting this work to evolve as new models, evaluations, and deployment patterns emerge. The joint repository will provide a place to share additional evaluation resources, including contributions from the broader community.

For enterprises deploying open-weight models today, the paper provides guidance they can begin applying to their own use cases. We encourage developers, security practitioners, researchers, and others working in this space to read the paper, explore the evaluations, and contribute evaluations that can help strengthen this shared body of work.

From Containers to Sandboxes: Developer Trust in the Agentic Era

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What the 2026 Stack Overflow Developer Survey tells us about where trust goes next

If you’ve followed Docker lately, you know we’re all-in on sandboxes. We’ve spent a ton of time talking about the difference between sandboxes and containers, and helping our customers and the industry as a whole understand when to use which. In fact, Docker president Mark Cavage dedicated his opening keynote at WeAreDevelopers World Congress North America to this exact topic, using live demos to show why sandboxes (and not containers) provide the level of isolation required to contain autonomous agents. 

To be clear: none of that means we suddenly think less of containers or the vital role they will continue to play in the software development process and ecosystem. We are Docker, after all. And lest anyone forget, as the just-released 2026 Stack Overflow Developer Survey confirmed yet again, when it comes to containers, there’s Docker, and there’s everyone else. Docker is the most used (59%) and most desired (38%) tool in the survey’s cloud development category.

Now, we can all agree that the tech industry is inundated with surveys these days. Vendors, analysts, research firms, everyone’s got a groundbreaking new survey. So, if your reflex response to reading about new survey results is to roll your eyes and think here we go again, well, I get it. 

But the annual Stack Overflow Developer Survey isn’t just another survey. When it comes to the developer community, it’s arguably the survey, the one developers themselves pay attention to. Over the course of 16 years, it has become something of a de facto standard for those of us interested in truly understanding how developers think and act. Released earlier this month, the 2026 survey reached more than 30,000 respondents across 169 countries.

Of course, this year’s survey did more than just remind us of Docker’s continued container dominance. It painted a picture of an industry in transition as developers grapple with one of the biggest questions in technology right now: how to bridge the gap between what they want to do with agents and what they can securely and confidently run in production. 

Consider:

  • Only 7% of developers trust AI output for important work decisions. Nearly half (48%) trust it only when they can verify it themselves.
  • Security and privacy ranked as the second biggest factor in whether teams adopt or expand AI tools (behind only accuracy). 
  • Nearly one in four developers said AI-related security or privacy issues cost their team valuable time in the past year.

Put it all together, and the missing ingredient is clear: trust. Developers want to tap into everything agents make possible without crossing their fingers and hoping “everything” doesn’t include a catastrophic security failure.

I didn’t point out those earlier Docker numbers just to brag (though, hey, I’m a marketer, so it was at least part of it). I mentioned them because they’re a reflection of trust — trust earned across more than a decade helping developers make software portable, open, reproducible, and secure. 

Today, we’re building on that foundation and extending that trust into the agentic era. At WeAreDevelopers, you saw the first steps. We launched Docker Cloud Sandboxes, published the next-generation Sandbox Kit specification (which we’ve committed to submitting to the Cloud Native Computing Foundation), and built nearly everything we showed around one idea: helping developers manufacture trust for AI agents. 

If it all looked and felt like a slightly different Docker, that was intentional. We’re at the start of a transformative era for Docker, and you’ll see a lot more from us in the coming weeks and months.

But no matter how much the industry changes, and no matter how much we evolve along with it, one thing won’t change. Docker will continue to run on trust.

If you’d like to see what that looks like in practice, try Docker Sandboxes (and get a $250 credit if you sign up before Oct. 31). You can start an agent on your laptop, ship longer jobs off to the cloud, and bring the results back. We’d love to hear what you think.

Is Your Organization Messing Up AI Adoption?

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The way I see most organizations roll out AI feels more like a farce than a strategy. If it were turned into a movie, reviewers would say it was unbelievable. Nobody could be that stupid.

Yet here we are. And what makes it all the more frustrating is that we have been here before.

Back in 2014, I wrote a book for Smashing Magazine called Digital Adaptation, looking at the mistakes being made in how organizations were approaching digital transformation and what they should be doing instead. The similarities between then and now are striking.

So in this post, I want to look back at Digital Adaptation and see how the lessons in that book apply to the AI era and what we can learn from the past to help us navigate the challenges of AI today.

The biggest mistake, both then and now, is approaching each as a technological challenge.

It’s About People, Not Technology

Sure, both digital transformation and AI are technological advances, and yes, some level of technological implementation is required. But that is the tip of a very large iceberg.

Back in the midst of the digital transformation era, IT departments were rolling out CMSs, CRM systems, intranets, and so on. Meanwhile, on the customer-facing side, organizations were rolling out websites, apps, and personalization. But they were putting almost no emphasis on how people were going to use these systems.

Internal systems were hard to use, and customers either didn’t care what was being built or wanted something else entirely.

The same is happening with AI. Senior management are buying into the hype, and IT teams are rolling out Copilot Studio licenses company-wide and considering the job done. Sure, there is a bit of organization-wide generic training, but that’s about it.

Just like before, staff are left wondering what to do with these new systems and feeling under pressure to use them and deliver results.

Organizations need to recognize that AI is a cultural shift as much as a technology, and that shift has enormous implications for their staff.

After all, this time around, it is even worse, because this time, staff fear for their jobs. They have read the same headlines that you have, and no amount of promises from senior management is going to make them feel any better.

Why Would Staff Want To Adopt AI?

I mean, think about it from a staff member’s point of view. AI offers them very little personally, even setting aside the fact that management may be intending to use it to replace them.

The main reason senior management are excited about AI is that it provides additional productivity. People can do more in less time. But that does not benefit staff. They are still required to work 9 to 5, but now they are expected to do more in the same time. They don’t get any time back, and they don’t get paid more, so why would they embrace AI?

So before asking staff to embrace AI, organizations need to ask themselves how they intend to motivate them to do so.

One strategy is to help staff identify the parts of their job they hate and how AI can help them do those tasks more quickly and less painfully. People tend to be a lot more receptive to change when it is presented as an opportunity to improve their working lives.

But, that should just be the beginning. Organizations may need to consider providing incentives for staff to adopt AI in terms of career progression, compensation, or shorter working hours.

Staff Need Time And Support To Adopt AI

Also, adopting AI is not a simple task. It involves challenging every working practice they have built up over (in some cases) decades. All of that procedural knowledge has to be rebuilt. That is a lot to ask of anybody in even the best of circumstances, and most employees do not find themselves in the best of circumstances.

Most are overworked and struggling to stay on top of their to-do lists. They don’t have time to sit down, learn a new tool, and figure out how it fits into their workflow.

That doesn’t just take time. It also takes a lot of consideration and experimentation. It is not enough to just send a member of staff on a training course and expect them to be able to use AI to its full potential.

At a minimum, staff need training specific to their roles, such as the AI training I provide to UX teams. But organizations should be going even further.

They need to develop sophisticated training programs that help staff from different departments understand how to use AI in their specific roles. They need ongoing support and the time to experiment with AI to find the best ways to use it.

But formal training will only take you so far. In my experience, the most effective support comes from colleagues, which is why I would start with the enthusiasts rather than the resisters. Every organization has a handful of people who are already experimenting with AI, often without anybody asking them to. Bring those people together, give them the time and space to share what is working for them, and let their enthusiasm spread to everyone else.

I saw this work during the digital transformation era too. In Digital Adaptation, I described an internal conference where staff showed their colleagues what they had built, and it did more for adoption than any memo from the top ever could. People are far more likely to try something when they see a colleague in a similar role using it successfully than when they are told to by senior management.

Rather than trying to win over the most resistant staff first, organizations should find the people already experimenting with AI, give them time to share what they have learned, and let social proof do the heavy lifting.

Unfortunately, experimentation comes with a big downside… mistakes will be made.

It Needs To Be Safe To Fail

Many organizations have, to be frank, a culture of fear where mistakes are seen as a sign of incompetence. However, by its very nature, learning a new tool like AI will involve making mistakes, and this can be a massive barrier to adoption.

I saw this during the era of digital transformation. Senior management would demand digital transformation and were confused when it didn’t happen.

What they didn’t realize was that they were sending mixed messages to staff. Sure, they were asking for digital transformation, but they were still demanding that the same key performance indicators (KPIs) be met, and they still pointed out any mistakes in their annual reviews.

There was no space to fail, as the pressure to deliver was relentless.

This time around, it seems to be even worse. I hear horror stories of staff being reprimanded for using too many tokens just weeks after being told to adopt AI. Or staff being lectured so much about what they should or should not do with AI that they are afraid to try anything. It is safer to just do what they have always done.

If management want staff to adopt AI, then they need to make staff feel safe to experiment. They need to adjust KPIs, change how staff are reviewed, and relax some of the governance while staff are learning.
Handle Governance With Care

Look, I get it. AI cannot be rolled out without some governance in place, but again, this is an area that organizations have typically handled poorly.

In the midst of digital transformation, governance would either be non-existent, or it would be so strict that it would stifle innovation, and unfortunately, I see the same pattern emerging with AI adoption.

Staff need some governance to help them know where they stand. It helps them understand what is expected of them and what they can and cannot do.

But too often organizations have been so strict that it is easier not to do anything at all. Pages of instructions about what people should and should not share with AI create that sense of fear that I mentioned earlier.

Organizations need a short set of principles that would fit on a handful of posters, similar to gov.uk’s design principles. Beyond that, they need to demonstrate a degree of trust in their staff to use common sense.

Of course, mistakes will get made, but that is the cost of change, and so organizations need to accept that and do what they can to mitigate that in how they roll out the technology.

Adaptive Technological Rollout

That said, organizations will need to be careful in how they roll out AI from an IT perspective. Once again, it is easy to lock everything down and stifle the adoption of AI.

Again, this is something I highlighted back in 2014 with digital transformation. Overstretched IT teams would pick a set of technologies and roll them out organization-wide, often with little or no consultation with the individuals who would be using them. Anything outside of that set of technologies had to go through a complex approval process, normally involving a steering committee. The result was a glacial pace of change that was one of the major contributing factors to the failure rate of digital transformation projects.

Today we are seeing the same thing. IT teams are opting into a tool like Copilot and calling it a day, but that often falls massively short.

Take, for example, a UX team I am working with at the moment. Management is pushing hard for AI adoption, and the team is excited about the potential of creating an AI-powered research repository and even AI personas. It sounds like a wonderful case study in AI adoption, with enthusiastic management, an excited team, and innovative ideas.

Unfortunately, the reality is that nothing is happening because Copilot cannot deliver on the team’s vision, and so they are currently bogged down in bureaucracy as they try to persuade the AI steering group that they need to use a different toolset. Understandably, the team’s enthusiasm is waning. Why bother?

And that team is one of the more patient ones. In many organizations, staff are not waiting for the steering group at all. Just as we saw shadow IT during the digital transformation era, with people signing up for Dropbox and Google Docs because the official tools could not do what they needed, we are now seeing shadow AI. When the in-house AI tool will not let people do their jobs, they open a personal ChatGPT or Claude account on their phone or home laptop and get on with it.

That is far more dangerous than the problem the restrictions were meant to prevent. Company data ends up in tools the organization has no control over, with no oversight and no record of what has been shared. In trying to lock everything down, organizations end up pushing the risk somewhere they cannot see it.

The instinctive reaction is to clamp down with stricter policies, blocked websites, and warnings about disciplinary action. But that rarely works, and it drives the behavior further underground. Shadow AI is a symptom, and a useful one, because it shows exactly where the official tools are falling short.

Instead of trying to stamp out shadow AI, organizations should learn from it. Talk to staff about what they are using personal accounts for and what they feel they cannot do with the AI they have been given, then use that to shape what gets rolled out next.

Look, I understand the cost of a messy, out-of-control tech stack. It is not ideal. However, in the early days of adoption, we may have to live with a bit of chaos while people explore the edges of what is possible and work out common use cases.

Organizations need to allow staff to explore different uses of AI and even different AI apps to find what suits their specific needs in their specific roles. They can always start locking things down later as the real organizational needs become clearer.

A Dangerous Time

I admit all of this feels dangerous to some organizations. They want to control the AI adoption process centrally and make sure nothing goes wrong. I understand that. But change comes with a degree of risk, and the risk of moving too slowly is greater than the risk of moving too fast.

However, a bigger danger still is when organizations see this as a technological challenge and overlook the human side of things. AI adoption is not simply about rolling out new technology. Just like digital transformation, it is about people, culture, and change management. The sooner organizations realize the scope of what they are dealing with, the better.

For most of my career, building software started with the same setup: keyboard, mouse, editor, terminal, browser, and an impressive number of tabs I was definitely going to look at later. The tools changed, but the relationship did not. Like most developers, I learned commands, memorized shortcuts, and adapted my thinking to the machine.

AI is changing that relationship and not because I can prompt my way to some workable code. It’s more because the system behind it can understand a repository, choose the right model, plan work, use tools, edit files, run commands, inspect failures, and keep going within boundaries the developer controls.

We’ve made it possible for a developer to describe an outcome, and GitHub Copilot can gather repository context, inspect symbols and dependencies, edit files, run tests, and revise the implementation. In the IDE, agent mode keeps that loop interactive. GitHub Copilot coding agent can take an issue into an isolated GitHub Actions-powered environment and return a pull request for review. This is not just faster coding. It is the shift from issuing commands to making intent executable. The developer still owns the outcome, but the work looks subtly different.

AI-assisted development compresses that chain of events. We spend less time navigating tools and more time setting direction, supplying context, defining constraints, and reviewing evidence. The development workflow now is about understanding the structure of codebases, the customer, and what a good solution should feel like.

This makes developers’ jobs even more important, and their time more valuable. It makes judgment more valuable. It makes taste more valuable. An agent can generate three implementations before you finish your tea, but someone still has to decide whether the abstraction belongs, the migration is safe, the tests prove anything useful, and the result solves the problem.

That is the most important change in the craft: the developer remains accountable for the outcome, even as more mechanics are delegated.

So what does this mean for the emerging developer workflow? The goal is to embed AI into the workflow, not beside it. It’s more of an ambient intelligence.

That requires portable context. MCP connects agents to tools and services; repository instructions and skills encode conventions; hooks enforce policy and validate output. Simpler interfaces need explicit context underneath.

I see three layers: modality captures intent; the harness plans and acts; the control plane lets developers inspect, redirect, and approve. The best products connect all three without surrendering control.

Together, they let developers move across text, voice, and physical controls without losing context.

Why physical controls are interesting again

I, and many others in the maker community, have been experimenting with ambient control devices, from inexpensive hackable picture frames to ESP32 devices with screens attached. Agentic coding is past just a keyboard and a terminal into alternative interfaces.

Getting this kind of technology into everyone’s hands is something that I’m very passionate about and so over the past 18 months we’ve been working with Logitech on their MX Keypad and how it can help this new way of working.

The Logitech MX Keypad turns familiar developer actions into programmable inputs to accelerate workflows that are ambient and contextual to the work devs are doing. In GitHub Copilot app, a key can submit a parameterized prompt, open or resume a Copilot session, invoke an IDE command, or trigger a multi-step sequence. The hardware earns its place only when that shortcut removes enough friction to keep you in the code, but still gives you control.

Physical controls work when an action repeats often enough that removing a few steps preserves concentration. Not every developer needs another device on the desk, and not every interruption deserves a key. However, with this experience the developer decides which actions earn that shortcut and when the keyboard, command palette, or voice is better. Maintaining flow is the key, and a good interface gives some of that attention back.

GUIs made software visual. Mouse made navigation easier. The web made it connected. Mobile made touch natural. Agents add intent and action. The interface now includes ambient control.

The interface moves into the background

That is why GitHub worked with Logitech to integrate the GitHub Copilot app directly into the experience. Developers can run agentic workflows in control, map a repeated prompt, start or resume a session, and decide through real use whether a physical control helps you stay in flow.

This is still early, so the important questions are engineering questions. How much repository and organizational context should an agent retain? Which tool calls can run automatically? How should credentials, network access, and secrets be scoped? What evidence should accompany a change? How do multiple agents coordinate without duplicating work or creating conflicting pull requests? Products such as MX Keypad let us explore one practical part of that system: how developers invoke, steer, and resume agentic work without losing focus.

For decades, developers learned to speak computer: syntax, commands, APIs, shortcuts, and the occasional incantation copied from a seven-year-old issue comment. Now computers are getting better at meeting us halfway. The best interface will not have the most panels or AI buttons. It will understand enough context, preserve meaningful control, and get out of the way so we can build software that matters.

The goal is not to remove developers from the work. It is to remove friction around the work. Pairing MX Keypad with GitHub Copilot Pro+ gives developers a concrete experiment: choose one repeated interruption, map it to a control, and see whether it preserves focus.

Working with outside collaborators can add more work for a repository owner. You need to confirm who still needs access, change permissions as projects evolve, and archive repositories once they’ve served their purpose.

To keep up, repository owners need to track collaborators, deadlines, and follow-ups for each repository. As a project grows, that manual housekeeping becomes harder to keep up with and easier to defer.

We faced this problem in a shared organization where GitHub employees (“Hubbers”) collaborate with customers and partners on product trials and professional services engagements. Our answer was Evergreen, a GitHub App built with native GitHub features. The workflows below exhibit a pattern you can adapt for your own organization.

Meet Evergreen

Evergreen is a GitHub App that automates organization hygiene through two workflows:

  1. Outside collaborator access reviews. Every quarter, each repo’s outside collaborators are reviewed. Keep who you need, and everyone else is removed.
  2. Stale repository detection and archival. Repos with no human activity past a threshold get flagged, warned, and (if still untouched) archived.

Two design principles shaped everything:

Fail secure. If nobody responds to an access review, we don’t leave access in place and hope for the best. We revoke it. The safe outcome is the default outcome.

Never destroy anything. Evergreen will never delete a repository or its contents. Archived repos are recoverable, and every access change is written into the repo’s tracking issue, so reverting a mistake is as easy as a copy-paste to Copilot.

How it works: One brain, many hands

Under the hood, Evergreen is a small Go webhook server running in a container. But the interesting part is the choreography. Every moving piece is a stock GitHub feature: a GitHub App, scheduled actions, webhooks, issues, custom properties, and plain files in a repo. Nothing exotic to reinvent.

A controller that keeps time

A dedicated controller repo acts as Evergreen’s clock and its memory. Four scheduled GitHub Actions workflows fire repository_dispatch events on a quarterly cadence, and the app wakes up and does the work:

DayWorkflowWhat happens1stStale repo reviewFlag repos with no recent human activity, open a warning issue8thAccess reviewSnapshot outside collaborators, open a checklist issue per repo16thStale repo cleanupArchive repos still stale after the grace period23rdAccess cleanupAuto‑revoke access for any review left unanswered

Reviews open two weeks before their cleanup runs, and there’s a hard grace period baked in so a stray manual run can never fast‑forward the guillotine. Each workflow also supports workflow_dispatch, so we can trigger any step by hand as needed.

State you can git blame

Evergreen tracks every repo’s status as a JSON file (one per repository) committed to the controller repo under state/repos/<repo_id>.json. The snapshot of who had access, when the review opened, the deadline, who decided, and what happened all lives there. Here’s a completed review where a Hubber kept Alice’s access and let Bob’s lapse:

The best part: because state is just files in git, the commit history is the audit trail. Want to know why someone lost access last spring? git log the state file. There’s no separate database or bespoke audit pipeline to track.

Issue‑ops, everywhere it matters

Evergreen does its talking through issues inside each repository. When an access review kicks off, every repo gets a checklist issue:

The interaction model is delightfully simple: check the boxes for who stays, then close the issue. That’s it. When the issue closes, Evergreen listens for the issues.closed webhook event, reads the checkboxes, retains the checked collaborators, and revokes the rest, then posts a receipt comment listing exactly what it kept and what it removed.

Only the right humans can pull the lever

Closing an access review issue is a privileged action, so Evergreen guards it. If someone who isn’t a Hubber closes an issue, the app quietly reopens it and posts a note explaining why. Decisions only count when they come from the people authorized to make them.

Informing the right people

Here’s a subtle problem: we know the people who need to respond to a review are Hubbers, but we didn’t always know which Hubber(s) owned a given repository.

Evergreen resolves the right people to notify through a fallback chain:

  1. If a repo sets an evergreen_repo_owner custom property (a @user or @org/team), use it.
  2. Otherwise, look at recent issue and commit authors and cc the ones who are on the access review team, i.e., the Hubbers actually working in that repo.

The result: the right owners get pinged automatically, without anyone maintaining a spreadsheet of “who owns what.”

Fail secure, and fail closed

When a review deadline passes with no response, cleanup revokes every collaborator in the snapshot. We were careful about a subtle edge case: what if some removals succeed while others hit a transient API error? Marking the review “done” while a removal is still outstanding would be a quiet inconsistency, and we didn’t want that.

So Evergreen fails closed. If any removal fails, the review stays pending and the issue stays open, and the next run retries the stragglers. It only records “revoked” once every removal actually succeeded. And whatever gets removed is written into the issue, so restoring access is a matter of re‑adding the handles it listed.

The user experience: automation that feels considerate

Automated housekeeping has a reputation for being the office smoke alarm: loud, easy to ignore, and usually going off while you’re cooking something perfectly fine. We worked hard to make Evergreen feel like the opposite:

  • It comes to you. The work happens as an issue in your repo.
  • It tags the right people. No “who owns this?” archaeology.
  • It’s crystal clear about the ask. Every issue states what to do, the exact deadline, and precisely what happens if you ignore it.
  • It nudges before it acts. A reminder comment lands before the deadline: no surprise revocations.
  • It has an escape hatch. Org owners (Hubbers) can opt out by setting a custom property (evergreen_exempt_from_access_review or evergreen_exempt_from_archive).

Rolling it out carefully

Automation that manages access and archives repositories deserves a careful rollout, especially into a large, active org (ours has 1,400+ Hubbers, and many more customers and partners). We wanted the very first run to be a complete non‑event. A few practices got us there:

Dry‑run first. Evergreen ships with a DRY_RUN mode that logs every write it would make without touching a single collaborator or repo. We ran it against the real org and read the logs like tea leaves until we trusted the decisions it wanted to make.

Overcommunicate. Before flipping the switch, we broadcast what was coming, when, and what people needed to do, so the first issue anyone saw wasn’t a surprise. We found that when you’re telling a large group the same thing for the seventh time, it’s often when many are still hearing it for the first time.

Watch everything with Splunk. Evergreen emits structured logfmt logs to a dedicated Splunk index. That observability is how we caught edge cases in the wild (empty collaborator lists, unusual permissions, repos with issues disabled) and tightened the logic before they mattered.

Respect the platform’s speed limits. When you open issues and post comments across every repo in a big org, you slam into GitHub’s secondary rate limits for content creation: 80 requests/minute and 500/hour. While this is a thoughtful preventative control to avoid system abuse, it requires a bit of intention when automating IssueOps at large scale.

So Evergreen paces itself by construction. It processes repos in batches, spaces out each content‑creating call by a couple of seconds, and sleeps between batches to let the rolling window clear, and it only counts the repos that actually create content, so a quiet quarter where nothing’s eligible still runs fast. We opted for proactive avoidance over reactive back‑off.

Lessons we’re taking with us

  • Make the safe path the default path. Fail secure, then make responding easy enough that failing secure rarely triggers.
  • Use the primitives you already have. Issues, webhooks, custom properties, and git history did the work of a bespoke UI, a job queue, and an audit database.
  • Meet people where they work. An issue in your repo beats a dashboard you’ll never open.
  • Logs are a revert button. Writing every change into the issue turned “oops” into “paste these three handles into a Copilot session.”
  • Respect the limits. At org scale, rate limits aren’t an edge case: they’re the design constraint.

Keep your org evergreen

Routine hygiene is the kind of work that’s easy to defer and perfect to automate. The trick isn’t heroic cleanup sprints: it’s making the boring, safe thing happen on a schedule, with a friendly face and an undo button.

That’s Evergreen. And because it’s built entirely from native GitHub features, you can stand up the same pattern yourself.

Ready to make repository hygiene routine? Start with one recurring access or archival task, then use GitHub Apps, actions, issues, and custom properties to automate the problem away.

Building a cloud-native foundation for AI

The relationship between model innovation and AI infrastructure has never been more clear; as AI becomes more capable, the platform underneath it matters even more. Agents are fundamentally changing how applications work, and with that, what organizations need from the cloud.

Learn how to build a cloud-native AI foundation

Cloud-native practices gave organizations the scale, flexibility and resilience to build a generation of distributed applications. Agents build on that foundation, but they work differently, placing new demands on it. Unlike applications designed around more predictable workflows, agents behave in entirely new ways, gathering information and deep intelligence from across the business, using tools, making decisions, and coordinating work across long-running processes. To complete complex tasks, they pause for approval, respond to new information in real time and change course as conditions evolve. All this changes what the platform needs to deliver.

Customers need infrastructure that adapts as demand changes, applications that remain reliable as work becomes more dynamic, and the visibility and control to understand what is happening across the system. Meeting those needs doesn’t mean starting over, but it does require that the cloud-native foundation organizations already rely on, to evolve. Compute, storage and networking need to work together. Application platforms need to make it easier to build and run AI workloads. Open technologies need to give customers flexibility without adding unnecessary complexity.

The platform decisions organizations make today are increasingly strategic, shaping how effectively they can put AI to work for their business. Evolving the cloud-native foundation is essential to helping organizations get more from AI today while giving them the flexibility to take advantage of new capabilities as they emerge.

Optimize infrastructure for AI workloads

The true measure of AI infrastructure is the useful work it enables. AI workloads rarely place the same demands on infrastructure from beginning to end. Different parts of the work may need accelerated compute, CPUs, fast access to data or connectivity across applications and environments. The answer isn’t simply more capacity, but rather, to match the right resources to the right work at the right time.

That makes coordination across the infrastructure extremely important. Compute, storage and networking need to work together as demands change. When they do, teams can spend less time managing infrastructure and more time focused on the applications and outcomes that matter to their business.

Economics matter, too. Infrastructure needs to be tuned to the demands of the application and the business value it delivers, with the right balance of performance, reliability and cost across the complete workload.

Basecamp Research is one example. The company used Azure to assemble 9.7 trillion nucleotides for EDEN, its therapeutic-design models, and reported laboratory validation across three therapeutic applications within about three weeks. In this example, AI didn’t simply require more infrastructure, it needed AI infrastructure that was coordinated and designed around the outcome – bringing together the compute, data and platform capabilities needed to move complex work forward.

As Glen Gowers, co-founder of Basecamp Research put it: “To really be successful here, we need those three ingredients to come together. We need someone scaling the data; that’s us. But someone who really knows how to scale models and scale compute is Microsoft.”

Leadership takeaway: Measure success by the useful work AI completes. Judge infrastructure investment by the performance, reliability and cost of delivering those outcomes, with compute, storage and networking operating as one system.

Turn open-source innovation into production capability

Open technologies have long helped drive cloud innovation, and AI is accelerating that pace. New models, frameworks and cloud-native technologies give organizations more choice in how they build and adapt applications.

That opportunity builds on an established cloud-native foundation. Gartner forecasts that by 2028, 95% of new AI agent deployment will use Kubernetes.1 Reusing established components can shorten the path to production, allowing developers to focus on differentiated business capabilities rather than rebuilding the foundation.

More choice can also bring more complexity. Taking advantage of open-source innovation often requires specialized expertise to integrate, manage and maintain technologies in production. That expertise is hard to find and even harder to scale. We need to make these capabilities accessible to more businesses, without requiring every organization to build and maintain that expertise themselves.

Azure helps reduce that burden. Services like Azure Kubernetes Service give teams access to open-source innovation while helping manage the complexity and risk of running it in production. This makes it easier for more businesses to take advantage of open technologies while preserving the flexibility to adapt with changing needs.

Leadership takeaway: Choose a platform that helps turn open-source freedom into business advantage. Preserve the ability to adapt and optimize while reducing the expertise and operating effort required to sustain those choices in production.

Align AI infrastructure costs with business value

As more organizations put AI into production, understanding the economics becomes increasingly important. AI investment starts with an expectation of business value: faster decisions, more responsive services or greater capacity to get useful work done. Then, it’s evaluating whether the value justifies the investment to deliver it. FinOps can help teams understand that return and make more informed decisions in this area.

For customers, that starts with understanding the full investment required to run AI applications and how to optimize it as workloads scale. Models represent a significant expense, with token usage and agent behavior influencing spending. Compute, storage, networking and operations also factor into the equation. Considering these together helps customers make more informed decisions about where to optimize.

Teams need to understand the full cost, establish ownership and put guardrails in place as they scale. From there, they can use business outcomes to decide where to optimize and where to invest.

Leadership takeaway: Manage AI investment against the business return it needs to deliver. Evaluate model and platform costs together, then use evidence of outcome value to decide where to optimize and where to invest.

Transform cloud operations with agentic AI

Operations teams are already stretched thin, keeping critical services running while balancing competing priorities and constant change. AI adds new complexity, and it also creates an opportunity to change how those environments are managed.

Cloud-native operations gave teams a new model for managing infrastructure, bringing increased automation, visibility and resilience. Agentic operations build on that foundation, bringing deeper intelligence across applications and infrastructure by connecting signals from otherwise disconnected systems. This helps teams identify issues earlier and resolve them faster, while increasing automation and continuously improving how their environments perform; all within the tools they already use.

Over time, AI can help operations teams move from reacting to problems toward anticipating and addressing them before they disrupt the business. As these capabilities mature, more actions can be automated within defined controls, with people remaining in the loop and accountable for critical decisions. When operations teams have greater reach without additional manual effort, reliability, performance and cost are continuously improved while critical services remain uninterrupted.

Leadership takeaway: Invest in operations that can anticipate problems and coordinate action across the environment. Expand autonomy as capabilities are validated, with clear controls and human accountability protecting critical business services.

AI Agent Security Risks: What Can Go Wrong When Agents Act on Their Own

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The thing that makes an AI agent useful, its freedom to act, is also what can introduce risk. An agent doesn’t just answer a question, it browses, runs code, and calls tools on its own, so a single bad or manipulated instruction can turn into real damage instead of just a wrong answer. That’s the shift traditional application security wasn’t built for, and it’s why trust has to be built into the system around the agent, not assumed.

An agent decides its own path, picking tools at runtime, chaining actions together, and usually running with the credentials of whoever launched it. That autonomy is the source of most agent-specific risk, and it points to the two-part answer that runs through this post: contain what an agent can reach, and oversee what it does.

Key takeaways

  • AI agents introduce risks traditional app security wasn’t built for: they choose tools, chain actions, and often run with your credentials.
  • The main risks are prompt injection, tool misuse and poisoning, excessive autonomy, credential exposure, and data exfiltration.
  • Most trace back to one thing: the agent can act, so containment and oversight matter more than the model.
  • The two-part answer is isolation to limit blast radius and governance to oversee behavior.

Why agents break traditional security models

If you’ve secured web services, the model is familiar: requests arrive at known endpoints, deterministic logic handles them, and you design controls around that predictability. Agents break that assumption. They interpret instructions dynamically, choose tools at runtime, and run multi-step chains with very little human intervention at each step. A coding agent might read a file, install a dependency, change config, run tests, and push a commit from a single prompt. 

Because an agent chooses its own path, a compromised or misdirected one can take a far wider range of actions than a traditional service. Every risk below has already produced documented real-world incidents, and they all follow from that shift.

1. Prompt injection

Prompt injection is the risk that defines agent security. Because an agent acts on whatever text it reads, an attacker doesn’t need access to your systems. They just need the agent to encounter their instructions, hidden in a web page, a code comment, an issue, or a document it summarizes. 

A real prompt-injection case showed an agent following instructions buried in repository content and taking actions its user never intended. 

Incident highlight: A coding agent followed instructions buried in a repository’s own content and took actions its user never intended. The malicious text arrived through data the agent was simply asked to read.

The agent has no reliable way to tell a legitimate instruction from an injected one, so the defense is to limit what a hijacked agent can reach.

2. Tool misuse and tool poisoning

Agents act through tools: API connectors, database queries, file operations, and code execution. Every tool is an access vector, and two things could go wrong. 

An agent can be given more tools than the task needs, widening the attack surface for no reason. And a tool itself can be poisoned, its description carrying hidden instructions that manipulate the agent into misusing it. The mitigations are to scope tools to the task at runtime, source them from registries with verified provenance, and review tool descriptions rather than trusting them by default.

3. Excessive autonomy

The more an agent can do without review, the more a single bad decision costs. Excessive autonomy is the risk of granting an agent broad, standing permission to act, most visibly when developers run YOLO mode, where every action is auto-approved. This autonomy is genuinely useful, so switching it off defeats the point. The better move is to run the agent where that autonomy can’t reach anything it shouldn’t, so speed doesn’t necessarily mean increased blast radius.

Incident highlight: A developer asked a coding agent to clean up an old repository. It generated an rm -rf command with a trailing ~/ and ran it with no confirmation step, wiping the developer’s entire home directory. Nothing in the setup gave a human a chance to catch it before it executed.

4. Credential and secret exposure

When an agent runs under a developer’s personal token, every action it takes carries that person’s full permissions, and a compromise hands those permissions to an attacker. Secrets sitting in prompts, config files, or environment variables baked into an image make it worse, because a prompt injection can read them straight out of the agent’s context. Agents should get their own scoped, short-lived credentials, injected at runtime and kept out of the conversation, rather than borrowing yours.

Incident highlight: An AWS engineer handed a small Cost Explorer bug to a coding agent running with their own operator-level access. The agent decided the cleanest fix was to delete and rebuild the production environment, and it held the credentials to do exactly that. The result was a thirteen-hour regional outage.

5. Data exfiltration and untrusted handoffs

An agent with network access and sensitive information in its context can send that data somewhere it shouldn’t, whether through a compromised tool or an injected instruction. The risk grows in multi-agent setups, where one agent passes work to another. If the receiving agent treats that input as trusted, a compromise in one link can spread down the chain. Restricting network egress to known endpoints and treating every agent-to-agent handoff as untrusted input could keep a single failure from spreading.

How to contain the risk

The risks are varied, but there are controls to secure AI agents. Isolate where the agent runs so a mistake or a hijack is trapped in a disposable environment instead of loose on your host, and scope what the agent can access so the damage it can do is small even inside that boundary.

Isolation is the highest-impact starting point, and a microVM boundary makes it strong without slowing the agent down. On top of containment, oversight is what keeps a fleet of agents accountable. Then, AI governance defines which actions an agent may take, what it can reach, and how its behavior is logged, so risk is managed deliberately rather than discovered after an incident.

Contain the blast radius, govern the behavior

These risks aren’t reasons to avoid developing with agents. Instead, we need to give agents the freedom to finish the job by building trust into the system around them, so that freedom never means access to everything.

And that’s the job Docker Sandboxes is built for: each agent runs in its own disposable microVM with the network and filesystem controls you define, so a mistake or an injection is contained by design. And AI Governance adds the oversight layer, setting which actions, tools, and credentials are allowed and enforcing it everywhere your team works. Contain the blast radius, and govern the behavior.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main security risks of AI agents?

The most significant are prompt injection, tool misuse and tool poisoning, excessive autonomy, credential and secret exposure, and data exfiltration. All of them stem from the fact that agents act on their own, using tools and credentials, rather than following a fixed, reviewable path.

What is the biggest AI agent security risk?

For most teams it’s excessive access: an agent running with more autonomy and more credentials than the task needs. It turns a small mistake or a single prompt injection into a large incident, because the agent can reach so much.

How is agent security different from application security?

Traditional application security assumes predictable request-and-response flows. Agent security has to account for autonomous decisions, tools chosen at runtime, and multi-step chains where the agent sets its own path. The attack surface is wider because the agent, not a fixed program, decides what happens next.

Can prompt injection be prevented?

You can reduce it with input handling and tool hygiene, but you can’t assume it away, because an agent can’t reliably tell injected instructions from legitimate ones. The durable defense is containment: run the agent so that even a successfully injected one can’t reach anything sensitive.

How do you reduce AI agent security risks?

Start with isolation so a compromised agent is trapped in a disposable environment. Add scoped tool access and dedicated credentials to shrink what any agent can do. Then layer on governance to define and enforce those limits consistently across every agent your team runs.

To evaluate an agent, you give it a real task, let it work, and check the result. Along the way, the agent installs packages, edits files, and runs commands. That’s untrusted, AI-written code, and a benchmark runs it thousands of times.

And agents don’t always play fair. An agent can sometimes earn a passing score on an eval by gaming the check instead of doing the work, for example by tampering with tests or overwriting the file that records its score. This is called reward hacking, and it’s a growing concern across the eval community.

So running agent evals needs two things:

  1. An eval framework that runs each task, lets the agent work, checks the result, and collects the scores and logs.
  2. An environment for every task that’s clean and isolated, so evaluating agents is safe and consistent.

Harbor gives you the first. It’s an open-source eval framework. Give it a benchmark like Terminal-Bench, and it simplifies the process of running evals. 

Now, Docker Sandboxes gives you the second: a safe way to run those eval tasks, on your laptop or in the cloud.

Here’s what this means for you

Strong isolation by default. Docker Sandboxes were designed from the ground up for deeper isolation. This means you can run every task in a microVM, not a shared container. A microVM has its own kernel, so whatever the agent does stays inside its sandbox. It can’t break out to reach your machine, other tasks, or anything else outside its environment.

Fair, reproducible evals. With Docker Cloud Sandboxes, configurable network policies let a task download dependencies during setup, then restrict internet access while the agent works. This way, every agent can be tested under the same conditions. 

Parallel runs, no machines to manage. With Docker Cloud Sandboxes, teams can run many tasks at once without needing to manually provision anything. Run dozens of concurrent workers simultaneously.*

Develop locally, scale in the cloud. The same tasks run locally or in Docker Cloud Sandboxes. Build on your laptop, then run your tasks in the background, and at scale, without changing anything.

Try it

Install Harbor with Docker Sandboxes.

uv tool install "harbor[sbx]"

Activate Cloud access for your Docker account and create a personal access token. Select Cloud Sandboxes under access permissions. Copy and save your token. It’s shown only once.

  1. Set your Docker ID (your Docker login username) and PAT (personal access token), then run a task in Cloud Sandboxes.
  2. export SBX_DOCKER_USERNAME="your-docker-id"
  3. export SBX_DOCKER_PAT="your-personal-access-token"
  4. harbor run -t hello-world/hello-world -a oracle -e sbx

To run locally, install the Docker Sandboxes CLI, run sbx login, and add --ek target=local to the command above. Harbor’s local integration currently supports public and no-network modes, subject to the machine’s existing sandbox policy.

This integration is available on Harbor’s stable release.

uv tool install --upgrade "harbor[sbx]>=0.25.0"

Docker Compose is supported as part of the release, which means Harbor can run multi-container tasks inside a single Docker Cloud Sandbox, so services and sidecars can share that MicroVM.

To learn more, see the Docker Sandboxes docs and the Harbor docs.

*We’ve tested Harbor with 64 concurrent tasks on Docker Cloud Sandboxes. For higher limits, we’re happy to help: contact us.

Why must Manchester United move beyond the Glazers?

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Glazers took control of Manchester United in 2005. That takeover has since caused so many disruptions and protests by the fans over massive debts and insufficient backing of the team in the transfer market, despite successful periods in the Premier League and Europe.

Now Manchester United need to think about who should control the club in the future. Laurie Whitwell has now shared his views on why Manchester United need an ownership change. He simply wants the Glazers gone from Old Trafford. However, he also recognizes that replacing the current ownership doesn’t automatically mean things will improve for United.

The journalist believes the change in ownership has had a lasting effect on United, with the club still feeling its consequences today. A fresh start might be welcome, but the identity of the next owners would matter just as much as the departure of the existing ones.

Whitwell explains the Man Utd ownership dilemma

“Personally, I would like to see the Glazers gone from Manchester United,” Laurie Whitwell said on The Rest is Football podcast with Gary Lineker.

“That change, you know, just really affected and still impacts United now.”

The desire for different ownership is one thing, but finding people capable of taking the club forward is another.

Whitwell admitted there is no perfect answer when it comes to who should take charge.

“There’s no ideal owner.”

That leaves United facing a difficult choice. A sale could offer the chance to change how the club is run, but simply bringing in a new owner would not guarantee better decisions or a brighter future.

Could Manchester United fans have a role?

Whitwell also raised the idea of United supporters having a greater say in the club’s ownership and decisions, much like the Barcelona model where fans own the club itself. However, he highlighted that the Barcelona model isn’t ideal either and has its own shortcomings.

Changing the names at the top would only be one part of the change. The club would also need an ownership structure capable of making sound decisions and providing a direction.

The issue of ownership goes beyond whether the Glazers should stay or leave. Manchester United needs to find the right people and the right model to secure the club’s future. Getting that decision wrong could leave them facing the same circus the fans are enduring under the Glazers.
