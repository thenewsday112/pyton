A stunning microscope video of a child’s airway won Nikon’s contest before being stripped of first place due to the use of generative ai

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A video of beating cilia from the airway of a child with a genetic lung disorder won Nikon’s 2026 Small World in Motion competition. It no longer holds first place.

On October 9, Nikon announced that the winning video “did not comply with the competition rules regarding generative AI.” It reshuffled the rankings but did not name the entrant.

Nikon has not described what the breach involved or which part of the footage was affected.

The original winning entry

The entry was titled “Abnormal beating of airway cilia from a child with Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia (PCD).” Nikon’s September 16 release listed the technique as diffractive super-resolution microscopy at 100X.

The video was presented as showing abnormal beating in airway tissue taken from a patient. In healthy airways, cilia beat in coordinated waves to clear mucus and debris.

The entrant’s team shaped the illumination with multiple light waves and a digital micromirror device. The goal was to capture fast movement at high resolution while limiting the light that reached the living sample.

Many advanced techniques rely on fluorescent labels or intense illumination, which can alter or damage living cells.

That video no longer appears in the 2026 Small World in Motion winners gallery on the contest’s website.

How cilia clear the airways

Cilia are microscopic, finger-like projections that stick out from the surface of cells, according to the U.S. National Library of Medicine’s MedlinePlus service.

In primary ciliary dyskinesia, the cilia are defective. People with the condition often have problems removing fluid and particles from their airways.

Children usually have year-round nasal congestion, a chronic cough and frequent respiratory tract infections beginning in early childhood.

Ongoing infections can lead to bronchiectasis, a condition that damages the passages leading from the windpipe to the lungs. The disorder can also affect the position of internal organs and reduce fertility.

As many as one in 7,500 people worldwide have the disorder. Most cases follow an autosomal recessive pattern, meaning both copies of the affected gene carry a variant.

Nikon reverses its decision

Nikon re-evaluated the video and its supporting materials and consulted members of its judging panel before reaching its decision.

“Nikon has adjusted the competition rankings to reflect this decision,” the company wrote in its October statement.

The decision rests only on whether the entry met the contest’s eligibility rules. Nikon stressed that it is not a judgment of the entrant’s professional reputation, scientific contributions or intent.

“AI generated videos are not permitted and Sponsor holds the right to request the original video for verification,” the official rules for the next cycle state.

Entries for that cycle close on April 30, 2027.

The same rules also bar sound and graphics of any kind from submitted videos.

A new order of winners

Nguyen Nam Nhat of Chu Se, Vietnam, originally second, now holds first place. His video follows a tiny roundworm and a single-celled Dileptus, filmed with differential interference contrast at 40X.

Benedikt Pleyer of Kirchberg, Germany, moved from third to second with jellyfish larvae suspended in water droplets, recorded in darkfield at 25X.

Andrew Moore of Ashburn, Virginia, rose from fourth to third. His confocal recording at 63X shows synchronized cell division in neighboring cells.

Patrick Hickey of Edinburgh, U.K., moved from fifth to fourth. His confocal video at 40X shows mitochondria and chloroplasts in turtle vine leaf cells, Callisia repens.

Fifth place now goes to Igor Adameyko of Vienna, Austria, for a darkfield video of a tiny, colorful sea slug, Cyerce kikutarobabai.

Scientific images in the AI era

“Advances in imaging and artificial intelligence continue to evolve rapidly, creating new challenges for organizations across many fields,” Nikon wrote.

In Nikon’s separate photomicrography contest, the 2026 winner was Ole Bielfeldt of Cologne, Germany. His image shows ink droplets in midair as an inkjet printer deposits ink onto paper.

Bielfeldt built his own imaging rig around a 20X microscope objective. He said he rejected the idea of using AI to generate his images.

For future entries, Nikon plans to revisit its rules and evaluation procedures but has not detailed any new checks.

Experts struggle to spot fakes

In a 2025 survey described by the University of Hamburg, 250 scientists were each shown a real or an AI-generated microscopy image of nanomaterials.

Participants were unable to tell real images from most of the AI-generated fakes.

Wolfgang Parak, a researcher at the university’s Institute for Nanostructure and Solid State Physics, took part in the work.

“Generative artificial intelligence is now capable of creating customized fake derivatives of any image-based technique,” Parak said.

The paper warned that traditional ways of detecting misconduct are becoming obsolete. Among other measures, it recommended publishing raw data files alongside articles, since these are harder for generative AI to fake.

The information was obtained from Nikon Small World.
