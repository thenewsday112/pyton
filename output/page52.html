The 12 strangest science stories we can’t stop thinking about

These are not those moments – but we can’t stop thinking about them anyway.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Push my button</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Surgeon Stuart Meloy was performing a pain-relief operation on a woman’s spine when she started exclaiming emphatically. “You’re going to have to teach my husband to do that,” the woman said. This inspired Meloy to invent an implant to help women who are unable to achieve orgasms in the usual way. <em>New Scientist</em> <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/1860732-push-my-button/">broke the news in 2001,</a> sparking a frenzy of worldwide media excitement. But 13 years later, Meloy <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2000167-push-button-orgasm-isnt-worth-6-million-to-medicine/">was still struggling to raise funds</a> for a full clinical trial, and the “orgasmatron” never reached a climax.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Monkey pleasure</h2> <figure class="wp-block-image size-full"><img alt="" class="wp-image-2591712" height="900" src="https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/SEI_314834831.jpg" width="1350" /><figcaption class="wp-element-caption"><div class="wp-image__caption--icon"><svg height="15" viewBox="0 0 15 15" width="15" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"><use></use></svg></div><div class="wp-image__caption--text">A long-tailed macaque</div><div class="wp-image__caption--credit">Shutterstock/fiz_zero</div></figcaption></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">We aren’t the only primates capable of coming up with creative ways to pleasure ourselves. In a study of long-tailed macaques (<em>Macaca fascicularis</em>) in Bali, Indonesia, <a href="https://www.ulethbridge.ca/artsci/psychology/camilla-cenni-phd-evolution-behaviour">Camilla Cenni</a> at the University of Lethbridge in Alberta, Canada, and her colleagues filmed hundreds of examples of male and female <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2333363-monkeys-in-bali-use-stones-as-sex-toys-to-fill-their-leisure-time/">monkeys using stone tools as sex toys</a>, rubbing or tapping them on their genitals. According to <a href="https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/persons/cyril-grueter">Cyril Grueter</a> at the University of Western Australia, the Balinese monkeys get plenty of food handouts from humans, and this frees up time for sexual experimentation. But some monkeys in other places have unusual kinks, too: another study reported a wild macaque in Japan rubbing its genitals on the back of a <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2117589-wild-monkey-filmed-mounting-deer-and-trying-to-have-sex-with-it/">sika deer.</a></p> <div class="advert-wrapper advert__mid-article"> <div class="advert"> <div class="advert__inner"> <p class="advert__copy"> Advertisement </p> <div class="advert__slot" id="mid-article-slot"> </div> </div> </div> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Head transplants</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">In 2015, surgeon Sergio Canavero announced plans to <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2017679-first-human-head-transplant-could-happen-in-two-years-2/">carry out the first human head transplant,</a> intended to extend the lives of people with muscle and nerve degeneration or widespread cancer. He claimed his technique could overcome technical hurdles, including how to fuse the spinal cord and prevent the body’s immune system from rejecting the head. In the following two years, Canavero published <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2073923-head-transplant-carried-out-on-monkey-claims-maverick-surgeon/">animal experiments</a> supposedly backing up his methods, <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2106382-head-transplant-teams-new-animal-tests-fail-to-convince-critics/">but other surgeons remained highly sceptical</a>. A human head transplant never did happen, but Canavero has reportedly moved on to an even bolder ambition: <a href="https://www.popularmechanics.com/science/health/a70295783/human-head-transplant-doctor/">transplanting a brain into a non-sentient clone.</a></p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Rats driving</h2> <figure class="wp-block-embed is-type-video is-provider-youtube wp-block-embed-youtube"><div class="wp-block-embed__wrapper"> </div></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Have you ever wondered if a rat can drive a car? Kelly Lambert at the University of Richmond in Virginia has. <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2220721-scientists-have-trained-rats-to-drive-tiny-cars-to-collect-food/">She and her colleagues built a tiny car</a> with three copper bars that would steer it left, forward or right. Sure enough, 18 rats learned to drive towards food rewards. They even found it relaxing, as measurements of their stress hormones indicated. Lambert proposed that driving challenges could replace traditional maze tests when using rat models to study neuropsychiatric conditions.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">The sniff test</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Humans are more similar to rats than we like to think. In a psychology experiment, <a href="https://www.weizmann.ac.il/lsc/lab/prof-noam-sobel">Noam Sobel</a> and his colleagues at the Weizmann Institute of Science in Rehovot, Israel, surreptitiously filmed 153 volunteers. After shaking hands with someone of the same sex, <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2018102-after-handshakes-we-sniff-peoples-scent-on-our-hand/">people smelled their shaking hand</a> more than twice as much as they did before the handshake. Puzzlingly, after shaking hands with someone of the opposite sex, people were more likely to sniff their non-shaking hand. The team suspected that humans use chemical signals all the time without being aware of it.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Urine fountains</h2> <figure class="wp-block-embed is-type-video is-provider-youtube wp-block-embed-youtube"><div class="wp-block-embed__wrapper"> </div></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Communication through scent is, of course, widespread in mammals, but <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2466289-amazon-river-dolphins-may-send-messages-with-aerial-streams-of-urine/">some never-before-seen behaviour in dolphins</a> took biologists by surprise in 2025. They saw dolphins do this a total of 36 times, and most of the time another male was nearby and would pursue the stream of urine. The purpose of this behaviour hasn’t yet been established, but hormones in urine might signal a male’s social position or physical condition, said Araújo-Wang.</p> <div class="read-more__standard wp-block-newscientist-read-more"> <a class="read-more__standard--wrapper no-animation" href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2457910-orcas-have-begun-wearing-salmon-hats-again-and-we-may-soon-know-why/"> <strong>Read more</strong> <p>Orcas have begun wearing salmon hats again – and we may soon know why</p> </a> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Giving the finger</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">As for human modes of communication, many of us struggle to put our smartphones down – so the last thing we’d want is for them to crawl across the table towards us. Nevertheless, Marc Teyssier at the University of Paris-Saclay in France and his colleagues <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2181564-smartphone-with-a-finger-crawls-across-the-table-to-stroke-your-wrist/">gave phones this ability in 2018</a>, with a robotic appendage dubbed the MobiLimb. It plugs into a device’s USB port and, for some reason, looks eerily like a human finger, giving it a spooky quality reminiscent of Thing from <em>The Addams Family</em>. The MobiLimb can stroke your wrist, wave, wag like a tail or, most disconcertingly, drag the phone around. Thankfully, the idea hasn’t taken off.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Horror frog </h2> <figure class="wp-block-image size-full"><img alt="" class="wp-image-2591711" height="900" src="https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/SEI_314835022.jpg" width="1350" /><figcaption class="wp-element-caption"><div class="wp-image__caption--icon"><svg height="15" viewBox="0 0 15 15" width="15" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"><use></use></svg></div><div class="wp-image__caption--text">A taxidermied male hairy frog</div><div about the nightmare-inducing hairy frog <em>Trichobatrachus robustus. </em>In 2008, researchers reported that these frogs <a team argued</a> that they were used in fights between males – and that the frog’s unusual hairs were a kind of armour to protect its vulnerable parts from sharp claws.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Corpse shrinking</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">In 1957, when <em>New Scientist</em> was 1 year old, we reported on a new method for <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/1891251-this-week-50-years-ago-49/">shrinking an adult human body to the size of a baby</a>. At an exhibition of inventors in Winnipeg, Canada, the unnamed inventor of this process claimed it would make disposing of dead bodies simpler, as they could be slid into underground tubes and take up less space. The method involved applying high pressure to the corpse and squeezing out fluids so its flesh could be compressed into a mould. “All this is, of course, highly ingenious but it is uncertain why the inventor thinks that his newly proposed form of burial is in any way better than the current and very popular method of cremation,” the writer mused.</p> <div class="read-more__standard wp-block-newscientist-read-more"> <a class="read-more__standard--wrapper no-animation" href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2449105-the-brain-has-its-own-microbiome-heres-what-it-means-for-your-health/"> <strong>Read more</strong> <p>The brain has its own microbiome. Here’s what it means for your health</p> </a> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Transient anus</h2> <figure class="wp-block-image size-full"><img alt="The warty comb jelly" class="wp-image-2591710" height="900" src="https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/SEI_314834910.jpg" width="1350" /><figcaption class="wp-element-caption"><div class="wp-image__caption--icon"><svg height="15" viewBox="0 0 15 15" width="15" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"><use></use></svg></div><div class="wp-image__caption--text">The warty comb jelly has an anus that comes and goes</div><div class="wp-image__caption--credit">Andrey Nekrasov/imageBROKER.com​/Alamy</div></figcaption></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">We may take it for granted, but the anus, which allows food to pass through the body, was an evolutionary breakthrough, both figuratively and literally. The earliest animals, like jellyfish today, had only one gut opening, where food went in and waste came out. In 2019, scientists discovered that the warty comb jelly (<em>Mnemiopsis leidyi</em>, pictured top) forms an anal opening when it needs to defecate, but it vanishes straight afterwards. This, they suggested, could represent <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2195656-animal-with-an-anus-that-comes-and-goes-could-reveal-how-ours-evolved/">how the anus evolved in the first place.</a></p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Fart science</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Flatus – gas expelled from the anus – has been a topic of endless fascination for many readers (and journalists). <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/1862836-raising-a-stink-2/">In 2001, we featured</a> the work of Michael Levitt at the Veterans Administration Medical Center in Minneapolis, a leading authority on the subject. He developed a “Toot Trapper” that reduces odours by trapping certain gases using activated charcoal. We also reported on a device for <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/1943693-pong-ology-sniffing-out-a-cure-for-iffy-whiffs/">monitoring gas emissions from dogs</a>, used to test dog snacks that were supposed to reduce flatulence. In 2016, researchers in Australia worked out <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2109040-these-are-the-foods-you-should-eat-if-you-want-less-smelly-farts/">which foods cause the smelliest farts</a> by mixing faeces with different foods. They recommended eating less protein and more slow-release carbohydrates to minimise hydrogen sulphide production. In 2022, <a href="https://www.mayo.edu/research/faculty/kannan-nagarajan-ph-d-m-s/bio-20233074">Nagarajan Kannan</a> at the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, and his colleagues worked out that <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2346620-we-now-know-why-some-poos-float-and-others-sink/">some poos float because of gas from gut microbes</a>, confirming a hypothesis first put forward by Levitt 50 years earlier.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Brew your own</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Even stranger things go on inside some people’s intestines, as we found out in 2019. A 46-year-old man started experiencing unexplained mental fogginess, dizziness and memory loss, and had to give up his job. Later, doctors found high levels of alcohol in his blood when he hadn’t been drinking. Eventually, <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2220432-mans-body-brews-its-own-beer-after-yeast-take-over-his-gut-microbiome/">they realised his gut contained high levels of <em>Saccharomyces cerevisiae</em></a> – brewer’s yeast – which got him drunk any time he ate a lot of carbohydrates. This rare condition, known as auto brewery syndrome, had been triggered when the man took antibiotics for a thumb injury, which disrupted his gut microbiome. This year, a study reported that another man with this condition fully recovered after getting a <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2510789-man-whose-gut-made-its-own-alcohol-gets-relief-from-faecal-transplant/">transplant of faeces from a healthy donor.</a> Both articles were written by <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/author/alice-klein/">Alice Klein</a>, who we also have to thank for the monkey sex toys, car-driving rats, smelly farts and floating poos. Almost as long as this blog has been going (coming up on 25 years now!), I&#8217;ve made the case for there to be a sliding scale of punishment for acts that meet the definition of violent conduct. Some things are just more violent than others, but as long as this blanket punishment of three games regardless of the seriousness of the incident is in place, the more those in charge do the game and the players a disservice.</p> <p>Still, it is what it is now, and Arsenal will have to live without Dowman for the games against Liverpool, Hull City, and the away trip to Newcastle on the far side of the upcoming November Interlull. There are some whispers that Christos Tzolis could be back in contention for Sunday, which would be a very welcome boost, but the forward line looks a bit light for a game of this significance.</p> <p>Tuesday&#8217;s game against Fleetwood will live in the memory because of the giant killing aspect, but for two young Academy players, it&#8217;s a night that will hopefully be formative. For Dowman, whether you think what he did was worthy of a red card or not, there&#8217;s a lesson to learn about how you react. To be quite honest, I think there&#8217;s something to like about the way he was prepared to stick up for himself against a grown man who wanted to &#8216;leave a bit on him&#8217;, as they say. The two have plenty of time on their hands right now, and I&#8217;m sure it will be part of his footballing education.</p> <p>The other player is Marli Salmon who probably should have done better on their goal, but for me is less culpable overall than some of the senior players who didn&#8217;t perform at the right level. Arsene Wenger once said:</p> <blockquote><p>You pay for the education of young players with points. If I play a 20-year old centre back, I know he’ll cost me points during the season and I have to stand up for that. A less talented 28 year old would cost me less points. However, by 23/24 you have a player.</p></blockquote> <p>Marli Salmon is 17. He didn&#8217;t cost us any points. There&#8217;s a long way still go in his development, but I&#8217;m sure with the coaching staff they&#8217;ll go over what happened against Fleetwood and he will learn from it. A mistake at the top end of the pitch, like Dowman scuffing a shot wide from a position he probably ought to score from, doesn&#8217;t feel quite as costly as a forward running off a young defender to score the opening goal of the game.
