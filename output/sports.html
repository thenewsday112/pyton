Cannavaro: ‘Spalletti and Allegri are similar, Kolo Muani and Hojlund less killer than strikers of my era’

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Fabio Cannavaro believes Luciano Spalletti and Massimiliano Allegri have plenty in common, and that Rasmus Højlund and Randal Kolo Muani are not the ruthless strikers of his generation.

With quotes via Calciomercato.com, the former defender, who played for both clubs, previewed Sunday’s clash between Juventus and Napoli in an interview with Betsson.Sport. It kicks off at 20:45 CET [19:45 GMT] at the Allianz Stadium.

“Spalletti and Allegri are two great coaches who have achieved great results in recent years,” Cannavaro said. “They know Italian football very well and everything that surrounds it: how to manage players, journalists and fans. Tactically, they’re not tied to one system and they know how to adapt to their players.”

Allegri will not be on the touchline. He is serving the second game of a two-match ban, so assistant Marco Landucci takes charge again.

The sides go into the game level on 14 points, but in very different moods. Napoli won 4-1 at Monza on Wednesday, while Juventus were beaten 2-0 at Genoa. Spalletti apologised to the fans after that defeat.

“Napoli have certainly paid for a few too many injuries at the start of the season, while Juve, who have changed more than last year, can’t find consistency,” Cannavaro said. “Both Gasperini and Chivu have worked with their groups, so this year we’re seeing the results.”

NAPLES, ITALY – JANUARY 13: Massimiliano Allegri Juventus coach greets Luciano Spalletti SSC Napoli coach before the Serie A match between SSC Napoli_Juventus at Stadio Diego Armando Maradona on January 13, 2023 in Naples, Italy. (Photo by Francesco Pecoraro/Getty Images)

Cannavaro on Bremer, Buongiorno and the strikers

Cannavaro won the Ballon d’Or in 2006 as a Juventus player, and he was full of praise for both sides’ centre-backs.

“I really like Buongiorno because he’s a real defender. He senses danger, he knows when to step up and when to be hard,” he said. “The same goes for Bremer, they’re two extraordinary players. Both always have a lot of injuries, but they’re fundamental for Juve and Napoli.”

Alessandro Buongiorno is out until the new year after knee surgery, while Gleison Bremer is a doubt for Sunday.

REGGIO NELL’EMILIA, ITALY – SEPTEMBER 13: Gleison Bremer of Juventus looks on prior to the Serie A match between US Sassuolo and Juventus at Mapei Stadium – Citta’ del Tricolore on September 13, 2026 in Reggio nell’Emilia, Italy. (Photo by Emmanuele Ciancaglini/Getty Images)

Cannavaro was less convinced by the strikers. “Højlund and Kolo Muani are great workers. Football today is much faster, more intense and more athletic,” he said. “Strikers from my generation were a bit more ‘killer’ in front of goal. Nowadays strikers play more for the team and work a lot off the ball.”

The Neapolitan, who grew up in Fuorigrotta in the shadow of the San Paolo, also looked back on his Napoli debut. “I think it was the most emotional day of my life,” he said. “Getting to play in Serie A for the team of your heart isn’t something everyone gets to do.”

Cardinale reiterates Amorim backing before derby but warns: “I will be crushed if we don’t win”

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By: Isak Möller

Gerry Cardinale has reiterated his complete backing for Ruben Amorim ahead of AC Milan’s derby against Inter, but also made it clear that he will be very disappointed if they don’t win. 

An important game is on the cards for Milan this evening, hoping to bounce back from their Bologna defeat with what would be a massive derby win. Success would bring them within four points of Inter, while a defeat would increase the gap to ten points.

Cardinale, the Milan owner, will be present at the stands in San Siro after also attending the games against Udinese and Bologna. Last night, he hosted a dinner for many Milan legends and explained his project for the club to them.

Cardinale’s post-dinner thoughts

After the dinner, Cardinale stopped to talk to the journalist Carlo Pellegatti outside the restaurant (see the video at the bottom). Among other things, he reiterated his backing for Amorim and also made it clear that he really wants to win.

What is your feeling about this evening?

“Look, it was a fantastic night. We had people from Milan getting together in support of AC Milan. And that’s all I could have hoped for. It’s a big day tomorrow, a big game, as you know…”

Are emotions ramping up ahead of the game?

“My job is not to increase the emotion. My job is to keep everything steady and focused, providing the leadership so that we know what we need to do. We need to win tomorrow [today, editor’s note], not just for the first 45 minutes but also the next 45 minutes.

“It’s football, it’s sport, and the great thing about sport is that you never know. But my job is to make sure that we have a steady hand to do the best that we can do and maximise our chance of winning.

“Tonight was a really amazing event. We had legends of AC Milan who know how to win, and that means so much to me, that they came back and bought into what we do. We are bringing this passion and love of winning back.

“Let’s see how we do tomorrow. It’s a long season, but it’s the derby and it’s our home derby, so I want the win more than anything. I will be crushed if we don’t win tomorrow.”

You gave Amorim heavy backing…

“Listen, we have to come together. If we want to win, it has to be everybody. The fans, us, the press, anybody who supports AC Milan. Let’s come together and all make this possible. Ruben is doing a great job, I made the right decision on Ruben.

“I watched him today at Milanello and he really cares about his training, his managing, his coaching, and his mentoring. I met with every single one of our players and they want to win. It’s only a matter of time.

“You either want to win or you don’t want to win. You can’t fake that. I want to win, the players want to win and Amorim wants to win. I’m telling you, that I got right. Now, football is up and down, but I promise you that they want to win.”

Kaka discusses Hall of Fame induction, tonight’s derby and the Milan project

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By: Isak Möller

AC Milan legend Ricky Kaka will be inducted into the Rossoneri’s Hall of Fame ahead of tonight’s derby, which he believes is anyone’s game. 

As confirmed in the last few days, after inducting the likes of Marco van Basten, Filippo Inzaghi, Andriy Shevchenko and more, it’s now time for Kaka. The Brazilian is one of the greatest players to have even worn the red and black shirt, scoring 104 goals and assisting another 81 in  307 games.

The ceremony will take place before tonight’s derby clash between AC Milan and Inter, which is a very important game for the Rossoneri. A loss would leave them 10 points behind the top, so hopefully Kaka’s presence and celebration can act as fuel for the team.

Speaking to Gazzetta dello Sport, Kaka shared his thoughts on the Hall of Fame, his memories of the derby and what will be needed from Milan tonight. He also commented on the Rossoneri’s project as a whole, stating that time is needed.

What kind of welcome do you expect?

“I’m getting ready. My family will be there. My wife, daughters, my brother, and many friends. I also wanted to thank the fans who voted. I can imagine the atmosphere. It’ll be exciting.”

Image: AC Milan

What do you remember about your first derby?

“A header from a cross by Rino Gattuso. It was also my first goal for AC Milan. Fantastic. The city experiences this match in a special way; among my memories, I’d logically put the Champions League derby in the quarterfinals. The comeback from 3-2 was also fantastic.”

You mentioned Gattuso, who as a coach, after changing many clubs, is doing well with Lazio…

“Being a coach is a tough job. Rino is doing really well now. He has a very special character, he’s someone who always wants to win, and I’m happy he’s able to express himself to the fullest. He’s a great friend, I always cheer for my friends and I’m very happy.”

Will you ever become a coach?

“Let’s say no, for now. The pressure of the coaching job isn’t for me. But we can talk about it again in a couple of years.”

Speaking of friends, what do you think of the Paolo Maldini and Leonardo affair with the national team?

“I’m amazed at how little time passed before their resignations. It’s difficult to judge from afar, but I think it would have been a good thing for Italian football to have such competent people at the helm. I hope Italy regains its place soon.

“There are so many things to manage, from youth development onwards. I often meet with former players who live in Italy and ask questions. Italy at the World Cup is a good thing for football as a whole.

“Plus, my connection to Italy is incredibly strong; I try to return at least once a year, and more if I can. In recent years, we’ve visited the art cities, Florence, Venice, but also the lakes, Capri, and the sea. And Sardinia is wonderful.”

Let’s get back to football. Were you surprised by Milan’s collapse last season?

“I think all the fans expected us to qualify for the Champions League. Was it a physical or mental decline? Maybe a bit of everything. When confidence starts to wane, everything changes. Now, however, we must aim to return to the best of Europe. The Champions League is special.”

Do you like this Milan?

“It’s under construction. For seven years, it was Leao’s Milan, a great player on whom a lot of trust was placed. Now it’s different. The team is a collective with new faces and two masters: Modric remains the brains, then there’s Rabiot. And Ramos is a goalscorer.

AC Milan players before Lecce clash

“It will take some time to put it together. The important thing is to always aim high, even in the Europa League.”

What kind of relationship did you have with the Inter fans?

“A relationship of great respect. Inter helped me grow a lot because I’ve always looked at competition as something that helps me grow, so I saw the matches against Inter or Juventus as important matches, and I had to prepare well because the team was very strong, the opponents were very strong.

“But with the Brazilians of my time, Maicon, Julio, and the others, there was friendship. And also with the Argentines: many others were and remain friends, like Materazzi.”

How do you see Inter on this evening, which will be special for you?

“They are still doing well after last season. They’re a team to beat, but we Milan fans can do it already in this derby. Chivu as a coach was a nice surprise; Inter didn’t play well at the start, but then they corrected their position. But I wouldn’t say the Scudetto is a one-man showdown like you might think in Italy.

“I see the game as open and the road to the title even more so. The season is long, but Milan must aim to be consistent. So far they’ve done better in the league than in Europe, there will be moments of decline in the other teams and we’ll have to take advantage of them.”

Ancelotti and Brazil. What do you think?

“Not great at the World Cup, but it takes a bit of time. The national team is different from a club where you’re there every day evaluating players. Carlo was only able to work with the group for a year.

“For the next World Cup, it will be five years of preparation, five years with these guys, learning the culture of the country, knowing a little about what it’s like to play in the World Cup with Brazil. So I have a lot of confidence.”

You won the Ballon d’Or in 2007 and no one had any reason to argue. This time they gave it to Harry Kane, and Lamine Yamal doesn’t seem to have taken it too well. Your opinion?

“The right choice. Harry Kane has done very well, he was a key player with Bayern Munich and won a lot. Mbappé didn’t win much with Real Madrid, Lamine had success with Barça but wasn’t as decisive as expected. Their time will come, but I think the choice based on their performance this season is acceptable.”

Let’s get back to the derby. Who are the key players?

“Modric and Rabiot are masters, they manage and set the pace. Pulisic enchants me with his intelligence in the game, Goncalo is a goalscorer. As for Inter, what can I say? They are a consistent, solid team and have many dangerous players.”

Do you have any favourite places in Milan?

“I like walking downtown, I like Brera, I love the hidden corners. Drinking coffee, talking to people.”

It’s that spooky time of year and so we’re taking a look at the 10 scariest Premier League managers to ever appear on the touchline.

This weekend’s Premier League football falls on Halloween so what better time to look back over the archives and find the 10 scariest men to ever manage in the league.

Let’s get into it.

10. Graeme Souness

They say players are a reflection of their manager and so perhaps it is no surprise that Kieron Dyer and Lee Bowyer fought each other while under the tutelage of Souness.

Souness is proper hard. You know the kind of hard that almost causes a riot when he plants a Galatasaray flag in the centre of Fenerbahce’s pitch.

Why is he so low then you may ask? That’s because he has got a heart of gold behind all that.

In his retirement years, he met a 14-year-old girl who suffers from a rare skin disease, Epidermolysis bullosa, and went on to swim the English Channel to raise money, getting £1 for charity.

9. Sir Alex Ferguson

It’s an obvious candidate with Ferguson as his hairdryer treatment is well known in football lore.

Get on the wrong side of him and he would give you the vengeful fury of a Scotsman who previously worked in a rough pub in Glasgow.

Wayne Rooney, a particular favourite target of Fergie, recalled a time when they were winning 1-0 away at Portsmouth and despite Rooney scoring, Ferguson “battered” him at half time.

Rooney, being the combustible character he is, gave it back and Ferguson let it be known who was in charge. The paint on the walls probably needed redoing.

8. Jose Mourinho

The reason why Jose makes the list is not because he is physically intimidating, but you’d imagine it’d be like playing against Jigsaw from the Saw movies.

Mourinho is a master of the mental mind games and would be able to manipulate you in such a way that you’d turn into Jack Nicholson in The Shining.

One day you’d wake up and you would have fully descended into madness with master Mourinho pulling the strings.

No thanks.

7. Iain Dowie

Is comparing him to Sloth from The Goonies harsh? Probably yes. Does he look him? Also probably yes.

Aside from his menacing look, Dowie was known for having a short fuse. He fell out with Simon Jordan and Robbie Savage (understandably) but also let his players know when they had wronged him.

In a recent interview with Planet Football, Clinton Morrison recalled that Dowie “had a bit of a temper where he could lose it, and he’s one you wouldn’t go toe-to-toe with for sure”.

6. Roy Keane

With us safe in the knowledge that Keane doesn’t know where we live, dare we say there is a soft centre to his supposed hardman shtick?

Keane obviously has very high standards and will let you know if you fall below them but his media appearances in his latter career have shown he does at least have some kind of heart and wit behind the tough exterior.

But even with that in mind, he is in the bottom 10 of managers we would want to cross.

And don’t you dare let your phone ring near him.

5. Paolo Di Canio

Can you name another Premier League manager whose actions led to a club changing the locks on their training ground?

When Di Canio resigned as Swindon boss in 2013, he reportedly waited three days before engaging in a midnight raid of the League One side’s training ground.

Di Canio and three members of his coaching staff went under the cover of darkness to “retrieve” mementoes of his 21 months in charge.

His strangeness was not limited to Swindon either. Whilst at Sunderland, he reportedly introduced a rule that players could not talk at lunchtime. He banned fried breakfasts (fair enough) and fell out with players like Lee Cattermole.

And there are few things scarier than someone who is unpredictable.

In 2012, against Swindon, Di Canio subbed off his 21-year-old keeper just 21 minutes into a game against Preston. With the score at 2-0, Di Canio then proceeded to tear into this young player, describing it as ‘one of the worst players I have ever seen in a football match’.

Good luck to anyone who gets on the wrong side of him.

4. Ian Holloway

The reason Holloway is so high is you’d never know where you stand with him.

One minute he’s your best mate, the next he’s got a face of thunder.

He was famously outspoken whilst in charge at Blackpool and he has only got more eccentric in recent years.

The Bristolian is the current manager of Swindon and, after speaking in a tone that would not be out of place at Sunday dinner with your grandmother, he out of nowhere started banging his chest, chanting “D M W”.

Then with eyes as wide as a vinyl record, he shouts “DEAD MAN WALKING.”

How can you reason with a man like that? Knowing that is somewhere in him, lurking, waiting for a moment to strike. That is the kind of frightening behaviour that keeps you up at night.

3. Felix Magath

What’s more haunting than looking scary? Looking friendly but being anything but.

Magath looks like a friendly German uncle you may have. He passes you sweets under the table and ruffles your hair but anyone at Fulham in 2014 will know this Danger Mouse lookalike was anything but warm and cuddly.

After the relaxed nature of Martin Jol, Fulham’s owners sought to install discipline into the team and so appointed Magath.

His credentials were there. He won the Bundesliga on three occasions. He was named German Football Manager of the Year in 2003, 2005 and 2009. He’d been at clubs like Bayern Munich but Magath came with a reputation of not only being a hard taskmaster, but also a crazed one at that.

In Germany, he gained the nickname Saddam after the fallen dictator for training methods that included sending the Bayern squad running through Alpine woods in the summer only to then hide their water bottles.

Whilst at Fulham, Brede Hangeland was given a recovery programme for a thigh injury only for Magath to send a kitman to a local Tesco and buy a block of cheese. According to Magath, the cheese had soothing effects and Hangeland was told to rub it on his injury. He soon left for Brighton.

He would have three training sessions in a day, one of which was solely devoted to running, and even Premier League footballers could not cope.

And while Hannibal Lecter also shared that innocent-at-first, unnerving-underneath quality, at least his methods brought results. Magath managed 20 games at Craven Cottage and lost 12 of them.

2. Marcelo Bielsa

There’s a reason Bielsa is known as El Loco.

Google his name and try and find a single photo where he does not have a face like thunder.

He stays up late, poring over VHS while his wife presumably asks if he fancies watching a movie tonight. The Leeds United hero is famed for being able to watch two matches at the same time and what kind of sane person can do that?

But the scariest story about him comes back in 1992. When faced with angry Newell’s Old Boys fans outside his home, Bielsa walked out to meet the mob.

He steps out of his house in his pyjamas, walks towards them and reveals a live hand grenade in his hand. Leave, he says, or he pulls the pin.

The fans scarper as he shouts “Do you still want to talk?”

That makes us ask the question of why does he even have a live hand grenade in his home? But one question we don’t need answering is, is Bielsa scary?

1. Nigel Pearson

There’s few managers where you feel an actual threat of violence but Pearson fits that bill.

Just watch the famous Ostrich press conference and see the eyes of a killer.

When looking back at the career of Guti, Real Madrid’s ‘Eternal Promise’, it’s hard not to think what could have been.

A visionary playmaker and occasional forward, the academy product could delight and frustrate in equal measure, his moments of genius almost but not quite atoning for his unfulfilled potential.

At least, that’s roughly how you’re supposed to discuss a player like Guti. Or the not-Arsenal Denilson. Or Antonio Cassano.

His natural talent was unrivalled, you’re supposed to say, but laziness and the allure of a celebrity lifestyle got in the way.

He might have been Real Madrid’s answer to Xavi, you continue, had he kept his head down, given 100 per cent in every match and spent a little more time on the training pitch instead of in front of the mirror.

Those 13 caps for Spain? Oh Guti, naive Guti! Had you been a little more professional, that might have been 113!

This is the common way to speak about players with ‘unfulfilled potential’ — those with bucketloads of talent who didn’t quite become all-time greats. But not only is it patronising, it’s also wide of the mark.

The good

First off, it’s worth remembering that Guti, a player once lambasted by his own club president for still being a ‘promise’ at the age of 31, won five La Liga titles and three Champions League trophies across 15 seasons at Real.

His was a career glittering with silverware.

Guti was part of several iconic Madrid line-ups, playing with the likes of Clarence Seedorf and Davor Šuker in the 90s, with Zidane, Brazilian Ronaldo and the other Galacticos in the early 2000s, then with the likes of Kaká and Cristiano Ronaldo in his final season.

He also played various roles within those teams. A midfielder at youth level, Guti briefly became a forward, netting an impressive 18 goals in 2000-01, before eventually switching back to midfield — partly in order to accommodate the striking talents of Ronaldo, Raúl and others.

From that deeper position, the Spaniard delivered his best performances, mastering the art of the assist.

Guti can also lay claim to a strange honour: to have completed not only the best backheeled assist in football but the second best as well. (Though it’s hard to say which is which.)

The first, in a 4-2 win over Sevilla in 2006, was a pinpoint through-ball, delivered from the centre of the pitch around 25 yards from goal. The spectacular flick fooled the entire Sevilla defence, finding Zinedine Zidane’s feet and allowing the Frenchman to slam home.

— Planet Football (@planetfutebol) January 15, 2020

The second came near the end of Guti’s Madrid career, in a match against Deportivo in January 2010.

Clean through on goal, Guti elected not to shoot but to backheel the ball away from goal to a grateful Karim Benzema. It was a moment of utter madness and sheer brilliance.

Marca dedicated its front page to the pass, its headline reading ‘The backheel of God’.

The bad

Those are the good bits, but Madrid fans will also talk of a player who went missing in big games, who was petulant and picked up cards seemingly out of boredom — a footballer who ultimately preferred making himself pretty to making himself play better.

It’s hardly an unfamiliar tale in the sport.

One of the most widely circulated stories about Guti’s less-than-impeccable attitude took place during Fabio Capello’s second spell as Madrid boss.

Capello, so the story goes, had instructed his players to wait in the centre circle at the start of the training session. Wanting to see whether the squad would follow orders, Capello turned up 15 minutes late…on purpose.

When he arrived, he found one player, Guti, sheltering from the Spanish sun in a dugout.

That love of leisure didn’t abate with age, either. During his final Madrid season, Guti may have been pulling off outrageous backheels, but he was already thinking of retiring to a beach in Thailand — a fact he gleefully admitted during a television interview.

And when it wasn’t about putting his feet up, it was seemingly about getting his hair right.

Former Real Madrid players Steve McManaman and Michael Owen recently agreed that Guti was the ‘vainest’ player they had ever played with. “He had a mirror right next to his space in the dressing room,” Owen said.

Unprofessionalism is a stick constantly used to beat Guti with. But even if these stories are only the tip of the iceberg, even if Guti was one of the laziest players in Real Madrid’s history, is that any reason to criticise him?

The lazy

There’s a certain moralism in football analysis when it comes to a player’s work ethic. Those who enjoy themselves in their spare time are deemed selfish playboys; those who stick around for an hour after training are worshipped as gods.

You only have to look at the current fawning over players like Jesse Lingard to see how this weird mindset pervades people’s thinking.

While many have (wrongly) criticised Lingard for his social media japes and choreographed celebrations, others have (perhaps also wrongly) applauded the winger for his professionalism: he makes the most of his abilities by giving 100 per cent in every match, they say, and he deserves a huge amount of credit for that.

I can understand why you might adopt this attitude when trying to motivate a child. But as a judgment on an adult’s life, isn’t it a little weird?

Cristiano Ronaldo received praise from Alex Ferguson for practising his horrible free-kick technique after training. And that makes sense: managers should encourage hard work among their own players in order to get results.

But does it make Ronaldo praiseworthy in any objective sense? God no. Go and make friends, you weirdo. You’re young. Work’s over.

With Guti, those so-called negative habits — the grooming, the fondness for celebrity culture, the love of leisure — are no more or less praiseworthy than spending time in a gym or doing a few extra keep-ups.

In fact, if you turned down a holiday with your friends, telling them you’d rather stay at the office to fulfil your potential as an account manager, they’d think you’d lost the plot. You would have lost the plot.

And so with football: yes, footballers can always dig a little deeper, raise their level a notch. But at some stage, after so many titles, you have to wonder whether that digging is really the best use of your time.

When he secured his place in Madrid’s first team, Guti had made it. Completely, utterly made it. Fulfilled the childhood dreams of a million Spaniards.

To wonder what could have been — to say that Guti wasted his potential by balancing football with his personal life — is a glass-half-empty way to look at an occasionally breathtaking career.

NWSL announces roster-building mechanisms for 2028 expansion teams

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Two expansion teams will play their inaugural National Women’s Soccer League seasons in 2028: Atlanta City FC and the to-be-named Columbus club. With the teams able to start building their rosters in 2027, the league announced Friday the mechanisms they can use to build their inaugural rosters, since they got rid of the expansion draft ahead of Boston Legacy FC and Denver Summit FC joining the league.

The options available to Atlanta and Columbus are relatively the same as the mechanisms Boston and Denver had but adjusted for the consistently increasing salary cap.

Each expansion club will have access to $1,430,000 in allocation money, roughly 30% of the 2028 base salary cap according to the announcement. Allocation money must be funded by the clubs and may be used to reduce salary cap charges, pay transfer and loan fees, and be used in trades with all other clubs. Unused, funded allocation money will remain available through December 31, 2030, including any funds subsequently traded to another club.

Comparatively, Denver and Boston had access to the same percentage of allocation money, but 50% of the funds were provided by the league and the rest funded by the teams at their discretion. They have until December 31, 2027, to use all of their funded allocation money. Allocation money purchased before 2024 via the mechanism’s original iteration will be phased out by the end of 2026, so it now appears that allocation money will live on as a unicorn available to expansion teams in the short term.

Atlanta and Columbus will also have access to an increased transfer fee threshold of $1.9 million over two years, compared to the threshold of $665,500 for non-expansion clubs in 2027. Starting on the first day of 2027 and through the conclusion of the 2028 secondary transfer window, the expansion teams have $1,167,950 available, then an additional $732,050 during the 2028 calendar year. Spending above the applicable threshold will incur a salary cap charge equal to 25 percent of the excess amount, same as the rest of the league.

For a more in-depth explanation of allocation money and transfer fee thresholds, check out this explainer from Jeff Kassouf.

In order to allow the expansion clubs to start signing players in 2027 ahead of their inaugural seasons, they will be subject to a limited salary cap of $628,320, equivalent to 14.28 percent of the 2027 base salary cap. High Impact Player (HIP) rule spending is not available until January 1, 2028.

The previous pair of expansion teams had a 6-month period during which they could sign players without being subject to the salary cap as long as they had a net-zero salary cap impact by being: loaned out to a team that picks up the full cost of salary and bonuses; qualified to be placed on SEI; or allocation money was used to cover the salary cap charge. Then they had a limited salary cap of $250,00 after the second transfer window opened.

Once again, expansion clubs will not be required to meet the league’s minimum roster size requirements in 2027 but must remain within the maximum roster size. So the players signed in 2027 can keep playing expansion clubs may loan them to clubs within or outside the NWSL. A new wrinkle for intra-league loans during 2027: the expansion clubs are permitted to retain up to 25% of the salary cap charge. Standard loan accounting rules and required roster compliance will resume in 2028.

Ahead of tomorrow’s game against AC Milan, Inter boss Cristian Chivu spoke to the media about his team’s chances.

Having dropped points once this season – a draw against Roma – they’re rightly the favourites heading into this game, especially with the injuries plaguing Ruben Amorim’s side. Nevertheless, there are never really favourites in these games.

Chivu’s pre-Milan words

Speaking before the game on Friday, Ruben Amorim spoke about a number of things, admitting that his team still needed to improve this season and he’s not the only side of the derby to say that.

In his own press conference this evening, Chivu admitted the same, as MilanVibes relayed.

How are you approaching the derby?

“We’re coming prepared and aware of how important this match is for everyone. In a derby, you shouldn’t think about the standings, but only about doing everything you can to win.

“Our approach will depend on the team’s physical and mental condition, but also on the opponent’s characteristics. We know Milan’s quality, their dribbling, and their ability to hurt us through technique and speed. We’ll have to be ready and stay calm at all times.”

Seven derbies without a win…

“If we haven’t won a derby in such a long time, it means we need to ask ourselves some questions and figure out what we can improve on. I don’t think we lacked commitment, because in matches like this, incidents can also have an impact, but we definitely need to improve.

“We can’t settle for recent results, nor for last season’s, nor for what we’ve done so far this season: we need to show our best.

“The derby is a match that can give a lot to the winner and take a lot away from the loser, but we’re only at the tenth matchday, and last season’s history shows that the result of a derby doesn’t always determine what happens next.

On the team’s centre of gravity…

“Our pressing is often misinterpreted. We don’t go looking for a man in every area of ​​the pitch, but rather work on references. In recent games, we’ve tried to be more organised, choosing when to attack and when to wait. We can’t have the same attitude throughout the entire match: we need to understand the moments and always find the right balance.”

The match against Venezia…

“We need to rediscover that hunger that allows us to go beyond individual qualities. We can’t think that individual talent is enough to resolve every situation. We need to be more aggressive in duels, in pressing, and in defensive runs, putting in even more sacrifice.

On Thuram…

“When you’re forced to come from behind, you inevitably expend a lot more energy. It also happened against Shakhtar, when we managed to win despite being down to ten men. The game we really messed up was the one against Brugge.
