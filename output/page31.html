Three Companies, One Lesson: When AI Judges People, Trust Is the Business Model

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I have started three companies, and in all of them, software makes some kind of judgment about people. At Pravoved.ru, the online legal marketplace I co-founded, it was about matching people with lawyers and answering their legal questions. At Jinn, an HR-tech platform, it was assessing employees and candidates for corporate clients. Now at Narvalus, which I co-founded in Miami, we are building an AI recruiting agent for U.S. hiring teams.

When I started each of them, I thought the main risk was technical. Will the model be accurate enough? It almost never turned out to be the problem. The problem was that people didn't believe the system, even when it was right. And that changed not only the product but how we sold it, priced it, and decided where to start.

At Pravoved, trust was the product.

When we launched Pravoved, legal help in Russia mostly meant going to an office, paying an unclear price, and hoping the lawyer was good. Nobody could compare. Our bet was that people would ask a question online if they could see several lawyers answer it and check their ratings from other clients.

That bet worked, but the interesting part came later. Most incoming questions were standard, the same consumer-law issues again and again. So we built an AI assistant trained on our lawyers' answers to handle them. Technically, it was fine. What made users accept it was that a real lawyer was always one click away and the reasoning was visible. When an answer came without context, people doubted it, even when it was correct.

The business lesson for me was simple. Automate what is standard, keep people for what is not, and never hide which is which. It also pushed us toward corporate clients. Companies wanted predictable, documented legal support, and over time B2B grew to about 65% of our revenue. Investors from AddVenture and Target Global backed that model, in part because trust was built into it and not added later.

At Jinn, clients didn't buy scores.

With Jinn, I moved into HR. The platform runs assessments, 360-degree feedback, and engagement surveys, and uses AI to turn that data into recommendations about people. We went from one corporate client to more than seventy in a few years.

For an HR director, the accuracy percentage of a model is not the real question. The real questions are "Can I explain this to the manager?" and "What do I tell the employee if they ask?" A score alone did not survive those questions. A score with clear reasons behind it did. So the explanation became the thing we really sold, and the score was more like a summary of it.

Narvalus: Same lesson, higher stakes.

Recruiting is where this matters most, because the AI influences who gets an interview and who doesn't. And in the U.S., the rules are getting stricter. New York City's Local Law 144 requires an independent bias audit, a published summary, and notice to candidates when automated employment decision tools are used. The EU AI Act puts AI used in recruitment in the high-risk category. And existing anti-discrimination law applies whether a person or a program made the decision.

So when we designed the Narvalus agent, we started from trust and worked back to the model. A few decisions came out of that.

The agent works from the job description, criterion by criterion. It turns the job description into concrete requirements and checks each candidate against each one, with a short reason for every match or gap. The recruiter can see exactly why someone is near the top, without writing Boolean search strings.

It flags questions; it doesn't reject. If two jobs on a resume overlap in time, or a career path is unusual, the agent marks it as something to ask in the interview. People make every hiring decision. This was not a hard choice for me after Pravoved: the unusual cases are exactly where a human is worth the most.

Scoring and auditing are kept apart. The scoring step is designed never to receive age, photos, gender, or other protected attributes. But just removing fields doesn't remove every proxy; a graduation year can still hint at age. So we are building the audit side separately: where candidates choose to share demographic data, it is stored apart from scoring and used only to report selection rates for bias audits. If you skip that part, you can't actually prove the first part works.

How trust shaped the business, not just the product.

This is the part I think founders underestimate. Explainability is shaping our go-to-market plan as much as our architecture.

First, the starting market. We chose to begin in Miami with staffing agencies and small and mid-sized employers. They have a lot of applicants and small teams, often without a dedicated sourcer or in-house counsel. A big company can afford to check a vendor's claims itself. A ten-person agency can't, so a tool that explains itself is worth more to them, and they can decide quickly.

Second, pricing. We plan a simple per-seat subscription with a free trial on real open roles. If the value is that recruiters trust the shortlist, the fastest way to prove it is to let them try it on their own vacancies, not to show a demo.

Third, the enterprise tier. Bias-audit reporting and documentation will go into the higher plan. Larger employers carry more legal exposure, and for them compliance is not a nice extra. It decides whether the deal moves forward at all.

What I would tell other founders.

If your product judges people in some way, for jobs, loans, housing, or legal help, build for the most skeptical person who will read the output: a regulator, a lawyer, or the person being judged. If the explanation doesn't hold up for them, the feature is not finished. And design your business around that skeptic too, because they are often the one who signs off on the purchase.

At Pravoved, the AI never replaced lawyers. It took the routine questions so lawyers had more time for the hard ones. I believe recruiting will go the same way. In a year or two, we will see if I was right.

Valery Meshkov is a serial technology entrepreneur. He co-founded Pravoved.ru, an online legal marketplace backed by AddVenture and Target Global, founded the HR-tech platform Jinn, and is a co-founder of Narvalus, an early-stage company developing an AI recruiting agent for U.S. hiring teams.

Selma Blair Fights Back Tears While Apologizing for Flight Incident

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Selma Blair is speaking out more than two weeks after she was arrested for an alleged altercation on a Delta flight.

The actress, 54, fought back tears as she addressed and apologized for the incident in a video posted via Instagram on Friday, October 30.

“Hi, it’s me. And I know I’ve been quiet for a couple of weeks since October 11th and you deserve to hear from me directly and I’m sorry that it’s taken this long,” she began. “There is a legal matter going on and my lawyers have asked me to not say anything about that day and I’m listening to them and I take this very seriously.”

While she did not get into specifics of the flight incident, Blair insisted she was sorry for what had occurred and she never intended any ill will.

Selma Blair Breaks Silence on Flight Disturbance: 'Did Not Intend to Harm'

“What I can tell you, though, is very simple, and the truest thing I know about myself, and that not one day ever have I intended to hurt or frighten anyone a day in my life,” she said. “To the people that I disappointed, that I hurt, that I frightened, alarmed, I am so sorry.”

Blair also shared her gratitude for people who had expressed their support in the wake of the scandal.

“And to the people that have written, or called, or sent wishes of love or caring, it has meant everything to me and my son, who is my world, as is [Blair’s service dog] Scout, who is also okay,” Blair said.

 

View this post on Instagram

The Cruel Intentions star concluded the message by sharing, “So right now I’m going to be taking care of my health and my son, and thinking of you all with gratitude and love.”

Blair was arrested earlier this month after allegedly assaulting flight attendants on a plane ride from Los Angeles to New York.

“She was taken into custody and transported to the hospital for evaluation,” the JFK Port Authority Police Department told Us in a statement on October 11. “Two flight attendants also reported injuries, and one was transported to the hospital for further evaluation. The FBI is investigating this incident.”

Blair, whose lawyer claimed the assault was not intentional and that she was confused after missing doses of her multiple sclerosis medication, pleaded not guilty to the charges during a October 12, hearing. She was released from police custody on bond.

“Selma Blair experienced a medical emergency on the flight,” Blair’s rep told Us in an October 16 statement, addressing the incident for the first time. “There was no attempt to open the door of the aircraft. Selma did not intend to harm anyone.”

Selma Blair Pleads Not Guilty to Assault After Plane Incident, Released on Bail

The spokesperson further hit back against “outrageous” speculation about Blair’s MS battle.

“Her diagnosis has been public since 2018. What Selma has been able to do physically in the years since is nothing short of a miracle. She still has bad days, and she lives with pain daily,” the statement continued. “Selma also has pseudobulbar affect, a neurological condition secondary to her MS, which bears directly on how a medical episode presents to people who do not know her. For the same reason, she travels with a service dog who doesn’t leave her side, and the authorities took her dog from her.”

Per the spokesperson, Blair has continued working on multiple projects since her diagnosis while also becoming a “visible and relentless advocate for disability rights.”

The statement concluded, “There is much more to this story than has been reported, and we ask to consider the entirety of the facts before rushing to judgment, whether in the courtroom or in the court of public opinion.”

Blair’s next hearing is scheduled for January 19, 2027.
