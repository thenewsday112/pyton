What Is EIP-7702? Ethereum's Smart Account Feature Explained, and How to Stay Safe

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

<p>On October 29, 2026, an Ethereum hot wallet belonging to Binance was emptied in a single transaction.</p> <p>The tool behind the drain was EIP-7702, a feature that went live on Ethereum in May 2025.</p> <p>As of October 30, 2026, no flaw in EIP-7702 itself has been reported.</p> <p>It did exactly what a valid signature told it to do, and that is the part worth understanding.</p> <p><strong>Key Takeaways</strong></p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; EIP-7702 lets a regular Ethereum wallet run smart contract code, and it went live on May 7, 2025.</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; A token approval covers one token for one app, while an EIP-7702 delegation hands a contract your whole account.</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Every delegation needs the wallet’s own signature, so the Binance hack looks like a signing-side failure, not an Ethereum bug.</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Exchange users should watch official notices, withdrawal status, proof of reserves and the post-mortem.</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Self-custody users only face EIP-7702 risk if they signed a delegation, which Etherscan’s Delegated Address field shows.</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; A delegation can be reset to the zero address, but if you don’t remember signing it, treat the wallet as compromised.</p> <h2 id="h-what-is-eip-7702"><strong>What Is EIP-7702?</strong></h2> <p>EIP-7702 is an Ethereum upgrade that lets a regular<a href="https://www.mexc.com/learn/article/what-is-a-cryptocurrency-wallet-/1?utm_source=Hackernoon&amp;utm_medium=article&amp;utm_campaign=what_is_eip_7702&amp;utm_id=MEXC_Learn_Channel"> crypto wallet</a>, known as an externally owned account or EOA, borrow smart contract code and behave like a smart account.</p> <p>It shipped with the Pectra upgrade, which went live on Ethereum mainnet on May 7, 2025.</p> <p>The official specification, EIP-7702: Set Code for EOAs, adds a new transaction type that carries signed “authorizations.”</p> <p>Each one names a contract and must be signed with the wallet’s own<a href="https://www.mexc.com/crypto-glossary/article/private-key-170181?utm_source=Hackernoon&amp;utm_medium=article&amp;utm_campaign=what_is_eip_7702&amp;utm_id=MEXC_Learn_Channel"> private key</a>.</p> <p>Our<a href="https://www.mexc.com/learn/article/17827791519018?utm_source=Hackernoon&amp;utm_medium=article&amp;utm_campaign=what_is_eip_7702&amp;utm_id=MEXC_Learn_Channel"> overview of the Ethereum Pectra upgrade</a> covers the rest of that release.</p> <h2 id="h-how-does-eip-7702-work"><strong>How Does EIP-7702 Work?</strong></h2> <p>It starts with a signature.</p> <p>You sign an authorization that names a contract, the chain it covers and your account’s<a href="https://www.mexc.com/crypto-glossary/article/nonce-170307?utm_source=Hackernoon&amp;utm_medium=article&amp;utm_campaign=what_is_eip_7702&amp;utm_id=MEXC_Learn_Channel"> nonce</a>, which is simply a running count of your transactions.</p> <p>Any account can then submit that authorization to Ethereum, which is how a third party can pay the fee for you.</p> <p>Once it lands, the network writes a small pointer on your account, which the specification calls a delegation indicator.</p> <p>From then on, calls to your address run the code of the contract it points to, while your private key keeps full control of the account.</p> <p>To undo it, you sign a new authorization that points to the zero address, and the account goes back to being a plain wallet.</p> <h2 id="h-how-does-eip-7702-bring-account-abstraction-to-wallets"><strong>How Does EIP-7702 Bring Account Abstraction to Wallets?</strong></h2> <p>Account abstraction is the push to make Ethereum wallets programmable instead of limited to one signature and one action at a time.</p> <p>EIP-7702 brings that idea to existing wallets, so nobody has to move funds to a new address.</p> <p>The specification names three uses:</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <strong>Batching:</strong> several actions, such as approve and swap, in one all-or-nothing transaction</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <strong>Sponsorship:</strong> another account pays the network fee for you and can be repaid in a token other than ETH</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <strong>Privilege de-escalation:</strong> a sub-key with narrow permissions, such as spending tokens but not ETH</p> <p>Each of these depends on the contract your wallet points to, which is why the next section matters.</p> <h2 id="h-how-is-an-eip-7702-delegation-different-from-a-token-approval"><strong>How Is an EIP-7702 Delegation Different From a Token Approval?</strong></h2> <p>A token approval lets one app move one token, usually up to a set amount.</p> <p>An EIP-7702 delegation hands the logic of your entire account to a contract, covering every asset inside it, until you reset it.</p> <p>That gap in scope is why a bad delegation does far more damage than a bad approval.</p> <p>Think of your wallet as a house.</p> <p>A token approval is like handing someone the key to one cabinet to take out one item.</p> <p>An EIP-7702 delegation is closer to hiring a house manager who runs every room.</p> <p>A good manager, like a reputable wallet app’s smart account contract, only follows your instructions.</p> <p>A malicious one can clear out the whole house in one trip.</p> <p></p><p>** <img alt="" src="https://cdn.hackernoon.com/images/InxBRjRIs6M1kdhuWcyNHiiUrxm1-7he3bv2.jpeg" />**</p><p>Your private key stays the master key, which also means anyone holding it can hire a new manager.</p> <h2 id="h-what-happened-in-the-binance-eip-7702-hack"><strong>What Happened in the Binance EIP-7702 Hack?</strong></h2> <p>On October 29, 2026, an attacker used signed EIP-7702 authorizations to point Binance’s Ethereum hot wallet at the attacker’s own contract.</p> <p>Minutes later, one transaction emptied it.</p> <p>Early on-chain estimates put losses across Binance’s wallets in the billions of dollars as of October 30, 2026.</p> <p>Public on-chain data shows four steps:</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <strong>Sign:</strong> Around 20:20 UTC, a batch of authorizations signed with the wallet’s key landed on Ethereum.</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <strong>Point:</strong> The wallet’s nonce kept changing, so the batch reportedly covered many values, and the one that matched handed the wallet to the attacker’s contract.</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <strong>Sweep:</strong> About three minutes later, one call moved out ETH, stablecoins and dozens of other tokens.</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <strong>Reset:</strong>Almost four hours later, the delegation was revoked.<br /><img alt="" src="https://cdn.hackernoon.com/images/InxBRjRIs6M1kdhuWcyNHiiUrxm1-4df3bv6.jpeg" />Step one is the key detail: a delegation needs the wallet’s own valid signature, so either the key itself leaked or a system that holds it signed when it should not have.</p> <h2 id="h-was-it-a-leaked-private-key-or-a-tricked-signing-system"><strong>Was It a Leaked Private Key or a Tricked Signing System?</strong></h2> <p>As of October 30, 2026, Binance had not published a root cause.</p> <p>Two explanations are on the table, pointing to very different weak spots.</p> <p>The first is a leaked private key, a familiar failure with a familiar fix: retire the key and lock down everyone who had access.</p> <p>The second is more unsettling.</p> <p>Many exchanges route<a href="https://www.mexc.com/crypto-glossary/article/hot-wallet-170628?utm_source=Hackernoon&amp;utm_medium=article&amp;utm_campaign=what_is_eip_7702&amp;utm_id=MEXC_Learn_Channel"> hot wallet</a> signing through automated services that check where funds are going and how much is moving.</p> <p>An EIP-7702 authorization is not a normal transfer.</p> <p>It is a short signed message naming a contract, with no recipient or amount for those rules to inspect.</p> <p>If a signing service approved one without decoding it, the exchange’s own security layer signed away control of the wallet.<br /><img alt="" src="https://cdn.hackernoon.com/images/InxBRjRIs6M1kdhuWcyNHiiUrxm1-nig3bwd.jpeg" />Early reports also described funds leaving on blockchains that have no EIP-7702 feature at all, which, if confirmed, would point to the signing side.</p> <h2 id="h-is-my-money-still-safe-on-an-exchange-after-the-binance-hack"><strong>Is My Money Still Safe on an Exchange After the Binance Hack?</strong></h2> <p>A hot wallet is only the slice of an exchange’s funds kept online for withdrawals.</p> <p>Whether customers are made whole depends on the exchange’s reserves and what it formally commits to, such as a<a href="https://www.mexc.com/learn/article/your-crypto-exchange-protection-fund-does-not-cover-the-loss-you-will-actually-have/1?utm_source=Hackernoon&amp;utm_medium=article&amp;utm_campaign=what_is_eip_7702&amp;utm_id=MEXC_Learn_Channel"> protection fund</a>, so trust official updates over social media posts.</p> <p>Watch for:</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Announcements on Binance’s own website and verified accounts</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; Deposit and withdrawal status for each network</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; An updated proof of reserves report</p> <p>●&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; A post-mortem that names the root cause and the fix</p> <p>Scammers move fast after big hacks, so check any “refund” link against our<a guide to spotting crypto scams</a> first.</p> <p>Proof of reserves is an exchange’s published evidence that its holdings cover what it owes users, and our guide on<a href="https://www.mexc.com/learn/article/how-to-assess-whether-an-exchanges-funds-are-safe-understanding-the-por-mechanism-and-verification-methods/1?utm_source=Hackernoon&amp;utm_medium=article&amp;utm_campaign=what_is_eip_7702&amp;utm_id=MEXC_Learn_Channel"> Merkle tree check</a>.</p> <h2 id="h-should-i-move-my-crypto-off-an-exchange-after-a-hack"><strong>Should I Move My Crypto Off an Exchange After a Hack?</strong></h2> <p>A hack at one exchange does how to move crypto from Binance to MEXC</a> covers network choice and test transfers.</p> <h2 id="h-does-eip-7702-put-my-own-wallet-at-risk"><strong>Does EIP-7702 Put My Own Wallet at href="https://www.mexc.com/crypto-glossary/article/non-custodial-wallet-170309?utm_source=Hackernoon&amp;utm_medium=article&amp;utm_campaign=what_is_eip_7702&amp;utm_id=MEXC_Learn_Channel"> because whoever holds the key can sign a fresh delegation a minute later.</p> <h2 id="h-could-an-eip-7702-attack-happen-again"><strong>Could an EIP-7702 Attack Happen Again?</strong></h2> <p>EIP-7702 is a finalized, live part of Ethereum, so the feature is not going away.</p> <p>The clearest fix sits with whoever controls signing: systems that decode every authorization and refuse any contract that isn’t pre-approved.</p> <p>The specification itself says there is no safe way to let apps request delegations freely.</p> <h2 id="h-faq"><strong>FAQ</strong></h2> <p><strong>Is EIP-7702 a bug in Ethereum upgrade added EIP-7702?</strong></p> <p>EIP-7702 shipped in the Pectra upgrade, which activated on May 7, 2025.</p> <p><strong>How do I revoke an EIP-7702 delegation?</strong></p> <p>Sign a new delegation to the zero address from your wallet app, which resets the account to a plain wallet.</p> <p><strong>Does an EIP-7702 delegation work on other chains?</strong></p> <p>Only on the chain 2026, no flaw in EIP-7702 had been reported, and the delegation carried the wallet’s own valid signature.</p> <p><strong>Do I need to do anything with my ETH because of EIP-7702?</strong></p> <p>No, your ETH needs no upgrade, and you only need to act if you find a delegation you don’t recognize.</p> <p><strong>Is my money on Binance safe after the EIP-7702 hack?</strong></p> <p>As of October 30,
