Not just San Siro’s replacement: The complete new range of Serie A stadiums being planned

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Inter and AC Milan have unveiled the plans for the new Generali Stadium that will replace the iconic San Siro – and it should be just one of several new grounds popping up across Italy.

Ahead of the country co-hosting Euro 2032 with Turkey, Italy is in need of a big revamp of its stadiums. That means some difficult and emotional decisions like the demolition of the San Siro to make way for Milan and Inter’s new shared home.

Italy last hosted a major tournament when it was the 1990 World Cup and many of its stadia are still stuck in that era.

It doesn’t help that many are run by local authorities rather than clubs themselves; only Juventus, Atalanta, Udinese and Sassuolo own their own grounds out of the current 20 Serie A clubs.

But many have ambitions to change that. Here are the Serie A stadium projects to keep an eye on.

AC Milan and Inter

Back in the summer of 2019, the two Milan rivals revealed their plans to club together for a new stadium in place of San Siro, which has hosted them both since the 1940s.

Now, we’ve finally got eyes on what the new stadium, to be called the Generali thanks to a sponsorship deal struck with an Italian insurance firm, will look like.

San Siro’s transformation into Generali Stadium 🇮🇹

Saying goodbye to a venue as historic as the San Siro will cause some conflicting emotions, especially when the new design lacks its characteristic beams.

But it’s a necessity for the two clubs to keep up with the demands modern football. The original San Siro wouldn’t have come into consideration for the Euros.

All being well, work will start on the 71,500-capacity Generali Stadium in 2028 and it should be up and running for the 2031-32 season.

The design is partly inspired by Milan’s Duomo Cathedral and has been overseen by the same architects behind the new – well, not really new anymore, but you know what we mean – Wembley Stadium.

Roma

You know what they say, Rome wasn’t built in a day. Well, it was back in the 1980s that then-Roma president Dino Viola vowed he wanted to secure a new stadium for his club. Fast forward to the 2020s and they’re still playing at the Stadio Olimpico.

Roma’s attempts to build a new stadium over the past 10 years or so have hit a wide range of setbacks. See in 2017, for example, when plans to build on one site were met with opposition because of… frogs and their nesting areas. We’re not joking.

But now there’s a sense that Roma are closer than ever to realising Viola’s vision, more than 30 years after his death.

Their plans for a new build in the Pietralata region of Rome, with a capacity of up to 62,000, got government approval in September.

They now hope to break ground in the spring of 2027 before finishing in 2029.

Incidentally, their local rivals Lazio also want to move out of the Olimpico, but their plans are to renovate the abandoned Stadio Flaminio nearby rather than oversee a complete new build.

Venezia

To get to Venezia’s current stadium, the Pierluigi Penzo, you have to travel by boat or on foot. It’s definitely quirky, but this is Venice, after all.

Of the current crop of Serie A teams, only Genoa have an older stadium. Venezia’s has been in use since 1913 and was most recently renovated in 2021.

But Venezia, who have been a bit of a yo-yo club in recent years, are now preparing for a move to the mainland.

Their new stadium will seat 18,500 people, with the capacity able to increase for concerts and other events.

Venezia will invest €125m into the project and have signed a 40-year agreement with the city council to manage their new stadium.

“After decades of debates and words, the dream of a modern and functional stadium is finally something we can touch,” exclaimed Venetian mayor Simone Venturini. “It will be the home of Venezia FC, but also a source of opportunities for the whole area.”

The opening date is slated in for the second half of next season. Venezia will, of course, be hoping to have retained their top-flight status by then, but they’re currently bottom of the league.

Bologna

Bologna had been toying with the idea of renovating the Stadio Renato Dall’Ara for a while, but have instead launched plans for a new stadium.

They hope to construct a 35,000-seater venue in the city’s exhibition centre by the spring of 2031.

The architects involved also worked on the development of the Juventus Stadium that opened 15 years ago and led the way for what should be a new era of Italian arenas.

Cagliari

Cagliari moved out of the Stadio Sant’Elia in 2017, with the venue now known as the Unipol Domus becoming their temporary home while they built their new home for good.

That ‘temporary’ has lasted a fair while longer than they expected.

But now their hopes of erecting a new stadium near the site of their old home are finally getting somewhere.

The city council have approved their plans for a venue with a capacity of up to 30,000, also revamping the surrounding area.

Should things run smoothly from here, they’ll have a chance of being chosen as a Euros host.

Bayern Munich are still interested in signing Rio Ngumoha.

Gianluca Di Marzio now reports that the Bavarian giants will try to make a push for the teenage gem in the upcoming January transfer window.

Bayern Munich could do with Rio Ngumoha after Luis Diaz injury

(Photo by Carl Recine/Getty Images)

The simple fact of the matter for FC Bayern is that their season – or, to put it more accurately, the fate of their Champions League campaign – may hinge on how they solve their left wing concern.

Luis Diaz, without question, is a sensational talent, but his hamstring injury (picked up in the 2-2 draw against Arsenal) raises questions around the security of the position long term.

Hypothetically, were the reigning Bundesliga champions to find themselves deep in European competition, they’re unlikely to want to be forced to use either Tim Binder or Alphonso Davies. Serge Gnabry can also cover the left flank, if need be, but this is a far from perfect solution.

So, if anyone could do with an extremely talented, 18-year-old left winger whose long-term pathway wouldn’t be obstructed by a senior starter (in this case, 29-year-old Diaz) – it’s most definitely Bayern.

“It’s true that Bayern Munich want Rio Ngumoha, and it’s true that Liverpool want to extend his contract – so it’s a difficult situation because anything is possible,” Di Marzio told Wettfruende.

“Liverpool are pushing to extend his contract and keep him in the team in the future, but it’s also true that Bayern Munich will try to sign him as early as January.”

The journalist went on to add: “I think it’s not a very high probability, but a good one that he will extend his contract, as he enjoys his time at Liverpool and has a good relationship with the manager; therefore, it’s hard to imagine Liverpool letting him go.

“FC Bayern Munich is pushing for it, and Arsenal is also interested.”

Arsenal shouldn’t be of concern to Liverpool

(Photo by Leonhard Simon/Getty Images)

We’re pretty confident that Rio Ngumoha will commit his immediate and long-term future to Liverpool with a contract extension.

The Englishman made clear that he chose life at Anfield over staying at Chelsea due to the pathway that was offered to him at L4, telling ITV Sport earlier in October: “Liverpool was always the right option – and the only option really.

“[I thought]: ‘If I go here I’ll get the opportunity and the pathway to showcase what I can do’. It looks like so far it’s paid off!”

After the 2-1 win over Brentford, the winger suggested that talks between his agent and the club were going well.

Yet, here we are, with rumours swirling around Arsenal and Bayern Munich. If we’re talking about a pathway to more regular first team minutes – we’re not particularly worried about Arsenal. We just can’t see the Gunners offering Ngumoha a better deal in that regard.

The concern for us is whether the teenager looks at the current state of play in English football – and the joy talents like Diaz and Michael Olise have had since departing – and opts for life in a less demanding league. Or, to put it differently, one in which he can operate without being kicked around quite as much.

In that sense, it would be naïve of us to just assume that Liverpool is definitely the “better” project compared to Vincent Kompany’s Bayern Munich.

If anything, the current climate, coupled with Harry Kane’s Ballon d’Or win in London, indicates that life in Germany isn’t quite so bad.

Newcastle United Sporting Director Ross Wilson, is he the elephant in the room at St James’ Park?

We saw a huge change at Newcastle United ahead of summer 2026, the club moving to a new model when it came to player recruitment and transfers in and out generally.

Rather than the Manager/Head Coach being the main man when it came to transfers, it was now Newcastle United Sporting Director Ross Wilson.

This isn’t some brand new concept, many other clubs in the modern game do the same, especially on the continent.

David Hopkinson remaining in charge of the business side of the football club.

Ross Wilson becoming undoubtedly the main man in charge of the football side, this no longer whoever picks the team.

This model has inherent dangers.

At Everton last night, the Newcastle United starting eleven had more outfield players who were bought in when Mike Ashley was in control than during the time Ross Wilson has been in control of transfers.

Fabian Schar, Joe Willock and Jacob Murphy were in the team last night, as were Amar Dedic and Nico Gonzalez. Goalkeeper Lukas Hornicek evened things up when it came to overall starters from the Mike Ashley era vs Ross Wilson in control of transfers.

How did the biggest crypto heist in history just happen?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Binance is the biggest cryptocurrency exchange ZUMA Press Wire/Shutterstock

The world’s largest cryptocurrency exchange Binance has been hit with the biggest ever cryptocurrency heist, in which billions of dollars of reserves were drained in just 20 minutes.

From 8:05pm on 29 October, $6.36 billion in cryptocurrencies were shifted from wallets in a coordinated attack believed to have originated from a North Korean-linked hacking unit called TraderTraitor. This group was also behind a $1.5 billion hack of the Bybit crypto exchange in February 2025.

The US Treasury has previously said that such stolen funds are used to finance North Korea’s nuclear missile programme.

Advertisement

Despite cryptocurrency’s founding idea of decentralisation, meaning no one has singular control over the money flowing through the network, there are some large single points of failure. It is thought one of these was the cause of the Binance hack, which gave access to a system the exchange uses to authorise transfers out of customers’ accounts.

Quantum computer quickly mines cryptocurrency while using less energy

In a statement, Binance said its early investigations indicate the security incident may stem from a compromised laptop belonging to an engineer. TRM Labs, a cybersecurity firm, suggests that preparations for the attack may have begun several days ago.

Binance claims it has found no evidence that the private cryptographic keys used to authorise transactions were stolen. Instead, the attackers appear to have gained access to the software that requests withdrawals, potentially allowing them to instruct Binance’s own systems to approve the transfers. However, the investigation is continuing and the firm has said in a subsequent statement there may have been “additional points of breach”.

“It shouldn’t be possible for a single [developer] PC to be compromised and somehow for that to allow the entire exchange to be compromised,” says Alan Woodward at the University of Surrey, UK. “I don’t understand how that can happen.”

If that is the case, it highlights how precarious major financial hubs in the crypto world can be to single points of failure.

“You can build a vault with mathematical firewalls, but if the individual holding the keys can be duped, the vault is opened,” says Clive Summerfield at FARx, a cybersecurity company. “Most crypto platforms are as centralised as any bank, with a small number of privileged individuals controlling billions. The difference is that once crypto is transferred, it’s gone.”

That centralisation can be a blessing as well as a curse, though: cryptocurrencies Tether and Circle have been able to freeze billions of dollars of stolen coins, stymying the hackers in attempts to transfer about half the stolen funds any further.

Woodward says the Binance incident potentially strikes a distinction between crypto security and more traditional finance. “You would hope there’d be things like two-man rules [where two separate people have to approve a change] or things that banks have for changing critical systems, but it doesn’t look like those were in place.”
