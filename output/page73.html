It delivers context-aware optimization without relying on manual audits or generic checklists.</p> <p>The agent evaluates your environment as an experienced cloud architect would. It automatically correlates utilization metrics, resource configurations, and application topology, and analyzes against Well-Architected best practices across 65+ AWS services. You declare your business objectives and share your application context. The agent automatically generates and prioritizes recommendations by impact and effort against those goals.</li> <li><strong>Three-level recommendations</strong>: AWS Well-Architected Agent provides individual resource findings with specific dollar impact (where applicable) and step-by-step remediation, consolidated findings across multiple resources scoped to your application, and broad architectural patterns and designs with Infrastructure as Code (IaC) code changes needed to align with Well-Architected best practices.</li> <li><strong>Optionality in remediation</strong>: You can choose your path on how you want to remediate with a complete implementation steps tailored to your environment: the console walk-throughs, updated IaC changes for architecture-level recommendations, and <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/cli?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">AWS Command Line Interface (AWS CLI)</a> commands.</li> </ul> <p><strong><u>AWS Well-Architected Agent in action</u></strong><br /> To get started, create an agent profile to define the scope of what Well-Architected Agent can access and provide recommendations on, complete the IAM role setup to access resources, conduct architecture review, and remediate recommendations.</p> <p><strong>Create an agent profile</strong><br /> In the <a href="https://console.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">AWS Well-Architected console</a>, choose <strong>Get started with Well-Architected Agent</strong>. This is a common method of &#8216;prompt injection,&#8217; in which attackers feed specially crafted, deceptive inputs into commercial AI tools that are programmed to avoid outputting malicious code that can be used to exploit computer vulnerabilities,&#8221; the sentencing memo reads.</p> <p>The government told the court it is unaware of evidence that Wagenius figured out how to use or deploy the vulnerabilities he was researching in the BOP&#8217;s systems, and when questioned said he was only researching &#8220;potential vulnerabilities to provide information to the BOP.&#8221;</p> <p>Incredibly, despite the enormous financial value of the data stolen from AT&amp;T and other telecom providers, Wagenius&#8217;s extortion efforts were largely unsuccessful. The government&#8217;s sentencing memo says Wagenius made a whopping total of around $1,500 from selling stolen data.</p> <p>&#8220;While Wagenius was not particularly financially successful as a cybercriminal, he both intended to and caused significant harm to numerous individual victims, U.S. companies, and the U.S. government,&#8221; the memo states.</p>

Man for Man: How Brighton’s Press Suffocated Arsenal

📅 Sep 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

<p>BrBrighton arrived at the match fresh off a 3–2 win at Old Trafford, while Arsenal boasted a clean defensive record and a reputation for controlling games and seeing them out like few other teams in Europe. The match ended 3–0, sparking immense hype around Brighton’s pressing. This analysis explores the underlying mechanisms: their man-oriented pressing across the entire pitch, their asymmetrical build-up play, their transition behavior after winning the ball, and the three goals themselves.</p> <p><span id="more-13672"></span></p> <h2 id="the-basic-idea-intensity-with-control">The Basic Idea: Intensity with Control</h2> <p>Arteta&#8217;s Arsenal are among the best sides in Europe at controlling games out of possession. Any opponent who sets out to establish order is playing on the ground Arsenal own. Hürzeler therefore decided against control – not control surrendered in the sense of a low block, but a chaotic game state that Brighton generated themselves and were therefore prepared for.</p> <div class="wp-caption alignleft" id="attachment_13685" style="width: 338px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/arsenal-grundordnung.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13685" height="506" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/arsenal-grundordnung.png" width="328" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13685">System &amp; movement with the ball &#8211; FC Arsenal</p></div> <div class="wp-caption alignright" id="attachment_13686" style="width: 342px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/brighton-grundordnung.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13686" height="513" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/brighton-grundordnung.png" width="332" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13686">System &amp; movement with the ball &#8211; Brighton &amp; Hove Albion</p></div> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>&nbsp;</p> <p>The thread running through ninety minutes: Arsenal were not to settle into an organised phase either with or without the ball. Three decisions delivered that. The build-up was set up asymmetrically – on the right Brighton prepared the long ball and occupied the landing zone before it was played, on the left counter-movements opened the short route. It was never long instead of short, but two prepared options that would have demanded opposite answers from Arsenal. After regains the ball was not secured but risked immediately, because a secured ball gives the opponent exactly the seconds they need to reorganise. And the press lived off intensity and constantly new starting points: when a line was played through, Brighton did not stand still but re-established their shape and triggered again.</p> <p>All three decisions give up ball control in the classical sense. Control came instead from preparation – if you know where the ball will land and you are standing there beforehand, you control the situation without the ball.</p> <p>What followed can be pinned to a single number: 41 Arsenal turnovers in their own danger zone. That is not a figure a side of this quality produces because they play badly, but because they never reached the game state their build-up is designed for.</p> <h2 id="out-of-possession-man-marking-across-the-whole-pitch">Out of Possession: Man-Marking Across the Whole Pitch</h2> <div class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_13679" style="width: 422px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/mid-block-Sprung-ins-hohe-Pressing.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13679" height="439" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/mid-block-Sprung-ins-hohe-Pressing.png" width="412" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13679">Brightons manorientated press against a 4-3-3</p></div> <p>The press was simple in principle and demanding in execution. Brighton set up with man-reference to their direct opponent and switched into pure man-marking on the trigger. Against a side that tracks from the first second, marking assignments can be pulled apart by movement before any pressure exists; Brighton refused to offer that, because their shape stayed compact until the trigger moment.</p> <div class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_13678" style="width: 624px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/mid-block-mit-Sprung-ins-hohe-Pressing-2.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13678" height="538" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/mid-block-mit-Sprung-ins-hohe-Pressing-2.png" width="614" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13678">In the &#8220;mid-block,&#8221; positioned +1 on the last line, but ready at any moment to transition into a high press upon a pressing trigger.</p></div> <p>The steering began with Ayari. He curved his pressing run so that Konsa stayed in his cover shadow and Raya was forced to the other side. Kostoulas marked Gabriel, while Chema Andrés and Groß pushed aggressively onto Arsenal&#8217;s No. 8s. Groß in particular tracked the dropping Rice and at times advanced into the first pressing line, in some sequences all the way onto Konsa. That requires Rice to be picked up by a team-mate in the same moment – the man-marking worked through handovers as much as through tracking. Centrally, the striker and the No. 10 rotated accordingly: whoever had the shorter route to the next man on the ball pushed out, while the other covered behind him and took over again on the next trigger.</p> <p>It only held up because the centre-backs came with it. Vušković and Struijk stepped out onto Ødegaard and the dropping Havertz and prevented an underload in central areas. When Arsenal built with three in the first line, Brighton kept one centre-back zonal in the back line – but with permanent reference to the unmarked player in the No. 6 space, stepping onto him the moment the press was triggered. Arsenal&#8217;s numerical advantage in build-up therefore never translated into a free man anywhere on the pitch.</p> <p>Raya was part of the marking scheme too. Ayari&#8217;s cover shadow left only the route through a No. 6, and under man-marking that is not an escape but a duel that begins with the first touch. Raya&#8217;s body shape reinforced it: he tended to open only half-way, which effectively removed the switch to the far side from his repertoire. Steered onto his right foot, only the right-hand build-up side remained; the left lay open for long stretches and was unreachable for the one player who could have found it.</p> <p>Every back pass served as a trigger. When the ball went back to Raya or the centre-backs, Brighton pushed up as a unit and raised the defensive line. The density around the ball came less from the base shape than from the recovery runs: players who had been beaten sprinted back, and they did not run at the ball but with a cover shadow onto the lay-off option behind it. An Arsenal player with a marker in his back therefore had neither the lay-off nor the chance to turn.</p> <p>This is precisely where Brighton differed from most sides who get played through against Arsenal. The usual response is to stay deep once a line has been broken and wait for the shape to settle. Brighton instead pushed up into the next line and used the mid-block to prepare the next jump back into the high press – even when that press had just been beaten. The press therefore had not one starting point per possession, but a steady supply of new ones, and Arsenal had to solve the same pressure several times within a single attack.</p> <p>Arsenal&#8217;s core problem was the absence of a threat in behind. Their attempts to play through the press came without pace beyond the back line, so Brighton were never forced to turn towards their own goal. That made the press cheap: no threat in behind, therefore no need to drop the line. There are three answers to committed man-marking – movement that drags your own marker away and opens space for a third player, a dribble that beats the assignment one-on-one, or a target man who wins the long balls. Arsenal produced none of them.</p> <h3 id="the-mid-block-as-a-fallback">The Mid-Block as a Fallback</h3> <p>When the high press was beaten, Brighton dropped into a 5-1-4 and inverted the priority. The five in the last line formed a deliberate overload against Arsenal&#8217;s front line – in exactly the moment a man-oriented side is most vulnerable, because its players are facing forward while the opponents are already moving. Brighton took the depth out of the situation first and only then thought about the ball again.</p> <div class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_13674" style="width: 653px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/mid-block-1-letzte-linie-neu.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13674" height="445" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/mid-block-1-letzte-linie-neu.png" width="643" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13674">A &#8220;mid-block&#8221; in a 5-1-4 formation. The foremost players protected the &#8220;red zone&#8221; by using their cover shadows and shifting extremely compactly. Arsenal was mostly forced to play around the block, repeatedly giving Brighton time to reorganize.</p></div> <p>A single No. 6 held the connection in front, while the four most advanced players stayed narrow and closed the centre with cover shadows rather than pressing the ball carrier. Arsenal found no central switch on the ball-near side and had to play around the block. Every trip around it cost seconds that Brighton used to shift across, so the end of the route offered the same congestion as the start. In some phases six players stood in the last line.</p> <p>The block was not a terminus. At every Arsenal back pass Brighton jumped out again and restarted the high press – the same trigger as in the high phase, only from a deeper starting position. The spare man in the last line made that possible, usually Vušković: because the overload left him without a fixed opponent, he could step out, cover the depth and pick up a man himself a moment later. That freed a midfielder to push forward without leaving an assignment open behind him – precisely the point at which sides with a similar approach usually come apart.</p> <div class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_13673" style="width: 590px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/mid-block-1-2-neu.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13673" height="492" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/mid-block-1-2-neu.png" width="580" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13673">In the &#8220;mid-block&#8221; phase, the &#8220;+1&#8221; players organized themselves to execute early handovers and jump into pressing situations. Here, Vuskovic—acting as the &#8220;+1&#8221; player from the defensive line—steps up to pick up the Arsenal player operating in the number 10 position.</p></div> <p>On top of that came the trap on the touchline. Once Brighton steered the ball wide, players jumped in from every direction: the ball-near winger closed the route back, the No. 6 covered inside, the back line stepped out. The back pass, the switch and the ball in behind did not disappear one after another but at the same time. Take away only the back pass and a side switches play; take away only the switch and they play backwards and start again. When all that remains is an individual action against several opponents with the touchline at your back, it almost always ends with the opposition.</p> <h2 id="transition-risk-over-retain">Transition: Risk Over Retain</h2> <p>After a regain there are two basic options: risk the ball immediately to launch the attack, or secure it to establish order. Hürzeler consistently chose the first, and it follows directly from the overall idea – a secured ball would have given Arsenal exactly the seconds in which their structure reorganises.</p> <div class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_13677" style="width: 525px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/hohes-Pressing-Ruckpass-zu.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13677" height="470" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/hohes-Pressing-Ruckpass-zu.png" width="515" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13677">When a high press was bypassed, Brighton succeeded in forcing Arsenal into congested situations in the area in front of the back four. In doing so, the players who had been bypassed shifted to block the passing lanes of the players positioned behind the ball. This created additional pressure on the ball carrier, who was simultaneously facing pressure from the front via a &#8220;negative&#8221; pressing movement (i.e., pressing towards their own goal).</p></div> <p>The counter was prepared before the regain. When Arsenal found a player in the No. 6 space after beating the press, Brighton immediately closed his back pass, so the turnover inevitably occurred in a moment when Arsenal were all ahead of the ball. The density around the ball, a defensive necessity, meant in attack that Brighton were central and therefore on the shortest route to goal. The first ball went to the deepest player, followed by a dribble at speed into the box, with no intermediate step and no square pass for safety.</p> <h2 id="in-possession-bait-them-in-then-go-over-the-top">In Possession: Bait Them In, Then Go Over the Top</h2> <div class="wp-caption alignleft" id="attachment_13683" style="width: 517px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/Spielaufbau-lang-1.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13683" height="571" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/Spielaufbau-lang-1.png" width="507" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13683">In the build-up phase, Brighton utilized a diamond shape. De Cuyper and Boscagli engaged in a 2v2 duel on the left flank, while Kostoulas, Gomez, and Kadioglu created a high 3v3 situation on the right. Briefly, both center-backs and a defensive midfielder would spread out to widen the formation. Additional support occasionally came from Groß, dropping back from the number 10 position. Ayari occupied Konsa and Gabriel through constant runs into the open spaces ahead of him.</p></div> <p>In deep build-up Brighton baited Arsenal in deliberately. Verbruggen put his foot on the ball while the centre-backs split and lengthened the pressing runs of their opponents. On paper that produced a 3v2 against Arsenal&#8217;s first line – except Hürzeler had no intention of playing through that overload on the ground. 25 of 40 goalkeeper passes were long, 62.5 per cent, and they went overwhelmingly to the right.</p> <div class="wp-caption alignleft" id="attachment_13682" style="width: 517px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/Spielaufbau-lang-2.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13682" height="531" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/Spielaufbau-lang-2.png" width="507" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13682">The structure allowed for a long ball to the right flank, feeding into the 3-3 formation. This created opportunities to contest the second ball or flick on the aerial pass. Additionally, Vuskovic offered a short, low passing option as the free player.</p></div> <p>There, Brighton consistently created a 3v3 – an intact structure for the second ball regardless of whether the first contact was won. That is exactly what separates it from an ordinary long ball: the action was not judged by the aerial duel but by the situation that followed it. Kostoulas dropping added to the disorganisation of Arsenal&#8217;s high line, because a centre-back had to decide whether to follow him between the lines or let him turn. After winning second balls Brighton played on at high speed and looked for an early shot or an early cross, even against a defensive overload – again the decision for tempo over the clean attack.</p> <div class="wp-caption alignleft" id="attachment_13681" style="width: 519px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/Spielaufbau-lang-3.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13681" height="510" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/Spielaufbau-lang-3.png" width="509" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13681">Once again, the 3-3 structure on the right flank. Here, from open play.</p></div> <p>On the left Brighton worked through pinning rather than through length. Boscagli stepping inside dragged Saka into central areas and took him out of the wide zone Arsenal needed him in defensively, while De Cuyper dropped and offered the mid-range diagonal once the assignments had been pulled apart. The asymmetry was a personnel decision: the players for second balls were on the right, the players who could drag markers out of position on the left. Arsenal would have had to condense against one side and hand over assignments cleanly against the other.</p> <div class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_13680" style="width: 519px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/Spielaufbau-flach-links-1.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13680" height="517" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/Spielaufbau-flach-links-1.png" width="509" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13680">The team’s build-up play was asymmetrical, featuring a high, wide approach on the right contrasted with the left flank: Boscagli often played an inverted role—supplementing the existing diamond formation—which opened up space for de Cuyper. Meanwhile, Groß and Kostoulas occupied the players on Arsenal’s right defensive flank, enabling Brighton to find low-ball solutions against the press.</p></div> <h3 id="in-midfield-stretching-the-block">In Midfield: Stretching the Block</h3> <p>With the ball in midfield there were two patterns. The first was the deliberate back pass to Verbruggen to pull Arsenal&#8217;s block out, followed by the same long-short structure as in deep build-up. The back pass was not a safety option here but the tool that created the space behind the pressing line in the first place.</p> <p>The second pattern worked through central and wide tension at high speed, supplemented by rotations within their own structure. Arsenal repeatedly failed to hand over their pressing assignments in a coordinated way against it – the same weakness Brighton had solved cleanly in their own press. In possession Brighton&#8217;s shape approached a 4-3-3: Chema Andrés as the deepest No. 6, Groß with freedom from the left half-space, Kadıoğlu pushing up early to support Gómez, Boscagli starting slightly deeper on the left.</p> <h2 id="the-goals-as-proof-of-the-idea">The Goals as Proof of the Idea</h2> <p>The 1-0 by Groß in the 31st minute is the whole plan in one sequence. Brighton play back to the goalkeeper from midfield, go long, lose the first contact and win the second ball. After a phase of circulation the next attack meets a back line that has already been destabilised: Kostoulas drops, Arsenal&#8217;s midfield and Konsa step out, and Kostoulas lays the ball back against the direction of the pressure to Groß. Because Ayari runs in behind at the same time, Arsenal&#8217;s defenders drop – and Groß has the time for the shot from around 20 metres. In the build-up Brighton had used rotations to drag Guimarães over to the left.</p> <div class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_13675" style="width: 567px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/1-0.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13675" height="447" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/1-0.png" width="557" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13675">The opening goal stems from a positional attack. Kostoulas spots open space and makes a run into it. With a pass played across his standing leg, he feeds the ball to Groß in the space between the lines. As Groß takes a few quick touches and dribbles toward the defensive line, Gabriel and Calafiori drop back together, thereby opening up a shooting lane.</p></div> <p>The 2-0 by Kostoulas shortly before half-time follows the same logic from the opposite direction. After a set piece of their own, Brighton clear an Arsenal counter and transition again immediately, 4v5 in Arsenal&#8217;s half, and once more the shot comes from distance while the centre-backs drop off.</p> <div class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_13676" style="width: 576px;"><a href="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/2-0.png"><img alt="" class="wp-image-13676" height="338" src="https://spielverlagerung.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/09/2-0.png" width="566" /></a><p class="wp-caption-text" id="caption-attachment-13676">Much like the goal that made it 1–0, both defenders went to ground near the ball carrier, thereby opening up space for the goalscorer—this time arising from a transition situation.</p></div> <p>Both goals share the same cause: Arsenal&#8217;s centre-backs stepped out too late to press the man between the lines. React only when the shooting motion starts and you cannot get into the block from 20 to 25 metres. What is striking is that this passivity is the same one Arsenal showed in their own build-up – a side that does not leave its position to offer itself does not leave it to close down a shooter either.</p> <p>The 3-0 by Chema Andrés came from a corner: Groß delivered from the left, Brighton had created an overload at the far post and protected it with two blocks, and Chema rose completely unmarked to head in off the inside of the post.</p> <h2 id="second-half-no-game-management">Second Half: No Game Management</h2> <p>Two goals up, many sides would have defended deeper. Brighton came out with unchanged and at times higher pressing intensity, and the coordination was visibly drilled: marking assignments were swapped cleanly even in dynamic situations, and Arsenal&#8217;s structural adjustments were absorbed.</p> <p>Arsenal had managed a few organised possession phases in Brighton&#8217;s half in the first period; in the second it became harder. In those phases they built with a back three, Rice as the third centre-back, Ødegaard dropping alongside Guimarães and the full-backs occupying the half-spaces. Arteta reacted around the hour: Zubimendi and Merino for Timber and Guimarães, with Rice moving to right-back, followed shortly by Gyökeres and Eze for Ødegaard and Tzolis. There was no noticeable effect, because the problem did not lie in the shape but in the assignments in front of it.</p> <p>There was also a layer that usually goes missing in analysis: Brighton used tactical fouls deliberately to stop Arsenal finding a rhythm. Arteta admitted afterwards that the game had been “very difficult to control”, pointing to lost first and second balls and to Brighton&#8217;s directness.</p> <h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2> <p>Arteta&#8217;s Arsenal being outplayed this clearly is rare. That it happened proactively and aggressively is rarer still – most sides who take something from Arsenal do it with a low block and efficiency. Hürzeler deliberately stepped back afterwards and praised his players&#8217; courage, which gets to the heart of it: the plan only works if eleven players are willing to carry it through in every single sequence.</p> <p>The interesting question is where the idea came from. Chelsea were a common opponent: Brighton had 74 per cent possession there and lost 3-4 against a counter-attacking side – control right down to the numbers, without return. Alonso&#8217;s Chelsea tried the opposite route against Arsenal, a deliberate surrender of control, also without success. The likely derivation lies between the two: neither give up control nor chase it, but generate the unsettled game state yourself and be prepared for it.</p> <p>There is no reason for panic at Arsenal, but Arteta will need answers against a high press. The build-up was predictable for long stretches, and above all the direct option with pace in behind was missing as a threat. As long as that does not exist, a man-oriented press costs very little against this side.</p> <p>For Brighton the open question is repeatability. Man-marking of this commitment, collective recovery runs with cover shadows and a touchline trap that requires everyone to trigger at once are hard to produce twice in an English week. Whether the plan works was answered on this afternoon. How often it can be produced will decide the season.</p> <p>LC can often be found in the classroom, preferably on the soccer field, and—on rare occasions—on <em><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/lukas-conrad-33a693273?lipi=urn%3Ali%3Apage%3Ad_flagship3_profile_view_base_contact_details%3BQUoXlM6NRcCK1Rc5%2F76FTg%3D%3D">LinkedIn</a></em>.</p>

Stop Treating CSS Container Queries Like Traditional Media Queries

📅 Sep 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

<p>To be completely honest with you, I missed the news when CSS Container Queries first shipped. And when I finally heard about it, my very first thought was, <em>“Why exactly do I need this when media queries already exist?”</em></p> <p>I’m not proud of that reaction, knowing what I know now, but it was comforting to know that I wasn’t alone. In fact, there are legions of us out there.</p> <p>What baffles me is that container queries aren’t a new feature, as it currently sits at around <a href="https://caniuse.com/css-container-queries">94% browser support</a>. And yet, very few people are actually using it. According to the <a href="https://2025.stateofcss.com/en-US/features/">State of CSS</a> survey, 86% of developers are aware of container queries, but only 41.4% actually use them. Surveys can be biased and not completely representative of our entire field, but this one is certainly the best indicator we’ve got.</p> <p><a href="http://youtu.be/4IBXIFe2CKA?si=2g3869tmp0yLOYz_&amp;t=297">Kevin Powell also talked about this</a> at SmashingConf Amsterdam 2026: <strong>Container Queries adoption has been terrible.</strong> And that is so strange to me, knowing that the ability for components to adapt to the size of their outer container <a href="https://css-tricks.com/2019-css-wishlist/">has been at the top of so many CSS wishlists</a> over the years.</p> <p>I’m not particularly interested in how many people are using container queries as much as in <em>how</em> they are using them. I can’t account for everyone, but from what I’ve seen — including in my own early attempts — many of us are using them <em>wrong</em>.</p> <p>The bottom line is that incorrect use comes down to the same impression I had when learning about them: <strong>they absolutely look just like media queries at first glance</strong>. And since they look similar, it’s easy to assume they serve similar purposes and work the same way.</p> <p>They don’t.</p> <p><strong>Note</strong>: I should state up front that what I’m focusing on in this article is using container <em>size</em> queries, i.e., a responsive design technique for responding to the size of a particular container. There are also container <em>style</em> queries that respond to a container’s computed styles (and are experimental at the time of this writing). You can catch up on those in <a href="https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2024/06/what-are-css-container-style-queries-good-for/">Juan Diego’s piece here on Smashing Magazine</a> where he examines their possible use cases.</p> Media Queries Look Outward <p>The viewport is a proxy. It always has been. Media queries are what gave us the illusion that screen width alone is responsible for how responsive apps adapt to their environment.</p> <p>Ask yourself this: When you write <code>@media</code>, what are you asking the browser?</p> <pre><code>@media (min-width: 1024px) { .card { display: flex; } </code></pre> <p>I, like most developers, am asking the browser: <em>How wide is the screen right now?</em> That’s it.</p> <p>Media queries answer that beautifully, but what happens when this <code>.card</code> component is placed in a grid cell that’s <code>300px</code> wide on a <code>1920px</code> desktop screen?</p> <p>The media query doesn’t care; it does its job. The viewport is still <code>1920px</code>, so <code>min-width: 1024px</code> fires and the matched query styles are applied, even though the card only has <code>300px</code> of space to work with. Eventually, everything in the card deforms, overflows, or cramps up.</p> <blockquote>“Media queries are dumb. Not dumb in terms of the concept, but dumb in that they don’t know very much. In fact, most people assume that they know more than they do.”<br /><br />— <a href="https://css-tricks.com/smart-layouts-with-container-queries/#:~:text=Because%20of%20how,than%20they%20do.">Kevin Powell</a></blockquote> <p>It’s common to think of responsive design purely as a system for updating complete page layouts, like going from two columns on a large screen to a single column on a small screen.</p> Container Queries Look Inward <p>Container queries are smarter than that. They make responsive layouts more reliant on what’s happening <strong>inside</strong> a component rather than on the outer context that has no insight into a component’s contents. It is more like: <em>“How much space is available for me in this specific spot, right now?”</em></p> <p>Here is the same card code example we looked at in the last section, but with a container query:</p> <pre><code>.card-wrapper { container-name: card; container-type: inline-size; } @container card (min-width: 450px) { .card { display: flex; flex-direction: row; } } </code></pre> <p>This changes everything. The card isn’t influenced by the viewport; its only concern is whether the <code>.card</code> component’s parent wrapper has at least <code>450px</code> of inline (i.e., horizontal in a left-to-right writing mode) space. If that condition is true, the component goes horizontal; if not, it goes to its default <code>block</code> display.</p> <p><img src="https://files.smashing.media/articles/stop-treating-css-container-queries-traditional-media-queries/1-normal-state-vs-wrapped-state.jpg" /></p> <p>The logic works like this:</p> <ol> <li>When there’s enough room, both items (<code>.flex-item</code>) sit side-by-side, each exactly half the parent container’s width.</li> <li>When there is limited space, the second item wraps to the next line.</li> <li>Because <code>flex-grow</code> is active on each item, the wrapped items stretch to fill most of the parent’s width.</li> <li>If the item is a container itself, it detects the sudden width expansion and fires.</li> </ol> <pre><code>/* The flex parent */ .flex-layout { display: flex; flex-wrap: wrap; } /* Register a flex item as a container */ .flex-item { container-type: inline-size; flex: 1 1 390px; /* Grow to fill space, wrap at 390px */ } /* Default Card Styles (narrow / side-by-side) */ .card { display: flex; flex-direction: column; background: #f4f4f4; } /* Once there's enough room for a full row */ @container (min-width: 600px) { .card { flex-direction: row; align-items: center; background: #e2f0d9; } } </code></pre> <p>This works. As the parent size shrinks and the cards wrap to two lines, the card item expands, the container query fires, and applies the necessary styles. </p> <p><img src="https://files.smashing.media/articles/stop-treating-css-container-queries-traditional-media-queries/2-reusable-component.jpg" /></p> Conclusion <p>At the end of the day, the core reason why container queries look incredibly similar to media queries is simply familiarity. They’re not exactly “new”, but they are way less understood and adopted than media queries. But media queries have plenty of their own limitations; otherwise, we wouldn’t need container queries to fill those gaps.</p> <p>What we have is a more effective feature for detecting when a specific component’s context changes and a means for adjusting styles based on its content, as it should be when that component can exist in multiple contexts.</p>

Building A UX ROI Case That Survives The Boardroom

📅 Sep 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p>Sooner or later, a CFO looks at your wireframes and asks what any of it actually <em>does</em> for the bottom line. Storyboards don’t answer that question, and the era when a 5-minute pitch could answer it ended, unfortunately, a while ago.</p> <p>These days, <a href="https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2021/12/tools-tips-resources-to-build-better-user-experiences/">if you want to win budget, buy-in, and backing for UX</a>, the design has to be provably good for the business, not only for the people using it.</p> <p>And proving that takes more than taping a dollar sign to a redesign. You have to understand how your organization defines value in the first place, how it measures that value, and how a credible line gets drawn between a design initiative and an outcome leadership already cares about.</p> <p>Rather than scatter tips, this article follows one worked example the whole way through. Meridian is a mid-size B2B SaaS company, and it is entirely made up — that label matters, so it gets repeated where it counts. Its onboarding redesign carries the same figures from <strong>goal-setting</strong> through <strong>cost accounting</strong>, <strong>causal testing</strong>, and the final <strong>ROI number</strong>, because a framework only becomes tangible when the numbers connect. Every step is one you can rerun inside your own organization.</p> Why ROI Matters More Than Ever in UX Conversations <p>Companies now want clarity on what every dollar buys, and “delightful user experiences” stopped clearing that bar some time ago. I still remember a former colleague celebrating a $1 million redesign he’d gotten greenlit mostly on the strength of a couple of three.js tricks. Try that pitch in front of a finance team today and see how far the particles get you.</p> <p><a href="https://uxplaybook.org/articles/10-ways-ux-managers-lose-credibility">Executives don’t hate UX, they just hate vagueness</a>. A pitch built on “users will find it easier” loses, every time, to the department promising 12% more sales in Q3. The difference is the one between <a href="https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/05/sunuva-case-study-ux-changes-result-increase-conversion/">a streamlined checkout flow that reduced cart abandonment</a> with completed purchases up 22%, and the same work rewarded with “the QA testers like it.” One of those goes on your resume. The rest of this article is about earning the first version, with Meridian’s numbers doing the work.</p> When Business Goals And KPIs Don’t Exist Yet <p>Most writing about UX ROI makes a convenient assumption: that the organization <strong>already</strong> owns clean business goals and KPIs for you to hook your work onto. Real companies are messier than that. Plenty run on ambitions like “grow faster” or “<a href="https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2015/01/all-about-customer-journey-mapping/">improve the customer journey</a>” that nobody ever broke into anything measurable, and an ROI case built on that ambiguity sounds impressive right up until somebody scrutinizes it.</p> <p>So the first job is often to <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/calculating-roi-design-projects/">help the organization define what success even looks like</a>. Interview stakeholders across departments — what does product consider a good quarter, where does customer success watch users struggle, where do sales deals stall — and listen for the themes that keep resurfacing across conversations, because those recurring themes are the company’s latent business objectives. A useful forcing function <a href="https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/set-goals-with-okrs">is the OKR model (Objectives and Key Results)</a>, which doesn’t tolerate vagueness.</p> <p>At Meridian, the stated ambition was <em>“improve the rate of new users’ adoption of the platform,”</em> which you can neither design toward nor measure against. Interviews turned up the real shape of the problem. Trial users needed a median of 14 days to reach first value, most churned before getting there, and onboarding questions were burying the support queue. Out of that came an OKR with actual edges: reduce median time-to-first-value from 14 days to 7 with the use of a guided setup flow, and lift trial-to-paid conversion from 8% to 9.5%.</p> <p><strong>One warning about formalizing KPIs</strong>: Impose them from inside the UX team and leadership will suspect you’ve rigged the field in your own favor, so co-create them with whoever owns the outcome — though never at the price of accepting targets that set your team up for an uncomfortable situation. Meridian’s head of product agreed that setup-completion rate was a fair proxy for onboarding usability, and customer success signed off on time-to-first-value, a number already sitting on their own dashboard.</p> <blockquote>A KPI ladder that ends at a metric somebody already watches buys you credibility before any design work starts.</blockquote> Quantifying The Full Cost Of The Investment <p>ROI has a <strong>denominator</strong>, and the denominator is where most UX teams go wrong. You can’t calculate a return <a href="https://www.farseer.com/blog/strategic-financial-planning/">without strategic financial planning</a>, yet cost usually gets counted as designer salaries or consulting hours and nothing else. A finance team will find the rest whether or not you counted it, so <strong>count it first</strong>.</p> <p>Direct costs are the visible ones. Meridian’s redesign ran $45,000 in design and research labor plus another $8,000 in tooling and participant incentives. <a href="https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2025/04/anima-playground-figma-designs-live-apps/">Licenses for Figma</a>, UserTesting, Hotjar, analytics platforms, research incentive spend — all of it belongs in the total, and that’s before the <a href="https://cast.ai/blog/vendor-lock-in-and-how-to-break-free/">inevitable instances of vendor lock-in</a> every UX team eventually faces. Engineering sits in the same column, because <a href="https://uxmastery.com/the-biggest-mistake-new-ux-designers-make/">a UX redesign doesn’t stop at the mockup</a>. Building the guided setup took two frontend sprints plus a QA pass, $38,000 worth, and the project generated about $4,000 of coordination overhead along the way in new syncs and shared dashboards.</p> <p>The line item nearly everyone misses, and the one worth stealing from this article if you steal nothing else, is <strong>stakeholder time</strong>. Workshops, design reviews, and feedback sessions all pull senior people away from their primary work. A VP of Product spending four hours a week in UX reviews is a VP not spending those hours on roadmap planning or partner negotiations. Log the attendance — who came, for how long, at what seniority — and price it at fully loaded cost, meaning salary plus benefits divided by productive hours. A quarter’s worth of workshops, reviews, and interviews at Meridian priced out at $22,000.</p> <p>Add it all up: $45,000 in design labor, $8,000 in tooling, $38,000 in engineering, $22,000 in stakeholder time, $4,000 in coordination. The investment is $117,000. Saying that number out loud beats saying “we spent $45K on design,” precisely because it already includes everything a finance team would have dug up on its own.</p> Proving Causation, Not Just Correlation <p>Most UX ROI pitches die right here. Conversions rose after the redesign, sure — and the CFO wants to know how you ruled out the new pricing, the seasonal traffic bump, and the marketing campaign that shipped the same week. Without a convincing answer, <a href="https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/09/formula-roi-design-system/">your entire ROI story crumbles</a>.</p> <p>After all these years, <a href="https://hbr.org/2017/06/a-refresher-on-ab-testing">the gold standard for proving causation is still A/B testing</a>: run the old experience against the new one on an even traffic split until the sample means something.</p> <p>Onboarding happens to suit a phased rollout, which is why Meridian could do this cleanly. For eight weeks, half of new trial signups received the redesigned guided setup while half stayed on the legacy flow. Control converted to paid at 8.0%. The variant came in at 9.4%. With roughly 6,100 trials inside the window, the difference was statistically significant, but a 1.4-point gap on a single test is still the kind of result that deserves a second look before anyone builds a budget on it, which is one reason the team held back on attribution below. Where a split isn’t feasible — a change too structural, a user base too small — fall back to a <strong>time series</strong> instead. Measure steadily for weeks before the change, implement, then keep measuring against the baseline you established.</p> <p>Documenting whatever else happens <a href="https://medium.com/@divinestocks/how-to-document-frequent-design-changes-in-ux-projects-without-losing-control-93be5d70c9ed">around the same time as your UX change</a> is the unglamorous half of causation.</p> <p>A pricing-page test from Meridian’s marketing team overlapped weeks five through eight of the rollout. The UX team noted it, confirmed it hit both cohorts evenly, and still chose to attribute only 70% of the observed lift to the redesign in the final math. There is no formula that produces that number; treat it as an illustrative assumption for this example.</p> <p>The team asked how much of the lift could plausibly belong to the pricing test if it had helped one cohort slightly more than the other, settled on a ceiling of about a third, and rounded the redesign’s share down to 70%. Your figure will differ. What matters is that it is written down and argued for before the results arrive, not fitted to them afterwards. That restraint is worth money in a skeptical room. <em>“We attribute roughly 70% of the lift to the onboarding change, with the remainder likely influenced by concurrent pricing work”</em> survives cross-examination; claiming everything does not. Cohort analysis then backed the number up, since the lift held across acquisition channels and tenure bands, and at that point the skeptics had very little left to work with.</p> <p>Leading and lagging indicators belong on the same slide, because each covers the other’s weakness. Meridian’s leading indicators moved first — setup completion climbed from 62% to 89%, median time-to-first-value dropped from 14 days to 6.5 — and the lagging trial-to-paid number followed. Mechanism first, business outcome second. Presented together, they form a <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-metrics/">causal chain that’s harder to poke holes in</a> than either one alone.</p> The ROI Calculation, End to End <p>So what did Meridian actually earn? The company sees about 40,000 trial signups a year. Lifting conversion from 8.0% to 9.4% adds roughly 560 paying customers annually, and at an average of $1,800 in annual recurring revenue per account, those customers represent about $1,008,000 in new ARR. Applying the conservative 70% attribution from the causal work trims the defensible figure to roughly $706,000.</p> <p>Set that against the full $117,000 investment and the first-year ROI lands near 5:1, with payback arriving in roughly two months. There’s a second line, too. Onboarding-related support tickets dropped about 30%, some 3,600 fewer tickets a year, worth another $54,000 annually at $15 per resolved ticket. Keep it as its own line rather than folding it into one swollen headline number. The case reads as more honest that way and loses none of its force.</p> <p>Three assumptions carry that result, and each belongs on the slide next to it. The 40,000 signups and the $1,800 average ARR are the prior year’s actuals held flat, so a growth or pricing change moves the outcome in either direction. The 70% attribution is the illustrative assumption from the causal work, not a measured quantity. And the two-month payback counts new ARR as it lands rather than revenue recognized net of churn, which flatters the timeline; on a net basis the payback stretches to roughly a quarter. State those three plainly and a finance team can adapt the example to its own numbers. Hide them and the whole thing starts to look like marketing math, however careful the experiment was.</p> <p>What persuades in the final presentation is not sophistication. Open with the baseline: what stalled trials and support volume were already costing. Show the delta in terms leadership reads fluently, <a href="https://useinsider.com/glossary/conversion-rate-uplift/">metrics like conversion rate uplift</a> chief among them. A chart of setup completion climbing from 62% to 89% will beat a paragraph of UX jargon, and a translation like <em>“each abandoned setup costs us 0.3 support tickets”</em> beats the chart. Above all, keep every figure identical from the first slide to the last. A room full of finance people forgives many things, but never numbers that wobble between slides.</p> Tailoring The Case To Whoever Holds The Purse Strings <p>Budget decisions come out of coalitions. A CFO may hold the final say when <a href="https://omniga.ai/blog/finance-os/systems-security/ai-finance-tech-stack-startups-2025">adding AI to the checkout process</a>, but marketing, product, and customer success all lean on that decision, and <strong>each means something different by “value.”</strong></p> <p>A CFO hears cost, revenue, and risk. A CMO hears conversion and acquisition cost, since <a href="https://www.coremountainmedia.com/insights/ux-marketing-roi">UX is a lever for increasing marketing ROI</a>. Product counts support tickets; customer success thinks in retention. The underlying numbers never change; only the framing rotates, and <a href="https://tgg-accounting.com/how-to-build-cfo-dashboard/">a CFO wants a projection, not a moodboard</a>. Meridian’s CFO slide read <em>“the onboarding redesign protects roughly $706,000 in new ARR a year against a $117,000 investment,”</em> while the CMO version led with what a 9.4% trial conversion does to blended acquisition cost.</p> Beyond the Dollar Sign: Qualitative and Non-Financial Metrics <p>Some UX outcomes never translate cleanly into revenue, and pretending they do weakens the parts of your case that are solid. </p> <blockquote>The trick with qualitative evidence is collecting it rigorously enough that nobody can wave it off as anecdote.</blockquote> <p>Scores like <a href="https://www.qualtrics.com/experience-management/customer/net-promoter-score/">Net Promoter Score (NPS)</a>, CSAT, and Customer Effort Score already sit inside most reporting cadences, which makes them cheap to borrow. Tie your work to their movement, and segment wherever the data allows.</p> <p>Meridian could say that NPS among trial users on the redesigned onboarding was 51 against 34 for the legacy flow, which lands far harder than any blended average. Verbatim feedback from surveys, support transcripts, and app store reviews adds the emotional weight the scores lack. Internal tools deserve the same discipline, <a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/davidarmano/2021/05/13/why-employee-experience-is-the-new-customer-experience-five-factors-driving-change-at-work/">since employee experience is increasingly recognized as a business driver</a> — a dashboard redesign that hands account managers 45 minutes a day back is a productivity gain, a satisfaction gain, and a retention lever all in one.</p> <p>Brand perception resists direct measurement but leaves tracks in repeat visits, organic referrals, and social sentiment. It carries extra weight <a href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07359683.2025.2564584">in trust-sensitive industries like finance or healthcare</a>, and it forms fast, given that <a href="https://www.hostinger.com/tutorials/web-design-statistics">UX design influences the first impressions of a whopping 94% of customers</a>.</p> <p>Whatever you collect, <strong>systematize the collecting</strong>. Run pre- and post-surveys with consistent question sets, <a href="https://www.usability.gov/how-to-and-tools/methods/usability-testing.html">use structured usability testing</a> with task-based scoring, and put the qualitative right next to the quantitative when you present.</p> <blockquote>“Setup completion rose from 62% to 89%, and in post-test interviews 8 of 10 participants called the new flow intuitive, against 3 of 10 for the old one” — a pairing like that is much harder to dismiss than either half on its own.</blockquote> Making the Case Stick <p><a href="https://www.userinterviews.com/blog/making-ux-research-essential-to-your-companys-success">UX loses the budget battle unless it’s mapped to company-wide objectives</a>, so phrase the proposal in the words of this year’s board presentation.</p> <p>Nobody at Meridian pitched “simplify the onboarding UI”; the pitch was a redesigned trial experience worth 1.4 points of upgrade rate, roughly $1M in annual recurring revenue before attribution. Bring evidence in both registers, since <a href="https://www.agilitypr.com/pr-news/public-relations/9-ways-to-use-social-proof-to-increase-your-conversions/">that is what social proof is for</a>: the case, clearly labeled, plus screenshots, impact graphs, and user quotes. Find <a href="https://mimischeibe.com/storytelling-ux-design-leadership/">internal allies who can repeat the ROI narrative</a> in rooms you’ll never enter, and write the playbook down, because <strong>repeatable ROI is what earns recurring investment</strong>.</p> Resources for Going Deeper <p>This topic has been explored extensively by researchers, practitioners, and consultancies. Here’s a curated set of resources worth studying if you want to build a stronger ROI practice around UX.</p> <ul> <li><a href="https://www.elsevier.com/books/measuring-the-user-experience/tullis/978-0-12-415781-1">“Measuring the User Experience” by Tom Tullis and Bill Albert</a> is the definitive guide to UX metrics. It covers everything from task-based measurements to survey design to statistical analysis, and it’s written for practitioners, not academics.</li> <li><a href="https://yourstory.com/2026/02/300-million-button-checkout-behaviour">Jared Spool’s “The $300 Million Button” case study</a> is a classic example of how a single UX change (removing a mandatory registration step) generated massive revenue uplift. It’s a story every UX professional should have in their back pocket.</li> <li><a href="https://www.forrester.com/report/the-six-steps-for-justifying-better-ux/RES117708">Forrester’s research on UX ROI</a> provides enterprise-focused frameworks for building business cases around experience design, including their widely cited finding that every dollar invested in UX returns $100.</li> <li><a href="https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/ux-strategy-2nd/9781492052425/">“UX Strategy” by Jaime Levy</a> bridges the gap between design thinking and business strategy, offering practical tools for aligning UX initiatives with organizational goals and market positioning.</li> <li><a href="https://www.dmi.org/page/DesignValue">The Design Value Index by the Design Management Institute</a> tracks publicly traded companies that invest heavily in design against the S&amp;P 500. The data consistently shows that design-led companies outperform the index by significant margins, and it’s a powerful data point for executive presentations.</li> <li><a href="https://research.google/pubs/pub36299/">Google’s HEART framework</a> provides a structured approach to selecting UX metrics at scale. HEART stands for Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, and Task success, and it’s particularly useful for teams that struggle to decide which metrics to track.</li> </ul> Conclusion <p>A seat at the table never comes from beauty or novelty. It comes from <strong>measurable, defensible impact</strong>, which means UX leaders have to trade the artist’s posture for the strategist’s. Speak in outcomes rather than outputs. Connect pixels to profit.</p> <p>When someone challenges the numbers, don’t flinch. Show the controlled experiment, the cohort analysis, and the before-and-after metrics, every figure holding steady from the first slide to the last, the way Meridian’s did, with the customer quotes and the employee-satisfaction data sitting right beside the revenue impact. Prove the work does more than delight users, and be ready to defend the ratio line by line. That’s when the CFO leans in, and that’s when design stops being optional.</p>

The Death Of The Button: Why The Best Interface Is No Interface

📅 Sep 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p>Ever since the commercialisation of the Graphical User Interface pioneered by systems like the <a href="https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/50007#:~:text=Xerox%20Star,today%20have%20become%20commonplace%20in">Xerox Star</a> and popularised by the original <a href="https://www.apple.com/mac/">Apple Macintosh</a>, software has relied heavily on point-and-click interactions. If you wanted to book a trip, buy a pair of shoes, or research a health symptom, you were expected to navigate a labyrinth of user interfaces. You click menus, adjust range sliders, fill out multi-step forms, deal with cookie pop-ups, and open dozens of browser tabs just to cross-reference basic information. We have become accustomed to spending more time <em>managing the software</em> rather than actually achieving our goals.</p> <p>That contract is officially changing. Driven by advances in artificial intelligence and large language models, a new generation of web tools is pioneering a radical philosophy: <strong>Intent-Driven Design</strong>. Rather than expecting our users to learn complex menus and click through elaborate sales funnels, these platforms operate by capturing high-level human goals and silently executing the grunt work in the background.</p> <p>This vision builds on long-standing HCI concepts like <a href="https://www.nointerface.com/">Golden Krishna’s “The Best Interface Is No Interface”</a> and <a href="https://www.jnd.org/">Don Norman’s principles of human-centered design</a>.</p> <p>The ultimate goal of modern web design is no longer to build prettier buttons or flashy animations; it is to eliminate the interface entirely.</p> <p>Well, at least what we understand based on our current experience. </p> <p>It is essential for UX designers to understand the changes we’re witnessing and even <strong>re-evaluate our role</strong>, shifting our focus from <strong>designing visible interfaces</strong> to guiding transparent, <strong>intent-driven AI experiences</strong>. </p> The Death Of The “10-click” Process <p>To understand where web design is going, we first have to look at the friction we have accepted as “normal” for decades.</p> <p>Consider the traditional workflow of buying a flight online. The user experience is intentionally hyper-interactive:</p> <ol> <li>Navigate to a travel aggregator or airline website.</li> <li>Select “Round Trip” from a dropdown menu.</li> <li>Type the origin city and wait for auto-complete.</li> <li>Type the destination city and wait for auto-complete.</li> <li>Click a calendar modal, toggle through months, and select departure and return dates.</li> <li>Choose the number of passengers and cabin class.</li> <li>Click “Search” and wait for the results page to load.</li> <li>Filter by price, layover duration, departure time, and airline.</li> <li>Sort the results and scan through dozens of individual options.</li> <li>Click through a three-page checkout funnel dodging upsells for rental cars and travel insurance.</li> </ol> <p>This is a classic <strong>point-and-click UI paradigm</strong>. The computer acts as a passive container of data, and the human acts as the orchestrator, manually inputting parameters, interpreting raw outputs, and executing each micro-step along the way.</p> <p>Intent-driven design flips this dynamic entirely. Instead of forcing you to navigate the mechanical steps of <em>how</em> to find a flight, the interface asks a simple question: <strong><em>What are you trying to accomplish?</em></strong></p> <p>When you express a goal, such as <em>“Find me a non-stop flight to Chicago next weekend under $300 that arrives before 5 PM”</em>, the software reads your intent, executes the multi-step search query behind the scenes, compares the options, and presents a single actionable resolution. The ten clicks dissolve into one clear intent outcome.</p> <p><img src="https://files.smashing.media/articles/death-button-why-best-interface-is-no-interface/1-10-click-process-vs-ai-driven-intent-flow.png" /></p> Three Web Platform Examples Replacing Buttons With Intent <p>This shift isn’t a theoretical vision of the distant future. Some of the world’s most accessible websites allow you to experience intent-capturing systems already.</p> <h3>1. Perplexity AI: Search Without The Open Tabs</h3> <p>Traditional search engines like <a href="https://www.google.com/">Google</a> were built as directories. You typed a keyword, and the interface rewarded you with ten blue links. The actual work of opening five tabs, skimming long articles, dodging ads, and manually assembling an answer was left to you. Although this is now changing, with AI content appearing first when you search on Google.</p> <p><a href="https://www.perplexity.ai/">Perplexity AI</a> allows anyone to perform instant intent-based searches. Instead of forcing you to click into multiple tabs, the interface synthesises the perfect result for your context.</p> <ul> <li><strong>The interaction:</strong> You ask a complex question in plain English: <em>“Compare the top three budget laptops for a computer science student, focusing on battery life and keyboard quality.”</em></li> <li><strong>The invisible work:</strong> In under two seconds, the platform searches the web, reads dozens of articles, evaluates hardware specifications, and cross-references user reviews.</li> </ul> <p><img src="https://files.smashing.media/articles/death-button-why-best-interface-is-no-interface/2-perplexity.png" /></p> <ul> <li><strong>The result:</strong> (See screen above) Instead of throwing web pages at you, Perplexity dynamically generates a single custom answer complete with formatted comparison tables, pros and cons lists, and concise inline citations. You get the outcome of thirty clicks in just one.</li> </ul> <p><img src="https://files.smashing.media/articles/death-button-why-best-interface-is-no-interface/3-synthesized-answer-output-bypassing-traditional-link-tab.png" /></p> <h3>2. Vercel v0: Web Design Without Dragging And Dropping</h3> <p>For years, creating a website meant using visual builders like <a href="https://www.figma.com/">Figma</a> or drag-and-drop web editors. As designers, we spent hours manually drawing rectangles, picking hexadecimal colour codes, adjusting padding values, and sometimes writing CSS code.</p> <p><a href="https://v0.dev/">Vercel v0</a> lets anyone test its generative interface builder right from the home page without signing in.</p> <ul> <li><strong>The Interaction:</strong> A user types a descriptive goal into the input prompt: <em>“Create a modern dark-mode dashboard for a subscription SaaS app showing monthly revenue, active users, and a customer churn chart.”</em></li> <li><strong>The invisible work:</strong> The underlying AI understands layout principles, accessibility guidelines, UI component libraries, and responsiveness. It writes clean HTML, Tailwind CSS, and React code in real time.</li> <li><strong>The result:</strong> Rather than spending an afternoon manipulating elements, the user receives a fully functional, pixel-perfect user interface in seconds! The traditional visual design tool disappears, replaced by raw human intent.</li> </ul> <p><img src="https://files.smashing.media/articles/death-button-why-best-interface-is-no-interface/4-saas-dashboard-ui.png" /></p> <h3>3. Goblin.tools: Breaking Down Overwhelming Tasks</h3> <p>Many traditional productivity tools require you to manually construct task lists, drag items across Kanban boards, and assign micro-deadlines. <a href="https://goblin.tools/">Goblin.tools</a> is a free, single-page suite of single-task AI tools designed specifically for neurodivergent users or anyone feeling overwhelmed, requiring zero sign-ups or configuration.</p> <p><img src="https://files.smashing.media/articles/death-button-why-best-interface-is-no-interface/5-goblin-tools-dashboard-800px.png" /></p> <ul> <li><strong>The interaction:</strong> You enter a vague, intimidating goal into the <strong>Magic Todo</strong> tool, such as <em>“Prepare for a job interview”</em>.</li> <li><strong>The invisible work:</strong> Instead of forcing you to plan every step, the system analyses the cognitive load of the goal and automatically breaks it down into small, actionable sub-tasks adjusted to your preferred level of detail.</li> <li><strong>The result:</strong> The interface eliminates the stressful task-planning phase and presents a clean checklist tailored to your exact goal.</li> </ul> <p><img src="https://files.smashing.media/articles/death-button-why-best-interface-is-no-interface/6-checklist-job-interview.png" /></p> The Core Pillars Of Intent-driven Web Design <p>When you remove traditional menus, sidebars, and forms, how do you keep a website usable? <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-paradigm/">Designers pioneering this space</a> rely on three core pillars:</p> <table> <thead> <tr> <th>Pillar</th> <th>Traditional UX approach</th> <th>Intent-driven UX approach</th> </tr> </thead> <tbody> <tr> <td><strong>User input</strong></td> <td>Micro-actions (Clicks, dropdowns, toggles)</td> <td>1. High-level goals (Natural language, context, habits)</td> </tr> <tr> <td><strong>Interface state</strong></td> <td>Static layouts (Everyone sees the same page)</td> <td>2. Generative UI (Layouts created dynamically on the fly)</td> </tr> <tr> <td><strong>Task execution</strong></td> <td>Manual execution by the user</td> <td>3. Autonomous execution by background AI agents</td> </tr> </tbody> </table> <h3>1. High-level Goals</h3> <p>An invisible interface doesn’t always wait for you to type a command — it leverages context to anticipate user needs. Guided by industry standards like <a href="https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/">Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines on Contextual &amp; Ambient Design</a>, modern systems read environmental metadata, such as device state, location, and past interaction patterns, to trigger proactive actions. </p> <p>For example:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Time and location:</strong> A food delivery web app prioritising your saved home address and dinner items automatically at 6:30 PM on a weekday.</li> <li><strong>Cross-app history:</strong> A calendar application noticing an email about an upcoming appointment and surfacing a one-click <em>“Add to Schedule”</em> card without forcing you to copy and paste event details manually.</li> </ul> <h3>2. Generative UI</h3> <p>In traditional web design, every user sees the exact same layout. An e-commerce store shows the same navigation bar whether you are a first-time visitor looking for customer support or a returning customer tracking a package.</p> <p>As highlighted in the <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-paradigm/">Nielsen Norman Group’s analysis on AI as a new UX paradigm</a>, computing is shifting from command-based interaction to outcome-specification. This enables <strong>Generative UI</strong>, where interfaces are rendered dynamically on the fly based on what you are trying to do in that exact moment. For example, platforms like <a href="https://v0.dev">Vercel v0</a> demonstrate how raw intent can instantly render functional code components without manual layout building. If you express a desire to compute complex financial data, the interface generates a dynamic calculator widget on demand, receding once your goal is completed.</p> <h3>3. Execution by AI Agents</h3> <p>Traditional interfaces are obsessed with prevention, constantly peppering users with confirmation pop-ups (<em>“Are you sure you want to delete this file?”</em>).</p> <p>Intent-driven interfaces adapt <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/">Jakob Nielsen’s 10 Usability Heuristics on User Control and Error Recovery</a> by shifting the safety net from <em>prevention</em> to <em>easy reversibility</em>. Because autonomous agents take actions on your behalf, products prioritise frictionless rollback mechanisms. A prominent “Undo” button, simple revision prompts (<em>“Make this summary shorter”</em>), and transparent audit logs replace intrusive warning modals.</p> <h3>The Hidden Risks</h3> <p>While removing interface friction is liberating, stripping away visual controls introduces significant product design challenges. When software acts on inferred intent rather than direct point-and-click commands, designers must navigate critical ethical and technical pitfalls.</p> <h3>The Illusion Of Control</h3> <p>When a website makes decisions for you, it can quickly feel patronising or invasive. As explored by the <a href="https://hci.stanford.edu/">Stanford Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) Group</a>, automation must maintain <strong>clear boundaries</strong> to prevent user frustration. Furthermore, ethical frameworks from the <a href="https://centerforhumanetechnology.substack.com/p/a-framework-for-incentivizing-responsible">Center for Humane Technology</a> emphasise that product designers must guard against manipulative defaults.</p> <p>Designers must strike a delicate balance: <strong>automate routine execution, but explicitly prompt the user for high-stakes confirmations</strong> (such as financial transactions, publishing public content, or altering privacy settings).</p> <h3>The Black Box Problem</h3> <p>In a traditional UI, if you get an unexpected result, you can usually diagnose the issue — perhaps you checked the wrong filter box or selected an incorrect date.</p> <p>With an intent-driven interface, troubleshooting becomes harder. To address this “black box” challenge, the <a href="https://pair.withgoogle.com/guidebook/chapters/feedback-and-controls/design-ai-feedback-loops">Google PAIR (People + AI Research) Guidebook</a> advocates for transparent feedback loops. The system must explicitly state its interpretation (<em>“Searching for non-stop flights to Chicago under $300...”</em>) so users can calibrate trust and correct misinterpretations instantly.</p> <h3>From A UX Perspective</h3> <p>As a UX designer, always being curious to understand how our design approach is changing in the world of AI, I find it essential that we re-evaluate the foundational elements of our craft.</p> <p>Rather than designing static buttons and rigid forms, we need to learn to map and present dynamic, generative UI components that adapt on the fly to user intent.</p> <p>By mastering these evolving patterns, like ambient feedback, inline prompts, and transparent AI status indicators, we can ensure that screenless, agent-driven tools remain intuitive, ethical, and deeply human-centered. </p> Conclusion <blockquote>The ultimate goal: The best design is no design.</blockquote> <p>For decades, the digital industry has evaluated software success through engagement metrics like <em>time spent in app</em>, <em>click-through rates</em>, and <em>page views per session</em>. Websites were deliberately engineered to maximise visual engagement and interaction volume.</p> <p>The philosophy behind <a href="https://www.nointerface.com/">Golden Krishna’s seminal book <em>The Best Interface Is No Interface</em></a>, aligned with the broader <a href="https://www.humanetech.com/impact-and-story">Center for Humane Technology’s Time Well Spent movement</a>, turns these metrics on their head. The ultimate test of a modern digital product is no longer how visually captivating its buttons are, but rather: <strong>How effectively did this software solve the problem and get out of the user’s way?</strong>, </p> <p>We are entering an era where web applications will no longer be measured by the beauty of their interfaces, but by their ability to render those complex interfaces completely obsolete. The future of the web isn’t more interaction — it is <strong>seamless, invisible completion</strong>.</p>
