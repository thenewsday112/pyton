Microduck, the Robot Pet That Imitates Life: Can We Really Grow Attached to a Machine?

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p>Imagine a small robotic duck walking around your home, reacting to its surroundings, and even picking up small objects with its beak. When it simulates sadness, it lowers its head and makes soft, melancholic sounds. When it expresses happiness, it produces higher-pitched, lively sounds accompanied by body movements. Its name is <strong>Microduck</strong>.</p> <p></p><p>Standing just 25 centimeters tall and weighing less than 800 grams, it illustrates how advances in artificial intelligence and robotics are making increasingly sophisticated interactions between humans and machines possible.</p><p></p><p>One of its most curious features is <strong>its voice</strong>. Every Microduck has a unique sonic identity generated from its processor's serial number. As a result, no two units produce exactly the same sounds. It may seem like a minor detail, but it makes each robot recognizable, almost as if it had its own personality. Through different sounds and postures, Microduck can also <strong>simulate emotional states</strong> such as sadness and happiness.</p><p></p><p>Of course, the robot does not actually experience these emotions: it reproduces their outward expressions. Yet, the effect can be surprisingly convincing to anyone watching. There is also another interesting aspect: <strong>social behavior</strong>. When two or more Microducks come together, they can interact and, when appropriately configured, even perform a kind of chorus. Their behavior resembles that of animals that live in groups and communicate with one another.</p><p></p><p>But behind this playful appearance lies an enormous technological challenge. Microduck's software is <strong>open source</strong>, allowing developers to study how it works, modify it, and create new behaviors. Moreover, thanks to a <strong>digital twin</strong> and reinforcement learning, the robot can be trained in a virtual environment.</p><p></p><p>There, it can experiment with thousands of movements, fall, correct its mistakes, and try again. Cloud computing makes it possible to run large numbers of experiments in parallel, something that would require considerable time, money, and resources in the physical world. Once learned, these behaviors can be transferred to the real robot, an approach commonly known as <em>sim-to-real transfer</em>. This is how it can acquire unusual skills, such as learning to move on skates.</p><p></p><p>Another major challenge is <strong>efficiency</strong>. Microduck is equipped with fifteen motors, sensors, and a compact computing system. A neural network helps control its movements, continuously adjusting its balance and position. All of this must run on a small battery, which provides limited autonomy. Nature has been dealing with similar challenges for millions of years: animals perceive their environment, react, and move using energy obtained from food.</p><p></p><p>We tend to take these abilities for granted, yet reproducing them in machines remains extraordinarily difficult. Consider the complexity of developing increasingly autonomous robots capable of understanding instructions, reasoning about what to do, and translating decisions into precise physical movements. <strong>Combining artificial intelligence, perception, and physical action in small, affordable devices with limited energy consumption is one of the major technological challenges of the coming years.</strong></p><p></p><p>This brings us to an interesting question: why did Microduck's developers choose these particular characteristics? Its distinctive voice, simulated emotions, interactions with other robots, and amusing movements seem directly inspired by the animal world. The goal is probably not just to make the product appealing, but also to encourage an emotional connection with its users.</p><p></p><p>A small robot like Microduck could anticipate <strong>a new generation of artificial pets</strong>, somewhere between intelligent toys and future household assistants.</p><p></p><p>It could keep us company, entertain us, and learn new behaviors through our interactions. It would also offer several practical advantages: it does not make a mess, does not need to be taken outside, does not get sick, and never requires a visit to the veterinarian. If we leave home for a week, we can simply turn it off and find it exactly where we left it when we return.</p><p></p><p>But a real pet is something profoundly different.
