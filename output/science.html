<p>Crater counts tell us that most of the Martian surface is more than 3 billion years old, but pieces of Mars we have on Earth tell a different story. The three main classes of Martian meteorites—shergottites, nakhlites, and chassignites—are all 2.4 billion years old or younger. Ancient pollen reveals a surprising twist in the evolution of flowers

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Flowering plants dominate landscapes around the world, and scientists have long debated how they became so successful. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">One familiar explanation points to the asteroid that wiped out the dinosaurs.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Fossil pollen tells it differently. The two moments that remade plant life came tens of millions of years before and after that impact, in the two hottest stretches of the past 120 million years. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">In both of them, plants were dying out and appearing at the same time. In between, when the planet was cooler, very little changed.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Kale Sniderman, an honorary research fellow at the <a href="https://www.unimelb.edu.au/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">University of Melbourne</a>, led the work with colleagues in Australia and Austria. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">They read it all from pollen and spores, the only plant fossils common enough to count this way. Every tally in the study is a tally of pollen types rather than whole plants.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;Each of these extremely warm &#8216;turnover intervals&#8217; lasted a few million years,&#8221; said Greg Jordan, a professor of botany at the <a href="https://www.utas.edu.au/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">University of Tasmania</a> and a co-author. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;Thus, the warmest greenhouse climates simultaneously destroyed and promoted plant diversity, but only when considered over million-year timescales.&#8221;</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-reading-the-fossil-record">Reading the fossil record</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Unlike larger plant remains, pollen can accumulate in enormous quantities in lake sediments, preserving a detailed record of ancient vegetation.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The catch is that paleontologists have not sampled every stretch of time equally. Dig harder in one period and it looks richer than the one next to it.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;The number of available fossils varies over geological time and between regions. Because of this, simple counts of fossilized species can give a misleading sense of diversity over evolutionary time,&#8221; Sniderman said.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">So the team built its own compilation and held the sampling effort steady across every slice of time before comparing one slice with the next. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The southeastern Australian dataset runs to 14,254 records from 335 fossil assemblages at 167 sites. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Much of it came out of decades of drilling reports archived by the <a href="https://gsv.vic.gov.au/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Geological Survey of Victoria</a> and its Tasmanian counterpart.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For most of the past 125 million years that corner of the world sat near 65 degrees south, close to <a href="https://www.earth.com/plants/ancient-amber-discovery-reveals-antarctica-once-had-forests/">Antarctica</a>.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-two-bursts-of-change">Two bursts of change</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Across the record, two short intervals carry almost all the action. One falls in the middle of the Cretaceous, about 95 to 90 million years ago. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The other falls in the late Paleocene and early Eocene, about 58 to 54 million years ago. Both are among the warmest greenhouse episodes of the past 200 million years.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The losses in those intervals were heavy. Of the ferns, mosses and their relatives first recorded before about 89 million years ago, roughly 75% were gone by the second warm episode. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Conifers and their relatives lost roughly 60% of their recorded pollen types by about 84 million years ago.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Even the first flowering plants took about 80% losses by the early Eocene.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">New types arrived in the same intervals. The land plant flora inherited from the Early Cretaceous roughly tripled in variety between about 120 and 55 million years ago, and the two bursts are where that growth happened. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Flowering plants finally overtook the other groups during the second one.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The flora reorganized twice, in other words, rather than drifting. One statistical break in the record falls at 83.5 million years ago and another at 54.3 million.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-both-latitudes-turned-over">Both latitudes turned over</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">A warm world isn&#8217;t warm everywhere in the same way. The Cretaceous tropics have often been read as hot and dry enough to be lethal. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">If so, hothouse episodes might have helped plants only in cool, wet places like the one the Australian record came from.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">So the team ran the same analysis on fossil pollen from northeastern Africa, which sat on the equator through the Cretaceous. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Those records, about 48,000 pollen grains from 19 wells and sites, show the same mid-Cretaceous burst of arrivals and losses in all three plant groups.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For the younger half of the story the team compared its record with a published analysis of pollen from the American tropics. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">There, too, the rate at which new types appeared peaked during the late Paleocene and early Eocene, and tracked global temperature.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-climate-beat-geography">Climate beat geography</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Warmth isn&#8217;t the only candidate. Australia was drifting north and breaking away from Antarctica over this period, and the plant groups were also competing with each other.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team tested all of it. Temperature, the continent&#8217;s isolation, its latitude and the plant groups&#8217; effects on each other went into models that let them compete to explain the record. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Of the 20 relationships those models supported, 13 involved climate.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The same held when whole classes of explanation were compared. Allowing climate terms improved the fit in 89% of cases, while isolation, latitude and plant-on-plant effects did so in fewer than half.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The asteroid comes out looking minor. Among the plants, the <a href="https://www.earth.com/climate/climate-states-mass-extinctions/">extinctions</a> ran well ahead of the impact. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The equatorial record shows ferns and conifers collapsing long before it, and in southeastern Australia the cone-bearing plants were as varied as ever just after the <a href="https://www.earth.com/earth-science/asteroid-dust-may-explain-what-really-killed-the-dinosaurs/">dinosaurs went</a>.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The authors wrote that some global crises, the end-Cretaceous extinction among them, are secondary to warming phases in shaping life on land. A long <a href="https://www.earth.com/earth-science/when-pangeas-tropical-forests-died-252-million-years-ago-they-locked-earth-in-a-five-million-year-hothouse/">hothouse</a> reaches the flora more deeply than a single catastrophe does.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-a-warm-miocene-stalled">A warm Miocene stalled</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">One stretch of time argues with the rest. Australia had drifted far enough north by the early Miocene, roughly 20 million years ago, that local sea surface temperatures nearly matched the early Eocene. Plants there barely responded.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The difference is that the planet as a whole didn&#8217;t match it. Peak Miocene temperatures ran about 18°F (10°C) above today&#8217;s, where the earlier hothouse ran about 36°F (20°C) above. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Global temperature predicted the Australian record better than the local water did, and the American tropics show the same muted Miocene.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Something global, then, rather than a warm patch. The authors propose a chain: extreme warmth, high carbon dioxide and a small gap between the poles and the equator speed up the water cycle. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That spreads warm, wet, <a href="https://www.earth.com/climate/earths-ancient-forests-carry-a-warning-for-forests-today/">forested</a> habitat, which is where new lineages take hold.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">But ancient warming offers little reassurance for today&#8217;s plants. Human-driven climate change is unfolding far faster than either prehistoric episode, leaving species much less time to adapt.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Chris Mays is a paleontologist at the <a href="https://www.nhm.at/en" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Natural History Museum Vienna</a> and a co-author of the study.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;While extinct species will eventually be replaced by new species, not many of us will be satisfied having to wait the few million years for them to evolve, especially since we have the power to prevent the loss of species today, if we stop emitting CO₂ from burning fossil fuels,&#8221; said Mays.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-mystery-behind-plant-diversity">The mystery behind plant diversity</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Why extreme warmth produces new plant species at all is still unsettled. Killing them is the easy half, since enough heat pushes a plant past what it can stand.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team lists candidates for the other half: a faster water cycle, steadier climates over the long haul, more carbon dioxide for photosynthesis. None of them is tested against the others here.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The African record stops at the end of the Cretaceous. It also holds only seven time intervals, too few to run the model comparison the Australian data supported.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The Australian analysis itself rests on 18 time bins, each a slice of geological time rather than a repeated experiment. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">And a count of <a href="https://www.earth.com/plants/ancient-pollen-reveals-how-grasslands-changed-after-the-last-ice-age/">ancient pollen</a> isn&#8217;t quite a count of plants, because plants that look identical in a pollen grain get counted once.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">More fossil records could help explain why extreme warmth transformed ancient ecosystems. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For now, the findings reveal how profoundly climate can reshape plant life – and why today&#8217;s rapid warming poses a very different threat.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The full study was published in the journal <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41477-026-02429-y" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank"><em>Nature Plants</em></a>.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8212;&#8211;</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Like what you read? <a href="https://www.earth.com/subscribe/">Subscribe to our newsletter</a> for engaging articles, exclusive content, and the latest updates.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Check us out on <a href="https://www.earth.com/earthsnap/">EarthSnap</a>, a free app brought to you by <a href="https://www.earth.com/author/eralls/">Eric Ralls</a> and Earth.com.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8212;&#8211;</p>

Ancient microbes reveal a new clue to how complex life began

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A white blood cell chasing a germ doesn&#8217;t swim. It crawls, reaching out with part of itself, gripping a surface and pulling the rest along. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Now researchers have filmed Asgard archaea, tiny microbes thought to be the closest relatives of complex cells, doing the same thing.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The archaea cells changed shape within minutes, grew and pulled back long, thin arms, and used them to haul themselves across glass. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Amoebas get around the same way, whereas bacteria and their microbial cousins mostly swim with spinning tails or tug themselves along on sticky threads.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The discovery suggests that crawling may have evolved long before complex life appeared.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Philipp Radler led the study in Christa Schleper&#8217;s lab at the <a href="https://www.univie.ac.at/en/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">University of Vienna</a>, working with an international team of researchers.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team watched two living species on lab glass, not the ancient microbe itself.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-how-scientists-filmed-asgard-archaea">How scientists filmed Asgard archaea</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most of what scientists know about Asgard archaea comes from reading their DNA. Only a handful have ever been grown in a lab.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That DNA carries instructions for proteins long linked to complex cells, which helps explain the group&#8217;s spot on the <a href="https://www.earth.com/science/humans-and-all-life-on-earth-may-have-spawned-from-asgard-archaea-eukaryotes/">tree of life</a>. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of those proteins is actin. In our own cells, actin builds a flexible inner frame that lets a cell change shape and move.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Electron microscope images had shown a round body with thin arms sticking out, but a still image can&#8217;t show movement.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">These microbes grow without oxygen, so the team sealed them inside thin glass chambers filled with nitrogen and carbon dioxide. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For runs of up to six hours, gas kept flowing into the chamber, which was held at about 72°F (22°C).</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-cells-reshaped-themselves-within-minutes">Cells reshaped themselves within minutes</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The main species, <em>Lokiarchaeum ossiferum</em>, has a body about 0.00004 inches (1 micrometer) across. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">A typical cell carried five arms, though some had one and others had ten. The longest arms reached 15 micrometers, roughly 14 body widths.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nothing held still. Arms grew at about 1.5 micrometers a minute, split into branches and pulled back, some slowly and others at about 11 micrometers a minute. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">This microbe takes about a week to divide, yet it rebuilt its shape over a few minutes.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The total length of a cell&#8217;s arms stayed about the same as it rearranged them, and cells with more arms tended to have shorter ones. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The researchers suspect the cells redistribute their existing outer membrane between arms rather than constantly producing more.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some cells changed their whole body too, switching from a stick shape to a ball.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-long-arms-pull-the-cells-forward">Long arms pull the cells forward</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">About half of the cells stuck to the glass also moved their bodies from one spot to another. Most wandered at random, and only 6 of 117 tracked cells kept going one way.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some cells anchored the tip of a front arm, and as the arm shortened, the body followed it, sometimes in a sudden jump. Others slid smoothly behind a single leading arm. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team found little sign of cells pushing from behind.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Their typical speed was about 1.6 micrometers a minute. At that pace, a cell moving in a straight line would take about 11 days to cover an inch (2.5 centimeters). </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That fits with crawling in complex cells, such as an <a href="https://www.earth.com/animals/this-microscopic-hunter-knows-which-bacteria-are-worth-chasing-before-it-learns-anything/">amoeba</a>, which move at 0.1 to 20 micrometers a minute. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.earth.com/animals/how-dead-sea-microbes-swim-through-some-of-earths-saltiest-water/">Swimming microbes</a> go far faster. The only other surface movement known in archaea relies on hairlike threads outside the cell. These cells used their own arms instead.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">When the team coated the glass with a sticky compound, both the arm movements and the crawling slowed down, a sign that the two are linked.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-actin-blocking-drugs-stopped-the-crawl">Actin-blocking drugs stopped the crawl</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">To test whether actin drives the motion, the team tried drugs that block actin in complex cells. Some barely changed anything. Three cut the number of cells holding on to the glass with arms.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team focused on a toxin called swinholide A, which worked at doses within the range used on complex cells. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">At 50 nanograms per milliliter, new arms stopped forming, existing ones stopped growing, and the cells stopped moving. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some remaining arms lost their inner support, bent, and swayed loosely in the water.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team also pulled out the proteins that cling to the microbe&#8217;s actin. Several belong to families known in complex cells for building actin strands up and cutting them apart.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">A second species, <em>Margulisarchaeum peptidophilum</em>, grown by the JAMSTEC group, behaved the same way.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Its arms grew about three times as fast as those of the first species, at 4.8 micrometers a minute, and its body moved about twice as fast. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Swinholide A stopped it too, halting arm growth and cutting the share of cells that moved.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Actin and the proteins that control it also turn up in almost every Asgard genome sequenced so far. To the authors, that suggests the group&#8217;s shared ancestor already carried them.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-did-crawling-help-complex-life-evolve">Did crawling help complex life evolve?</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Researchers think the <a href="https://www.earth.com/earth-science/complex-life-emerged-long-before-oxygen-flooded-earth/">first complex cells</a> formed about 2 billion years ago, when an Asgard microbe teamed up with a bacterium that ended up living inside it. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That bacterium became the ancestor of mitochondria, which make energy inside our cells.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Both species in this study are alive today and likely differ from that <a href="https://www.earth.com/science/microbe-asgard-archaea-from-2-billion-years-ago-could-be-the-ancestor-of-us-all/">ancient ancestor</a>. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The authors suggest the ancestor might have used its arms to move toward a partner, attach to it, and eventually wrap around it. They offer it as a possibility, not a result.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team also had to rely on drugs designed for the actin of complex cells, because there are no genetic tools for these microbes yet. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">And the cells crawled on glass, while in nature Asgard archaea live in tiny spaces within seafloor mud.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Scientists still don&#8217;t know exactly how these microbes control their movements, but the discovery offers a remarkable glimpse into the distant past.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The ability to crawl, change shape and reach toward other cells may have existed long before animals, plants or fungi appeared. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Those ancient movements could have helped set the stage for complex life itself.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The full study was published in the journal <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11063-9" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank"><em>Nature</em></a>.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8212;&#8211;</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Like what you read? <a href="https://www.earth.com/subscribe/">Subscribe to our newsletter</a> for engaging articles, exclusive content, and the latest updates.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Check us out on <a href="https://www.earth.com/earthsnap/">EarthSnap</a>, a free app brought to you by <a href="https://www.earth.com/author/eralls/">Eric Ralls</a> and Earth.com.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8212;&#8211;</p>

Helix Nebula offers a stunning glimpse of our Sun’s future

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Today&#8217;s Image of the Day from NASA reveals the Helix Nebula, a vast shell of glowing gas left behind by a dying star about 650 light-years from Earth.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">At first glance, the <a href="https://www.earth.com/space/hubble-captures-sharpest-view-yet-of-the-mysterious-egg-nebula/">nebula</a> resembles a colorful cosmic eye staring back at us. Its apparent ring, however, is partly an illusion of perspective.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Astronomers have concluded that we are looking almost directly down a tremendous cylinder of glowing gas, rather than at a simple bubble floating in space.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-looking-into-the-helix-nebula">Looking into the Helix Nebula</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The Helix Nebula lies in the constellation Aquarius and is one of the closest planetary nebulae to Earth.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Its relative proximity makes it appear enormous in our sky. The nebula spans nearly half the apparent diameter of the full Moon, even though its faint glow makes it far harder to see.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">At its distance from Earth, that apparent size corresponds to a ring nearly three light-years across.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The portrait combines observations from the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_Space_Telescope" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Hubble Space Telescope</a> with a much wider view captured by the Mosaic Camera on the National Science Foundation&#8217;s 0.9-meter telescope at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Hubble could not capture the entire sprawling structure in a single exposure.&nbsp;</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Astronomers used its Advanced Camera for Surveys to take multiple images, which were assembled into a mosaic and blended with the wider ground-based view.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The result exposes structures that would otherwise be difficult to see across the entire nebula.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-thousands-of-strange-filaments">Thousands of strange filaments</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Along the inner edge of the Helix are thousands of narrow structures that resemble tiny <a href="https://www.earth.com/space/dark-comets-hidden-near-earth-ice-giants-can-be-both-friend-and-foe/">comets</a>.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Each appears to have a dense head followed by a long tail, with the structures pointing back toward the star at the center of the nebula.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Ground-based astronomers had known about these features for decades, but Hubble revealed them in remarkable detail.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">They formed as hot gas flowing away from the dying central star encountered cooler material that the star had expelled earlier in its life.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The interaction helped sculpt the surrounding gas and dust into the intricate structures visible today.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Astronomers have suggested that the comet-like filaments could lie within a disk surrounding the central star, forming something like an enormous collar around it.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-colors-reveal-glowing-gases">Colors reveal glowing gases</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The dramatic colors in the image are not simply decorative. They trace different elements glowing within the nebula.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Blue marks light associated with oxygen, while the red regions trace hydrogen and nitrogen.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Together, these gases form the bright ring surrounding the small but extremely hot stellar remnant at the center.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That central object is a <a href="https://www.earth.com/space/hungry-white-dwarf-stars-eat-heavy-metals/">white dwarf</a>, the exposed core left after the original star shed much of its outer material.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The Helix therefore offers a preview of a possible distant future for our own solar system.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Stars similar in mass to the Sun eventually exhaust the fuel sustaining their interiors. As they approach the ends of their lives, they can cast their outer layers into space, exposing their hot cores.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Radiation from the remaining star then illuminates the expanding material around it.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-a-planetary-nebula-has-no-planets">A planetary nebula has no planets</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Despite the name, planetary nebulae have nothing to do with the formation of planets.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The term dates back to early telescope observations. Through small instruments, these round, fuzzy objects could resemble the disks of planets, and the name remained even after astronomers understood their true nature.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">With more powerful telescopes, structures hidden within those fuzzy patches become visible.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">In the Helix, what looks like a doughnut-shaped ring is actually part of a much more complicated three-dimensional structure.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The glowing tube extends an enormous distance through space and happens to be oriented nearly toward Earth. That alignment gives us the unusual perspective seen in the image.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-hubble-found-an-unexpected-opportunity">Hubble found an unexpected opportunity</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The circumstances behind Hubble&#8217;s observations of the Helix were unusual.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">During the Leonid meteor storm in November 2002, spacecraft controllers needed to protect <a href="https://www.earth.com/space/andromeda-galaxy-the-violent-history-of-our-closest-cosmic-neighbor/">Hubble</a> from the stream of particles passing through Earth&#8217;s neighborhood.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">They pointed the observatory&#8217;s more protected rear section toward the incoming meteor stream.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That restricted where Hubble could safely look, but the Helix Nebula happened to lie almost exactly in the opposite direction.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Rather than leaving the telescope idle, astronomers used the opportunity to observe the nebula.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Hubble spent nine orbits photographing the Helix and captured nine separate snapshots needed to cover its enormous apparent size.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Those observations ultimately became part of one of the most detailed portraits of this nearby stellar graveyard.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Image Credit: NASA, ESA, C.R.
