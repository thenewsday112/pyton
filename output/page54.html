NASA Explores Potential Commercial Servicing for Hubble Telescope

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →
The Hubble Space Telescope returns to orbit as an improved telescope after its second servicing mission in February 1997. NASA

NASA issued a Request for Information Oct. 29 to learn more about industry capabilities for in-space servicing to support the agency’s Hubble Space Telescope.

“Hubble still has important science ahead of it, and we should be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible,” said NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman. “If there are innovative and cost-effective ways to extend the life of an asset already on orbit, we owe it to the American taxpayer and the scientific community to explore them. Extending Hubble’s life would mean more discoveries, more science, and more opportunities to expand our understanding of the universe.”

The Request for Information seeks commercial interest in: potentially conducting a mission to boost Hubble to a higher altitude to extend its science lifetime; servicing Hubble to enhance its capabilities; or performing a safe, controlled re-entry into Earth’s atmosphere or orbital disposal at the end of Hubble’s mission.

“Hubble is a national icon that has fundamentally changed how humanity views our universe for more than 35 years,” said Nicky Fox, associate administrator, Science Mission Directorate, NASA Headquarters in Washington. “Commercial industry has rapidly evolved in recent years to offer new capabilities for in-space satellite capture and servicing. Given the time we have until Hubble’s anticipated re-entry, we are eager to explore the breadth of possibilities to boost Hubble to a higher orbit while we develop the flagship mission to follow.”

The Request for Information is available online and will remain open for responses until 3 p.m. EST, Monday, Nov. 30.

Like all spacecraft in low Earth orbit, Hubble’s altitude is slowly decreasing over time because of atmospheric drag. Hubble does not have a propulsion system to maintain its own orbit, and analysis predicts Hubble is likely to re-enter the atmosphere in the 2033 to 2034 timeframe. As such, NASA anticipates that a mission would need to take place by 2032.

The Hubble Space Telescope has been operating for more than three decades and continues to make ground-breaking discoveries that shape our fundamental understanding of the universe. Hubble is a project of international cooperation between NASA and ESA (European Space Agency). NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, manages the telescope and mission operations. Lockheed Martin Space, based in Denver, also supports mission operations at Goddard. The Space Telescope Science Institute in Baltimore, which is operated by the Association of Universities for Research in Astronomy, conducts Hubble science operations for NASA.

AlpineBSD announced – an AI-free FOSS OS

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Just days after Daemonarchy, there's another new OS in the BSD family – and one that is heading determinedly in almost the opposite direction.

AlpineBSD is so new that it barely exists yet. There are no installation media available, though it's possible to manually bootstrap an installation. What AlpineBSD does already have is an AI policy, and the plan is to avoid "the output of generative AI models (e.g. LLMs)" as far as possible:

"Any software whose code, documentation, binaries, or other assets include, in any part, the output of a generative AI tool such as an LLM, is considered 'tainted' for our purposes."

The core idea is to combine the kernel of NetBSD with "a userspace inspired by Alpine Linux and the objective of making it possible to use as little AI-generated software as possible."

Back in 2024, the NetBSD project made its position on AI plain. Its commit guidelines say that "code generated by a Large Language Model […] is presumed to be tainted code."

Back in July, Linus Torvalds stated that "Linux is not one of those anti-AI projects". Since then, NetBSD has seen a spike in donations and interest.

For someone mainly used to Linux, trying to install NetBSD is a little like hopping in a time machine to 30 years ago, and we speak from personal experience of Linux in the mid-1990s. As we mentioned when Slackware turned 30 a few years ago, back in 1996, this vulture tried Slackware 3.0 on his own hardware, and we never did manage to install it.

We tried NetBSD 11 a couple of months ago, and despite considerable help from the folks on the NetBSD-users mailing list, we failed to get the CDE desktop to start.

As an example, installing software on NetBSD is not the easy, comfortable task it is on Linux. It does have a package manager for handling binary packages: it's called pkgin, but as the NetBSD FAQ mentions, it's an optional extra. You must manually tick a box in the setup program to install pkgin with the rest of the OS, and when we tried NetBSD 9.3 four years ago, we didn't know that, overlooked that checkbox, and had to find a way to add pkgin later. (It is quite tricky to install a package manager when you don't have a package manager to install it with.)

This time around, we discovered that pkgin sometimes doesn't complete the installation process for you – it just leaves a text file in a destination directory somewhere, and you're expected to know that, go there, read it, and do any further steps yourself.

The NetBSD community has been quietly going its own way since 1993. It has its own ideas about what is normal, and its users expect that. Some folks on the mailing list told us that they never use binary packages anyway – they compile what they need from source code.

AlpineBSD's ports repository and source code are hosted on Sourcehut, under the account of sircmpwn, better known as Drew DeVault. We have quoted DeVault a few times over the years: we've often found that his views align with our own in various ways… such as about the best laptops ever made, and on simplicity in Linux distro design, or even on dot-com billionaires at FOSDEM.

Alpine's tools are quite well-suited to being adapted to other systems. We have previously mentioned the use of its packaging tool, APK, in the GNU-free Chimera Linux, which uses much of the FreeBSD userland, and also in Adélie Linux.

We will follow AlpineBSD with interest. There's strong appeal in the plan of combining perhaps the best-known, longest-established AI-free Unix-like kernel with the user-facing parts of pretty much the smallest and cleanest Linux distro there is. The AI advocates often warn LLM skeptics that they risk being left behind. Well, here's an OS for those who don't want to surf the AI wave. ®

The Newcastle United exit from the Carabao Cup leaves Matthias Jaissle facing a fair question: are we watching the uneven beginnings of something promising, or a team struggling to establish the foundations it needs?

For now, there is evidence for both views.

Everton should sharpen the scrutinybut it should not settle the verdict.

Jaissle was appointed on 5 August. He has had less than three months in charge, arriving shortly before the competitive season and succeeding a manager whose achievements gave supporters both wonderful memories and legitimate expectations. That is a demanding inheritance. It is also too short a period to judge a managerial appointment conclusively.

The league record offers some perspective. Newcastle have taken 11 points from eight matches, with three wins, two draws and three defeats. They have scored 12 goals and conceded 14. That is an uneven start, rather than evidence of either a breakthrough or a collapse.

There are genuine glimmers of hope. Winning 2–0 at Tottenham demonstrated that this side can deliver away from home. More recently, the victory by the same score at Crystal Palace provided an encouraging response to successive league defeats. Jaissle made five changes for Palace, and several of those introduced contributed directly to the result. That suggests a willingness to reconsider his selections and an ability to produce a response.

Availability also matters.

For the Hull game, Newcastle had nine players missing, while Joelinton, Anthony Elanga and Jacob Ramsey have been among those working their way back. A new coach trying to establish relationships and patterns deserves some allowance for that disruption. The allowance cannot be unlimited but it should be acknowledged.

Everton itself contained more NUFC attacking endeavour than the scoreline might suggest. Newcastle registered 28 shots against Everton’s 11 and more than twice as many touches in the opposition box. Those figures support the argument that the team continued to compete and create opportunities. They do not establish that Newcastle deserved to win: shot totals alone tell us too little about the quality of chances or control of a match.

The strongest concern is the recurrence of damaging defeats. Losing 4–1 at Leeds, 4–1 at Coventry and now 3–1 at Everton means conceding 11 goals across these three away matches. The clean sheets at Tottenham and Palace are encouraging but the contrast exposes how inconsistent Newcastle’s defensive performance has been.

At Coventry, Newcastle conceded an early penalty, failed to deal with a long throw for the second goal and then conceded twice after pulling themselves back into contention. At Everton, avoidable losses of possession and failures to defend decisive moments again proved costly. My concern is whether Newcastle United have sufficient collective security when something goes wrong.

Matthias Jaissle has emphasised individual mistakes and the need for greater maturity. That is understandable but repeated mistakes eventually become a coaching question too.

Are instructions clear? Are players receiving enough support? Does the approach suit those available? Players remain responsible for their decisions; the manager remains responsible for creating conditions in which good decisions become more likely.

The cup exit also represents a lost opportunity. Newcastle selected a side showing only one change from the Palace victory. This was a serious attempt to progress and it failed. Supporters are entitled to feel disappointed without being accused of impatience.

My assessment is that Matthias Jaissle deserves time, accompanied by clear expectations. Over the coming weeks, Newcastle United need fewer damaging errors, greater resilience after conceding and performances that can be sustained from one match to the next. Another impressive afternoon will encourage; a dependable run will persuade.

There is enough promise to support Matthias Jaissle. There is enough fragility to question him. The fairest position is to do both and judge whether the team is learning from setbacks rather than repeatedly explaining them.
