Ollie raised $7.5 million in September and shuts down on 6 November

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A privacy-first family assistant told users on Friday that it closes on 6 November. That is about a week of notice, and six weeks after it announced a $7.5 million seed round led by Khosla Ventures.

Ollie is worth ten minutes of your attention, and not because a startup failed. Because of the shape of it.

What it was

San Diego based, text-message first: no app to install, just a number you message. It connected to calendars and email, and handled the unglamorous part of a household, schedules, meals, to-dos, groceries, appointments, payments. For logins it opened a cloud browser session and sent you a link rather than asking for your password.

It also held SOC 2, the audit standard that says a company handles data to a documented process. In a category where "we care about privacy" is usually a paragraph in a pitch deck, that certificate was the product's argument.

The part developers should notice

A funded company with a compliance certificate, a subscription product and real users closed six weeks after announcing the round. Users got a week.

There is a structural reason assistant products are hard. The big platforms ship a general assistant for free, with the calendar, the phone and the mail already inside it. A startup has to be better at one narrow job, charge for it, and pay per token for every conversation. Privacy is a real differentiator, but it is expensive to prove and it does not reduce the token bill.

If you build on top of small vendors

Three habits that cost nothing and save a weekend:

1. Export weekly if a tool holds your calendar, receipts or requests, keep a local copy 2. Keep a fallback one script or spreadsheet that can do the job badly but without the vendor 3. Read the notice check how much warning the terms give before a shutdown

The third one is the one people skip. A week of notice is normal in this market, not an edge case.

The same week, the money side

On 30 October the FT reported that OpenAI had cut its latest round from up to $30 billion to about $20 billion, four days after lowering the valuation it was asking from $1.4 trillion to $1.25 trillion.

Read those two stories together and you get the quarter: capital became more careful, and small subscription products feel it first. A seed round announces the intention to build; it does not announce the runway.

Compare offers in net-worth-per-hour, not headline salary

A larger number on an offer letter often hides a worse deal. The human mind anchors on base pay because it is the most visible figure, but compensation is a bundle of conflicting variables.

Do the arithmetic on paper, because the gut always loses to the printer. Start by converting every offer into an hourly rate based on expected actual hours worked, not the forty hours listed in the handbook. If a role requires ten hours of uncompensated overtime each week, a ten percent salary bump disappears instantly. A longer week can eat a ten percent premium in a year through sheer time dilution.

Next, examine the mechanics of the non-cash components. Bonus structures tied to company metrics that never trigger are worth zero. Equity with a four-year cliff and no secondary market is a lottery ticket, not income. Calculate the real cost of the commute, both in fuel and in dead hours that could be spent building equity of your own.

Ask the boring benefits questions early, because they are checks, not fights. Find out the exact health insurance deductible, the employer match on retirement accounts, and the policy on hardware replacement. These details shift your annual take-home pay by thousands of dollars without showing up in the headline figure.

When you strip away the branding and the recruiter's enthusiasm, an offer is simply a contract to trade specific units of time and skill for specific units of capital. Run the math before you sign.

Fifteen site pages, 45 hand-written links between them, and the one page nothing points at

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Notifio has fifteen per-site guide pages: Kamernet, Rightmove, Idealista and twelve more. They are not CMS entries. Each one is a TypeScript object in src/lib/alerts/sites/, split across three files by region, assembled into one array:

/** * Every site that has an /alerts/<slug> page. * * This array is the single source for the hub grid, the route's static params, * the footer column and the sitemap. Adding a site is one edit here; forgetting * to list it somewhere is not a failure mode that exists. */ export const ALERT_SITES: readonly AlertSite[] = [ ...NETHERLANDS_SITES, ...UK_SITES, ...EUROPE_SITES, ];

The last sentence of that comment turned out to be slightly optimistic, and the rest of this post is why.

Each page names its own siblings, by hand

The type has a related field, and the comment on it is the whole spec:

/** Slugs of three sibling pages, for internal linking. */ related: string[];

Fifteen pages, three slugs each, forty-five edges, all typed out by a human who was thinking about that one page at the time. The resolver is forgiving:

/** Resolves `related` slugs, dropping any that do not exist yet. */ export function relatedSites(site: AlertSite): AlertSite[] { return site.related .map(getAlertSite) .filter((related): related is AlertSite => related !== undefined && related.slug !== site.slug); }

That forgiveness is deliberate, because it lets you write a slug for a page you are about to add. It also means a typo is not an error, a failed build or a warning. It is one fewer link, silently, forever. So the first thing worth doing is counting the graph rather than trusting it:

$ node --experimental-strip-types - <<'EOF' const dir = "./src/lib/alerts/sites/"; const mods = await Promise.all(["netherlands.ts","uk.ts","europe.ts"].map(f=>import(dir+f))); const sites = mods.flatMap(m=>Object.values(m).flat()); const inbound = Object.fromEntries(sites.map(s=>[s.slug,0])); let edges = 0, dangling = []; for (const s of sites) for (const r of s.related) { edges++; r in inbound ? inbound[r]++ : dangling.push([s.slug, r]); } console.log({ sites: sites.length, edges, dangling }); console.log(Object.entries(inbound).sort((a,b)=>b[1]-a[1])); EOF { sites: 15, edges: 45, dangling: [] }

No dangling slugs. Good. The distribution is the interesting part:

Inbound from siblings Pages 8 kamernet 4 pararius, huurwoningen, spareroom, openrent, housinganywhere 3 rightmove, zoopla, idealista 2 funda, immobilienscout24, spotahome 1 roommatch, wg-gesucht 0 facebook-marketplace

The page with nothing pointing at it

The Facebook Marketplace page is named by none of the other fourteen. It names three of them itself, so it gives links and receives none, and you can confirm that from outside the repo without reading a line of it:

$ mkdir -p /tmp/alerts $ for p in idealista immobilienscout24 wg-gesucht housinganywhere spotahome \ facebook-marketplace rightmove zoopla spareroom openrent \ kamernet pararius funda huurwoningen roommatch; do curl -s https://notifio.app/alerts/$p -o /tmp/alerts/$p.html done $ grep -l '"/alerts/facebook-marketplace"' /tmp/alerts/*.html | wc -l 0 $ grep -l '"/alerts/kamernet"' /tmp/alerts/*.html | wc -l 15

Kamernet scores fifteen out of fifteen rather than eight because of the second linking mechanism, which I will come back to. Facebook Marketplace scores zero on both.

It is not orphaned from the site. The hub links all fifteen, and so does the sitemap, which is generated from the same array:

const alertPages: MetadataRoute.Sitemap = ALERT_SITES.map((site) => ({ url: `${APP_URL}/alerts/${site.slug}`, lastModified: new Date("2026-09-14"), changeFrequency: "monthly", priority: 0.8, }));

So it is reachable in two clicks from the home page and it is in the sitemap. What it has is the weakest internal link profile on the site, and the reason is obvious once you look at who would plausibly link it. The other fourteen are rental portals. This one is a general-purpose marketplace that happens to carry rentals, it sits alone in its own market section, and when I was writing each portal's three siblings I was picking other portals every time. Nobody decided to strand it. It is what you get when every edge is chosen locally and nothing looks at the graph.

Eleven of the forty-five edges are one-way, for the same reason. A page links a sibling, the sibling links three others, and nothing notices the asymmetry.

Why the footer duplicates five slugs

The second linking mechanism is the footer, which carries a hardcoded five:

/** * A hand-picked five, rather than the full catalogue from `lib/alerts`. * * The footer is a client component, and importing the catalogue here would ship * every word of every site page to the browser on every route to render five * links. These are the highest-traffic guides; everything else is one click away * behind "All supported sites". */ const FOOTER_SITES = [ { slug: "kamernet", name: "Kamernet" }, { slug: "pararius", name: "Pararius" }, { slug: "funda", name: "Funda" }, { slug: "rightmove", name: "Rightmove" }, { slug: "spareroom", name: "SpareRoom" }, ];

This is the one duplication in the system I would defend. Those site objects are large, every field is hand-written prose, and a client component importing the barrel would put all fifteen of them into the JavaScript bundle on every route on the site to render five anchors. Five slugs and five display names is a cheap copy.

It is still a copy, and it is why the counting above has two different answers for Kamernet. Five pages get a link from every page on the site including their own, and the ten that are not in that list get only what their siblings give them. The footer is also the reason "nothing in the catalogue can be half-added" is not quite true: add a site and the hub, the route, the sitemap and the footer's "all supported sites" link all pick it up, but the footer's five do not change, and nothing says they should.

What the template is not allowed to do

Worth saying why these pages exist as fifteen fat objects rather than one template with a name interpolated into it, because it is the decision that made the link graph matter at all:

/** * The shape of an /alerts/[site] page. * * Every field here is prose written for one specific site. The page template in * app/alerts/[site]/page.tsx contributes layout and nothing else: there is no * sentence in it with the site name interpolated into it, because a set of pages * that differ only by a noun is a doorway-page pattern and gets treated as one. * If a field below cannot be filled with something true about this site and this * site alone, the site does not get a page. */

Which is why there are fifteen and not fifty. Every claim about another company's notifications also carries a citation that renders on the page:

/** * A claim about a third party's product, with the page it came from. * * Anything we say about how another company's alerts behave carries one of * these. The citation is rendered on the page, which keeps us honest and gives * a reader a way to check a detail that may have changed since we wrote it. */ export type Source = { label: string; url: string; };

A template could not satisfy either rule, and a page that satisfies both is expensive enough that you write it only when you have something site-specific to say. That caps the catalogue, which is the actual mechanism keeping the thing honest.

The hub section nobody designed

One more artefact of deriving the hub from the array. Six market sections, rendered in a two-column grid:

$ curl -s https://notifio.app/alerts | grep -o "Another site?" | wc -l 6

Three, counted honestly. Each match appears twice because the RSC flight payload repeats the markup. The grid gets a filler card whenever a section has an odd number of sites:

{market.sites.length % 2 === 1 && ( <div className="hidden bg-[#0a0a0a] p-6 sm:block"> <p className="mb-2 text-[15px] font-semibold text-[#3a3a3a]">Another site?</p>

Netherlands has five, Spain has one, Marketplaces has one, so three of the six sections carry it. Nobody chose which sections get a "Notifio is not limited to this list" nudge. The parity of a hand-written array did.

What I would change

The graph needs a test more than it needs editing. Three assertions, all of them cheap next to the metadata-length test that already reads these same files:

  • Every slug in related resolves. Currently true, enforced by nothing, and silently false the moment it is not.
  • Every page has at least one inbound edge. This is the one that would have caught Facebook Marketplace on the commit that added it.
  • No page names itself. relatedSites already filters self-links at render time, which means the data is allowed to contain one and you would never find out.

Reciprocity I would leave alone. Forcing all forty-five edges to be mutual would make the UK four and the Dutch five into two closed cliques, and the whole point of Facebook Marketplace naming SpareRoom and OpenRent is that a person reading about a marketplace might want a real portal next. One-way is sometimes the correct answer. Zero inbound never is.

The pages are all up if you want to check the counts: the hub lists all fifteen, Kamernet is the one everything points at, and Facebook Marketplace is the one that found out about this post the hard way.

When a Small Team Should (and Shouldn't) Adopt Kubernetes

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Kubernetes has become the default answer to almost every infrastructure question. Read a few conference talks or job postings, and it can feel like running anything else is a mistake. But Kubernetes was built at Google scale, for problems most small teams do not have yet.


For a team of three to fifteen engineers, adopting Kubernetes too early can cost months of productivity. Adopting it too late can leave you stuck with fragile deployments. This post gives you a practical way to decide.

What Kubernetes Actually Gives You

Kubernetes is a container orchestrator. It schedules containers across machines, restarts them when they fail, rolls out new versions gradually, scales them up and down, and gives them a consistent way to find each other on the network.


Those are real benefits. But each one comes with something you have to learn, configure, and maintain: networking, ingress, storage, RBAC, secrets, upgrades, monitoring, and cost control. The platform does not remove operational work. It moves the work to a different layer.


When a Small Team Should Adopt Kubernetes

  • You run many services that need to scale independently


If you have a dozen or more services and each has different traffic patterns, orchestration starts to pay off. Scaling one busy service without touching the others is exactly what Kubernetes does well.


  • You need frequent, safe, zero-downtime releases  


Rolling updates, readiness probes, and easy rollbacks are built in. If you ship several times a day and downtime directly costs revenue, this is a strong reason to adopt.  

  •  You already have containerization and CI/CD habits


Teams that already build container images, run automated tests, and deploy through a pipeline can adopt Kubernetes far more smoothly.


The platform fits into a workflow that already exists. 

  • You need portability across clouds or environments


If customers require deployment in different clouds, or you want to avoid deep lock-in to a single provider's compute service, Kubernetes gives you a common layer.

  • Someone on the team genuinely owns it


This is the most overlooked condition. Kubernetes needs at least one person who understands it deeply, or a partner who does. Without an owner, small problems become long outages.

When a Small Team Should Not Adopt Kubernetes

  •  You have one application, or a handful


A monolith or a few services rarely need orchestration. A well-configured virtual machine, or a managed platform, is simpler and cheaper.


  • Your traffic is steady and predictable


If load barely changes, autoscaling gives you very little. You would be paying a complexity cost for a feature you do not use


  • Nobody on the team has operated it before


Learning Kubernetes in production, during an incident, is painful. A steep learning curve combined with a small team is the most common cause of failed adoptions.

 

  • You are still finding product-market fit


Early-stage teams should spend their time on the product. Infrastructure that lets you ship the next feature faster is better than infrastructure that is impressive.   


  • You are adopting it because it looks good on a CV or a pitch deck


Trend-driven adoption is expensive. If you cannot name the specific problem Kubernetes solves for you, you probably do not need it yet.

Simpler Options to Consider First

Skipping Kubernetes does not mean skipping good practice. Several options give you most of the benefits with much less overhead:

  • Managed container services such as AWS ECS with Fargate, Google Cloud Run or Azure Container Apps. You run containers without managing a cluster.
  • Platform-as-a-service options such as Heroku, Render or Elastic Beanstalk for straightforward web applications.
  • Docker Compose on a single server for small internal tools or early products, paired with good backups and monitoring.
  • Managed Kubernetes (EKS, GKE, AKS) if you do decide to adopt. It removes control-plane maintenance, which is the hardest part to run yourself.


Many teams start with one of these and move to Kubernetes later. Because the workloads are already containerized, that migration is far easier than starting from scratch.

A Quick Decision Checklist

Ask yourself these questions honestly:

  1. Do we run more than roughly ten services, or expect to within a year?
  2. Do different services need to scale independently?
  3. Do we deploy often, and does downtime hurt us financially?
  4. Is our application already containerized, with a working CI/CD pipeline?
  5. Do we have at least one person who can own the cluster, or a reliable partner?
  6. Can we budget time for upgrades, security patching, and monitoring?


If you answered yes to most of these, Kubernetes is probably a good fit. If you answered no to most, a simpler platform will likely serve you better for now.

If You Do Adopt It, Start Small

  • Use a managed control plane rather than running your own.
  • Begin with one non-critical workload and learn from it.
  • Set resource requests and limits from day one to avoid noisy-neighbour problems and surprise bills.
  • Invest early in monitoring and alerting, since a cluster you cannot observe is a cluster you cannot trust.
  • Keep configuration in version control and automate deployments with CI/CD.
  • Plan regular upgrades. Kubernetes versions leave support quickly.

Final Thoughts

Kubernetes is a powerful tool, not a milestone. The right question is not "Are we big enough for Kubernetes?" but "Do we have problems that Kubernetes solves better than anything simpler?"


For many small teams, the honest answer is not yet, and that is a perfectly good engineering decision. Choose the simplest platform that meets your reliability needs today, keep your workloads containerized, and move to Kubernetes when the pain of not having it clearly outweighs the cost of running it.

Voyager 1 gives controllers the silent treatment

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Voyager 1 gave controllers the silent treatment for a few days this month before resuming communication weeks before the spacecraft reaches the one-light-day milestone.

The incident happened on October 18: NASA's Deep Space Network (DSN) couldn't detect a signal from the probe. On October 19, on the next scheduled communication attempt, there was still nothing. Was this it? Had the decades-long galactic venture finally ended?

Things are operating on a knife's edge for the two veteran spacecraft, which will reach their half-century next year. Voyager 2 suffered an antenna pointing issue in 2023, and a low power condition caused Voyager 1 to unexpectedly turn off one of its two radio transmitters.

The good news is that on October 20, the signal reappeared, leaving engineers wondering why Voyager 1 temporarily went silent.

Thus far, it seems a group of thrusters went offline and their backups were turned on. This could indicate onboard computers detected a problem and put the spacecraft into a safe mode.

Kareem Badaruddin, Voyager mission manager at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, said: "This is the first time we've seen the spacecraft do something like this, but we are also at a point in the mission where we have to expect the unexpected.

"We're relieved to be back in contact with Voyager 1 and are eager to understand what happened."

The Register spoke to Badaruddin in 2024, where he described the challenges of working with the veteran spacecraft, but he reckoned the work was the "best job at JPL."

The twin Voyager spacecraft have recently been through a procedure dubbed the "Big Bang," where controllers turned off onboard devices and switched to lower power alternatives. The result will extend the lifetime of the spacecraft by a few more years and postpone further instrument deactivation. It was completed first on Voyager 2, and then Voyager 1, six weeks later in September.

However, the brief lapse in communication highlights that despite the ingenuity of the team that keep the spacecraft running, the project must eventually come to an end. In 2026, Suzanne Dodd, Project Manager for the Voyager project, told The Register what the end would look like.

She said, "We're just going to one day not hear the signal from the DSN, and we're going to rush around and make sure that it's not something on the ground."

Months of listening will follow, before engineers throw in the towel and declare the mission over. And then? Maybe a farewell message sent to the last known location.

Thankfully, in this case, the signal returned, so no need for a goodbye transmission just yet.

In a few weeks, Voyager 1 will be so far away that it will take a full 24 hours for commands to reach it and another 24 hours for a response to reach Earth.

Communications blip notwithstanding, there is every chance both Voyager spacecraft will still be operational as they pass their half-century milestone next year and, technology and funding permitting, continue sending data to Earth for a good few years yet. ®

NASA ponders a Hubble servicing mission or a controlled deorbit

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

NASA has posted a contract opportunity notice, also known as a request for information, from commercial space orgs who can service the Hubble Space Telescope.

Hubble was always looked after by NASA astronauts flying on the Space Shuttle, yet the last Hubble Space Telescope (HST) servicing mission was the STS-125 Space Shuttle flight, launched on May 11, 2009. At the time, scientists hoped the mission would add another five years of operational life to the venerable spacecraft. Almost 17 years on, the HST (aka Hubble) is still delivering science.

However, time is running out for the observatory. NASA reckons there is a 10 percent chance it could reenter the Earth's atmosphere by September 2032, rising to 50 percent by December 2033. As the end of the Swift mission showed, increased solar activity can cause greater-than-anticipated atmospheric drag.

Also, like Swift, Hubble has no propulsion system, and so when it reenters (as it will, without intervention), the reentry will be uncontrolled. Hubble is sufficiently massive that there is a remote chance somebody on the ground could have a very bad day when that happens.

As such, one mission option suggested by NASA is a vehicle to grapple the observatory and perform a controlled reentry and disposal.

However, there are other options. Something to boost the telescope's orbit up to at least 600 km, or another to "enhance its ongoing performance and increase its resilience and expected on-orbit lifetime."

Both are intriguing options, and important since, as NASA acknowledges, no replacement for Hubble is expected before the 2040s, when the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) could become operational. Despite the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the Nancy Roman Space Telescope, Hubble remains in high demand, with NASA reporting a 7:1 oversubscription.

Before becoming NASA Administrator, Jared Isaacman advocated a SpaceX mission to boost the Hubble, but the idea was ultimately rejected. Similar plans could now be dusted off. NASA's requirement is for a mission to occur by 2032, and not harm the Hubble.

A soft-capture mechanism was fitted to Hubble during the STS-125 mission, which will mean docking with the spacecraft will be easier than the grappling planned for Swift. Then, the question is what to do with it. A controlled reentry is one option. The other options – boosting to a higher altitude or "enhance its ongoing performance and increase its resilience" are intriguing. Northrop Grumman's Mission Extension Vehicle (MEV), for example, is capable of on-orbit servicing and extending the lifetime of satellites. The Hubble is an order of magnitude more challenging, but the technological hurdles can be overcome with time and funding.

Last year, The Register spoke with Dr John Grunsfeld, one of the astronauts (now retired) who worked on Hubble during the STS-125 mission. Grunsfeld told us that the veteran spacecraft faced death by a thousand cuts as its budget was whittled away. The observatory itself recently switched from three to one gyro mode (the telescope uses gyros to control where it is pointed) to eke out a few more years of operational life.

At this point, NASA is looking for information and comment – this isn't a request for proposals. There is no indication of how much the agency expects to pay. However, the timescale is immutable. Without intervention, Hubble will make an uncontrolled reentry in the coming years. ®

I came up with the idea of the planet Eden in 1992 for a short story called The Circle of Stones. They bring heat to the surface to power their metabolisms, they keep the air warm and, if you live on Eden, the steady “Hmmmph hmmmph hmmmph” of their constant pumping is the background of your waking and sleeping life, merging in the distance to a steady hum. All the trees are hot, and some of them are so hot that you’d burn yourself if you touched their trunks. If you cut down a tree, you have to watch out for the spurt of scalding liquid that bursts out.

When I read stories set on alien planets, I’m sometimes disappointed in the “animals” (I’ll drop the inverted commas now). Writers often seem to dream up a motley collection of bizarre creatures without much regard for how they might actually survive or how they are related to one another. I tried to design my animals on evolutionary principles, giving thought to how they were adapted to their environment, the role they played in the ecosystem and how they might plausibly have evolved. Animals on Earth fall into a large but finite number of basic body plans. For example, monkeys, cows, crocodiles and seagulls all have four limbs and, though some descendants of quadrupeds have lost their limbs (snakes, whales), there are no examples of quadrupeds developing extra ones. In the same way, large animals on Eden all have a six-limbed body plan. There are other animals that don’t conform to this, just as there are arthropods, et cetera on Earth, but the large animals are all based on this six-limbed pattern.

I’ve already mentioned how different Eden eyes are from the eyes of terrestrial animals. They have evolved alongside the evolution of bioluminescence, rather in the way that colourful flowers and nectar-eating insects have evolved alongside one another on Earth. Eden trees use luminous lanternflowers to attract the creatures that help them reproduce, just as flowering plants on Earth use colour and scent.

I gave a lot of thought to the biology of Eden, but I have to admit that, in strictly scientific terms, my imaginary world isn’t entirely viable. Free-floating planets with warm cores? Yes! Not just possible, but a certainty! But free-floating planets with warm surfaces? Sadly no. More than one astronomer has told me they just couldn’t happen. No matter how many radiator-like trees there might be, I’ve been told, it still wouldn’t be enough to maintain liquid water or a warm atmosphere.

Let’s call it “fiction science”, science made up for a story, which works in some places and in others allows itself poetic licence. Almost all science fiction uses fiction science and Dark Eden contains several other examples. I took some liberties with human genetics, for instance. And then there’s the question of how people got to Eden in the first place. (It’s many light years away from Earth.) Like the writers of pretty much all stories set in interstellar space, I pretended that faster-than-light travel was possible and went with that, although it most probably isn’t.

(I think that’s OK. It allows us to visit, in our imaginations at least, parts of the universe we could never otherwise hope to reach.)

What I wanted from Eden was for it to be a world that hung together well enough to be a satisfying setting for my story, one in which I and my readers could immerse ourselves. The story is about exile and loss. The perpetually dark sky, and the animals with their blank unreadable eyes, serve as reminders that Eden folk are exiles. At the beginning of Dark Eden, every human being in Eden lives around a circle of stones that marks the place where their ancestors first touched down. And this circle represents to Eden folk the hope for return and redemption, which is central to their whole world view.

The fact that I took some liberties when constructing Eden doesn’t mean it wouldn’t be interesting also to write about a real free-floating planet. And, as it happens, I’ve had the opportunity recently to do just that. In a project initiated by Comma Press, I was one of a number of writers who paired up with astronomers from Jodrell Bank to write stories for an anthology called Firmament. My astronomer collaborator, Ida Janiak, helped me build a scenario in which a free-floating planet called Lupus arrives in the solar system. Apart from being roughly Earth-sized, Lupus isn’t much like Eden, but somewhat like the Jovian moon Europa. Its surface is covered by a kilometres-thick shell of ice in no danger of melting, with a liquid ocean beneath and… well, read the story! The anthology will appear in November but there will also be a condensed version of mine and Ida’s story in the Christmas edition of New Scientist, out online on 16 December and in print on the 19th.

I hope, meanwhile, you will immerse yourself in the luminous forests of Eden with their pulsing trees. Possible or not, they are very beautiful, if I say so myself.

Why costume contacts often aren't safe

Cheap Halloween contacts tend to be one-size-fits-all, but the AAO points out that contacts should never be one-size-fits-all. A contact that is too large or too small for your eye can cause corneal abrasions, corneal ulcers, and can result in infections like keratitis. Infections can, in severe cases, lead to blindness. A 2017 report from the FDA found bacterial contamination in 27% of costume contacts.

If you do wear contacts (Halloween or otherwise), the AAO recommends keeping an eye out for symptoms like redness, swelling, pain, or excessive discharge. Seek care as soon as you suspect an infection. Your eyes are more sensitive to infections than other parts of the body, so be cautious.

How to find safe costume contacts

Legally, contacts require a prescription, so if those colored contacts you're eyeing can be bought without showing a doctor’s prescription, they’re not legit. The real thing can be expensive, though: I found prices of $40 and up from lens.com, Discount Contacts, and Wicked Eyez.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission has told prediction-market operators Novig and ProphetX not to list a set of sports event contracts the two companies had already self-certified, according to Front Office Sports. The agency also asked both firms to explain how the markets would comply with federal commodities law.

A CFTC spokesperson confirmed that letters had been sent to both companies. “Any derivatives contract offered by a CFTC-regulated exchange must be compliant with the Commodity Exchange Act,” the spokesperson said.

Micro contracts and scorigami

The two letters target different products. Novig was told that its micro contracts, such as a market on which player opens the scoring in a basketball game, are open to manipulation. ProphetX was told that its scorigami contracts, which settle on whether a game finishes with a scoreline that has never occurred in that sport, do not meet the Commodity Exchange Act’s definition of a swap.

Sources told Front Office Sports that the letters describe the filings as false self-certifications, the term used when an exchange certifies a contract that may not be permitted under the statute. Neither set of contracts had been listed, so no customer has traded on them.

ProphetX framed the request more narrowly. A company spokesperson said CFTC staff “requested that we voluntarily defer listing the Scorigami contracts pending further review, not that we withdraw them.” The company said it complied, has since supplied further analysis on the contracts’ compliance and economic utility, and remains in contact with agency staff. Novig did not immediately respond to a request for comment, the outlet reported.

A signal to the wider market

Under the current framework, exchanges can self-certify and list new contracts without prior approval, and the CFTC can step in afterward. The agency would not say if it has ever before told an operator to hold back a self-certified sports contract. One source described the letters as “kind of a warning shot” for the sector.

The move does not appear to be limited to two companies. Senior CFTC officials have also spoken with DraftKings and FanDuel and cautioned that listing micro contracts could bring the same response, according to the report. Other exchanges have voiced frustration with the agency’s stance, pointing out that many state regulators allow comparable in-game wagers at licensed sportsbooks.

The letters follow a separate round of CFTC inquiries last month into incentive programs at Kalshi, Polymarket, Rothera and Novig. They also come ahead of a final rule the agency is expected to issue after its June proposal, a 267-page notice that would restrict in-game props and limit markets on high school sports. The proposal sits inside a wider dispute over how sports contracts are treated under federal rules, with several states arguing those products should fall under gambling law.

Leagues have pushed for limits

One source said the Novig and ProphetX markets were exactly the type of event contracts professional leagues have objected to most. On October 19, the NBA tightened the rules on player props and micro bets for its sportsbook partners, among them markets on who scores a game’s first basket, Yogonet reported. Those league rules cover traditional sportsbooks, not prediction markets.

It is not clear what the CFTC would do if either company went ahead and listed the contracts. Both firms have the chance to make their case to the agency, and the outcome is likely to shape how far other exchanges push into single-play sports markets before the final rule lands.
