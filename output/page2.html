NIST Just Rewrote the Post-Quantum Deadline. Your RSA-3072 Plan Is Out of Date

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On October 27, NIST published the final version of IR 8547, Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards. If you followed the draft from November 2024, you might think you know what it says. You probably don't, because the final version changed the one thing that mattered most.

The draft deprecated only the 112-bit parameter sets after 2030. That left a comfortable reading: RSA-3072, P-256 ECDSA, and Ed25519 looked acceptable until 2035. The final report closes that door. Every approved quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithm is deprecated after 2030 and disallowed under NIST standards and guidelines after 2035, regardless of key size.

That is not a small edit. It moves the algorithms most products actually ship today into the deprecation category, with just over four years on the clock.

What the schedule actually says

For signatures, RSA, ECDSA, and EdDSA are all deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035. For key establishment, finite-field Diffie-Hellman, elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman, MQV, and RSA key transport get the same treatment. The dates are the same as the draft. The scope is much wider.

"Deprecated" in NIST usage does not mean banned. It means the algorithm may still be used, but there is a security risk, and the data owner has to examine that risk and decide whether to continue. In practice, that turns into a documented risk-acceptance obligation. For federal high-value assets and high-impact systems, the grace period is shorter than it looks: an executive order from June 2026 requires post-quantum key establishment by December 31, 2030, and post-quantum signatures by December 31, 2031. Those systems do not get until 2035. They get 2030 and 2031.

And the report does not stop at federal systems. NIST explicitly positions it as a blueprint for critical infrastructure and other organizations. The deadlines reach private vendors through procurement, not through NIST directly.

How a government report becomes your vendor requirement

NIST IR 8547 is not binding on private companies. It reaches them through five mechanisms, and together they explain why this report functions as a vendor requirement in practice.

First, the executive order directs the FAR Council to propose a rule requiring covered contractors to comply with NIST's post-quantum FIPS by December 31, 2030. That proposed rule is due around December 19, 2026, under a 180-day clock. Second, OMB Memorandum M-26-15, issued in June 2026, tells agencies to build post-quantum integration into product requirements and to settle post-quantum responsibilities with FedRAMP cloud providers. Agencies owed their migration plans by October 22, 2026. Third, CISA published a list of product categories back in January 2026, from web browsers to endpoint encryption, where agencies "should acquire only PQC-capable products." Fourth, NIST is revising its module validation processes to move faster. Fifth, CISA owes minimum elements for a cryptographic bill of materials by around March 19, 2027.

If you sell software, cloud services, or hardware into any of those categories, expect to be evaluated on post-quantum capability in federal acquisitions now, not after the FAR rule lands. And vendors are unlikely to keep separate federal and commercial cryptographic baselines for long. One baseline wins, and it will be the post-quantum one.

A Government Accountability Office report from October found that none of the 24 agencies it reviewed had fully addressed all three preparatory practices: building a cryptographic inventory, identifying funding, and planning testing. The people with the biggest budgets and the clearest mandate are behind. That should tell smaller teams something: this is not a task you start in 2029.

The hybrid question finally has an answer

For builders, the most useful clarification in the final report is about hybrid key establishment, the approach that pairs ML-KEM with a classical exchange like X25519. Until now, it was unclear how such constructions would fare in FIPS 140 validation once the classical half became deprecated or disallowed. The final report answers it: as long as at least one input to an approved composite key-establishment technique comes from an approved scheme, the combination can be validated. A hybrid mode does not lose its validation status because its classical half ages out.

That removes the last respectable excuse to wait. You can ship hybrid now without painting yourself into a validation corner later.

Two cautions, though. A validated module that supports hybrid ML-KEM does not prove that a given deployment actually negotiates it. Capability and enablement are different things, and buyers should check which one they are getting. And on hybrid signatures, NIST is more careful: it will accommodate dual signatures in validation but notes that their security properties are complex and need careful analysis. Signatures are the slower, more operationally disruptive half of this transition. They touch certificate authorities, firmware signing, hardware security modules, and smart cards, and none of those move quickly.

NIST also named ML-KEM-768 as its recommended default parameter set, calling it a large security margin at a reasonable performance cost, and said it has no current plans to standardize additional KEMs beyond HQC. If you are picking a parameter set today and want the choice least likely to be second-guessed, that is the one.

What I would do this quarter

I build a small end-to-end encrypted messenger, and I went through this migration myself: ML-KEM-768 for key establishment and ML-DSA-65 for identity signatures, replacing a classical design. The protocol part was not the hard part. The hard part was everything around it: key storage, migration paths for existing sessions, and making sure a new key type did not silently break an old client. That experience shapes how I read this report, and it is why I think the practical checklist matters more than the dates.

First, make a cryptographic inventory. You cannot migrate what you cannot list. Walk your codebase and your infrastructure and write down every place a public key is generated, exchanged, stored, or verified. Include the boring ones: TLS certificates, code signing, backup encryption, JWT signing keys, SSH host keys. The inventory is the part the GAO found agencies missing, and it is the part most small teams skip.

Second, put ML-KEM-768 behind a flag in your key establishment path, hybrid with your existing classical exchange, and turn it on where you control both ends. Do not wait for a perfect migration plan. The hybrid validation answer means this step is safe to take now.

Third, design for crypto-agility from the start. The algorithm you pick today will not be the last one you ship. If swapping a KEM or a signature scheme requires touching twenty files and a database migration, fix that structure before you fix the algorithm. A clean seam between "what key type" and "what the protocol does with it" is worth more than picking the perfect parameter set.

Fourth, treat signatures as a separate, longer project. Key establishment can go hybrid quickly. Signatures touch trust roots, and trust roots move slowly. Start the inventory for signatures now, even if the migration itself takes years.

Fifth, watch two dates that are closer than 2030: the FAR proposed rule around December 19, 2026, and the CISA bill-of-materials elements around March 19, 2027. If you sell to anyone who sells to the government, those will show up in your contracts and questionnaires before the 2030 deadline does.

The deadline stopped being a draft

For two years, the post-quantum transition had the feel of a long-range plan: real standards, vague dates, and plenty of room to say "we will get to it." The final IR 8547 changes the texture of it. The dates are now attached to procurement rules, validation processes, and contract clauses. The deprecation scope now covers the strong classical defaults, not just the weak ones. And the agencies that were supposed to be ready first have, by the GAO's account, not finished their homework.

None of this means a quantum computer is about to break RSA next week. It means the paperwork, the procurement, and the product roadmaps have caught up with the standards. If your plan still says RSA-3072 and P-256 are fine until 2035, that plan is out of date as of October 27. Rewrite it now, while rewriting it is still cheap.

SpecterLAN: Find the Ghosts on Your Network

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This is a submission for the Hacktoberfest Open-Source AI Challenge Week 4: Ghost in the Machine

What I Built

I built SpecterLAN, a local-first network monitor that helps you understand which devices are connected to your network and what changed between scans.

SpecterLAN can show:

  • New devices
  • Devices that went offline
  • Devices that returned
  • IP address changes
  • Devices that still need review

For the Ghost in the Machine theme, unfamiliar or forgotten devices become the "ghosts" on your network.

SpecterLAN does not assume an unknown device is dangerous. It shows what changed and lets you identify the device yourself.

It also includes Ask the Ghost, where a local AI model explains network changes using evidence collected by SpecterLAN.

Everything runs locally on your device.

Demo

Watch the SpecterLAN demo

The demo shows:

  • A baseline network scan
  • A new device appearing
  • SpecterLAN detecting the change
  • Ask the Ghost explaining the change with local AI
  • A device changing IP while keeping the same identity
  • Another device going offline

SpecterLAN also includes Demo Séance, which uses simulated network data so people can test the full experience without exposing their real network.

Code

GitHub repository:

https://github.com/rishabh-hasija/spectarlan

SpecterLAN is open source and released under the MIT License.

How I Built It

SpecterLAN is built with:

  • Python
  • Streamlit
  • SQLite
  • Ollama
  • Gemma 3 4B as the default local AI model
  • Pydantic
  • Nmap when available, with a local fallback when it is not

Network scanning happens on the user's own device.

Scan history, device names, trust labels, and other information are stored locally in SQLite.

For AI analysis, SpecterLAN sends structured evidence only to the locally running Ollama service.

The default model is gemma3:4b.

SpecterLAN also detects other compatible Ollama models already installed on the device and lets the user select one from a dropdown.

Ask the Ghost does not decide whether a device is malicious.

Instead, SpecterLAN creates evidence records, checks the evidence referenced by the model, and removes unsupported AI output.

Why Does Open Innovation Matter?

Network information is private.

A network scan can contain device names, local addresses, hardware details, and information about devices inside someone's home.

I did not want SpecterLAN to send that information to an external AI API.

Using Ollama and an open-weight model means the AI runs on the same device as SpecterLAN.

There is:

  • No cloud AI
  • No API key
  • No SpecterLAN account
  • No registration
  • No shared database
  • No network history uploaded to a SpecterLAN server

Users can also switch to another compatible local Ollama model.

Even without AI, device discovery, tracking, and scan history continue to work.

This open and local approach keeps the user's network data under their control.

The Spirit of It

Devices appear, disappear, return, and sometimes change addresses.

SpecterLAN treats those changes like apparitions on your network.

A forgotten device might suddenly appear again. A new device might show up without a recognizable name. Ask the Ghost helps explain what SpecterLAN observed.

The Halloween theme is part of the experience, while the findings remain evidence-based.

Unknown does not mean malicious.

Prize Categories

Best Use of Gemma

SpecterLAN uses Gemma 3 4B through Ollama as its default local AI model.

Gemma powers Ask the Ghost, which explains changes between saved network scans using structured local evidence.

The model runs on the user's own device, so private network information does not need to be sent to a hosted AI provider.

Make Playwright Email Tests Explain Their Waits

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Email verification tests often fail in a confusing way: the browser is on the right page, the message eventually arrives, and the test still reports only Timeout 30000ms exceeded.

That error is not a diagnosis. It does not tell you whether the application never sent the message, the inbox query used the wrong address, the provider was slow, or the assertion looked for an old message. When an email test is part of a Playwright suite, the wait itself should leave evidence.

The symptom: a test that waits but cannot explain why

A common first draft looks roughly like this:

await page.getByRole('button', { name: 'Create account' }).click(); await inbox.waitForMessage({ subject: 'Verify your email' }); await expect(page.getByText('Account verified')).toBeVisible();

The code is readable, but waitForMessage may hide several different operations. It may create an inbox, poll a mailbox, filter messages, and extract a link. If all of those steps share one timeout, the failure has no useful boundary.

I prefer a test to answer four questions: which mailbox was allocated, when was the message expected, what messages were observed, and why did the final candidate pass or fail? This is especially important when a team searches for phrases such as “temp mail so” or “temp mail email” while comparing test-inbox options. The search phrase is not a test contract; the observable message is.

Model the email flow as observable states

Treat the inbox interaction as a small state machine rather than one long sleep:

  1. allocated: the test owns a unique address.
  2. triggered: the application accepted the signup request.
  3. observed: at least one message was returned.
  4. matched: the message has the expected sender, subject, and correlation value.
  5. consumed: the verification link was opened once.

Each state should have a timestamp and a small diagnostic payload. If a run reaches observed but never reaches matched, you have a filtering or application-content problem. If it never reaches observed, investigate delivery, the sender, or the inbox service. The distinction saves a surprising amount of re-running.

Build a bounded inbox probe

The probe should poll at a fixed interval, stop at a deadline, and return the messages it saw. Do not use an unbounded retry helper that turns every failure into “try again.” A bounded probe makes the delivery timeout a visible part of the test design.

type ProbeResult = { state: 'matched' | 'timed-out'; messageId?: string; seenSubjects: string[]; }; async function waitForVerification( inbox: InboxClient, address: string, token: string, deadlineMs = 20_000, ): Promise<ProbeResult> { const started = Date.now(); const seenSubjects = new Set<string>(); while (Date.now() - started < deadlineMs) { const messages = await inbox.list({ to: address }); for (const message of messages) { seenSubjects.add(message.subject); if (message.text.includes(token)) { return { state: 'matched', messageId: message.id, seenSubjects: [...seenSubjects] }; } } await new Promise((resolve) => setTimeout(resolve, 1_000)); } return { state: 'timed-out', seenSubjects: [...seenSubjects] }; }

The exact inbox client will differ, but the shape is portable. Notice that the probe returns subjects even on timeout. A timeout with an empty list points in a different direction than a timeout where three unrelated messages arrived.

Capture evidence at every decision

Attach the probe result to the Playwright test, along with the allocated address, correlation token, and trigger timestamp. Keep the message body out of ordinary logs if it can contain user data; a subject, message ID, and redacted excerpt are usually enough.

It also helps to record the application request ID. That gives you a join between browser actions, server logs, and inbox data. For CI, save this small JSON object as an artifact:

{ "address": "run-1842@example.test", "triggeredAt": "2026-10-31T11:20:00Z", "state": "timed-out", "seenSubjects": ["Welcome"], "requestId": "signup-7f2a" }

This same one-change-set thinking for CI infrastructure is useful when the test depends on more than the browser; a related example is one-change-set thinking for CI infrastructure. The goal is not to collect every log, but to preserve the smallest chain of evidence that explains a failure.

A Playwright example

The test can now make the failure boundary explicit:

const mailbox = await inbox.allocate(`pw-${test.info().workerIndex}`); const token = crypto.randomUUID(); await page.getByLabel('Email').fill(mailbox.address); await page.getByLabel('Signup token').fill(token); await page.getByRole('button', { name: 'Create account' }).click(); const result = await waitForVerification(inbox, mailbox.address, token); await test.info().attach('email-probe.json', { body: JSON.stringify({ address: mailbox.address, token, result }, null, 2), contentType: 'application/json', }); expect(result.state, `Inbox subjects: ${result.seenSubjects.join(', ')}`).toBe('matched');

If several tests can trigger the same mailbox, queue email checks instead of duplicating them, as shown in queue email checks instead of duplicating them. A unique lease per test is simpler, but queueing can be the safer option when the provider has strict limits.

Failure analysis checklist

When this test fails, check the evidence in this order:

  • No messages observed: verify the sender accepted the request, the test address is correct, and the inbox provider is reachable from CI.
  • Messages observed, no match: inspect subject filters, token generation, and whether an older message was selected.
  • Match found, link fails: check URL environment, link expiry, and whether the link was already consumed by a retry.
  • Only parallel runs fail: look for reused addresses, shared inbox filters, or a race in cleanup.
  • Failures appear randomly: compare delivery latency and polling deadlines before adding retries.

Small search typos such as temp gamil com should stay out of selectors and assertions. They can be useful as plain-text content only when you are explicitly testing search or input normalization; otherwise they add noise.

Keep the wait boring and repeatable

Reliable email testing is less about finding a magic timeout and more about giving every wait a purpose. Allocate a mailbox, trigger one action, poll within a deadline, record what appeared, and assert on a correlation value. The test don't need to be clever; it needs to make the next failure understandable.

That workflow turns flaky email verification from a vague browser timeout into a bounded QA signal. Once the evidence is stable, you can tune delivery budgets with confidence instead of hiding the problem behind another retry.

n8n 3.0 removes the Function node, and published workflows stop activating

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

n8n 3.0.0 shipped as a beta on 27 October, stable lands in early December, and it is an operational release rather than a feature release. Around three dozen legacy nodes go away, including Function, Function Item, Item Lists, Cron and Interval.

The dangerous sentence is in n8n's own docs: "Workflows that use a removed node fail when they run. If such a workflow is published, it doesn't activate when n8n 3.0 starts, so its triggers stop firing."

No red execution. No retry. No email. The workflow is simply not active, and you find out from a customer.

The mapping

Removed in 3.0 Use instead -------------------- ---------------------------------------------- Function Code node, Run Once for All Items Function Item Code node, Run Once for Each Item Item Lists Split Out, Aggregate, Sort, Limit, Remove Duplicates, Summarize Cron, Interval Schedule Trigger HTML Extract current node version iCalendar current node version Binary file nodes current node versions

Some migrations are automatic (the AI Transform node becomes a Code node with the same generated JavaScript). Function and Function Item are not. You replace those yourself.

Find them in ten minutes

  1. Settings, then Migration Report. Exists from 2.39 onwards, and it lists the affected workflows, nodes and settings on your own instance. Save a copy.
  2. On an older version, upgrade to 2.39 first. The report is worth one small, reversible upgrade.
  3. Count the node types yourself. Export workflows through /api/v1/workflows or the CLI and search the JSON for n8n-nodes-base.function, functionItem, itemLists, n8n-nodes-base.cron, n8n-nodes-base.interval.
  4. Write the list down with workflow name, node and purpose. That list is your upgrade plan and your rollback argument.

Fix on 2.x, not after

The Function to Code conversion is the only real work, because the execution model changes:

// Function (legacy): input is items, you return an array of items return items.map(item => ({ json: { ...item.json, seen: true } })); // Code, Run Once for All Items: read all input, return an array const items = $input.all(); return items.map(item => ({ json: { ...item.json, seen: true } }));

For Function Item, use Code with Run Once for Each Item: the input is a single item and you return that item's new data.

Test each migrated workflow on 2.x, run it once manually, check the output shape the next node expects, then publish. Then upgrade, with a backup, because 3.0 cannot be rolled back.

The check nobody does after upgrading

Open the workflow list and look at the active toggle on every workflow, not only the ones you migrated. A workflow that lost activation looks identical to a healthy one in a screenshot. The list is the only place the difference shows, and only if you look.

Two Redis Ports, One TLS Boundary

A Redis Cluster node can expose a client port and a cluster bus port. The first carries application commands; the second carries node-to-node cluster traffic. Enabling TLS only for application clients leaves a second network path that deserves its own review.

The cluster bus is a separate path

Redis documentation describes each cluster node as needing a client TCP port, commonly 6379, and a cluster bus port. The bus port is used for cluster communication. It is not an ordinary application endpoint, even when it is reachable from the same network segment.

The TLS documentation states that tls-cluster yes enables TLS for the cluster bus and cross-node connections when Redis Cluster is used. Replication links are another communication path. Teams should check the relevant replication setting as well if replicas are part of the deployment.

The practical consequence is that a network scan can find two listeners and report different protocol behavior. A client certificate policy on the application endpoint does not automatically describe the cluster bus. Inventory both ports, the listening addresses, and the certificate or key material used by each node.

Shared names are not shared trust

Cluster nodes need a consistent view of node identity and reachability. Certificate names, client authentication requirements, and address announcements can affect both security and cluster formation. Changing a certificate can break a node's connection to peers even when ordinary clients still connect.

Use a test cluster with the same node count and network layout as production, if possible. Verify peer discovery, failover, and a rolling restart. Check that a node rejects a client certificate it should not trust. Then check cluster traffic from an address that should not be a member. Test both client and peer paths before treating the rollout as complete.

Avoid placing files with cluster credentials or private keys in a container layer that is copied to every role. Mount them with the right ownership and rotate them through a process that accounts for all nodes. Keep the private key out of logs and command arguments.

Announcement addresses need review

Redis Cluster is sensitive to how nodes announce addresses and ports. A setting that works inside one Docker network may produce an address that remote clients or peers cannot reach. This can lead an operator to broaden firewall rules until the cluster works, which is a poor way to discover the intended network boundary.

Record the announced address, client address, and bus address separately. Compare them with the DNS names and network policies used by applications. If a proxy or NAT layer changes the path, document the translation and test from both sides.

A rollout checklist

  1. Confirm that the client and bus ports are both in the asset inventory.
  2. Review tls-cluster and replication TLS settings on every node.
  3. Verify certificate names, trust roots, and client-authentication policy.
  4. Test peer join, failover, and restart on a disposable cluster.
  5. Test denied peer and client paths from outside the intended networks.
  6. Keep a rollback node configuration and a certificate rollback path.

After rollout, monitor for nodes that repeatedly join and leave or report certificate errors. A cluster that appears healthy from one node can still have one peer using a weaker path. The useful evidence is a complete map of listeners and a tested rejection for each path, with no unencrypted bus ports left behind.

  • Redis cluster specification

Ollie raised $7.5 million in September and shuts down on 6 November

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A privacy-first family assistant told users on Friday that it closes on 6 November. That is about a week of notice, and six weeks after it announced a $7.5 million seed round led by Khosla Ventures.

Ollie is worth ten minutes of your attention, and not because a startup failed. Because of the shape of it.

What it was

San Diego based, text-message first: no app to install, just a number you message. It connected to calendars and email, and handled the unglamorous part of a household, schedules, meals, to-dos, groceries, appointments, payments. For logins it opened a cloud browser session and sent you a link rather than asking for your password.

It also held SOC 2, the audit standard that says a company handles data to a documented process. In a category where "we care about privacy" is usually a paragraph in a pitch deck, that certificate was the product's argument.

The part developers should notice

A funded company with a compliance certificate, a subscription product and real users closed six weeks after announcing the round. Users got a week.

There is a structural reason assistant products are hard. The big platforms ship a general assistant for free, with the calendar, the phone and the mail already inside it. A startup has to be better at one narrow job, charge for it, and pay per token for every conversation. Privacy is a real differentiator, but it is expensive to prove and it does not reduce the token bill.

If you build on top of small vendors

Three habits that cost nothing and save a weekend:

1. Export weekly if a tool holds your calendar, receipts or requests, keep a local copy 2. Keep a fallback one script or spreadsheet that can do the job badly but without the vendor 3. Read the notice check how much warning the terms give before a shutdown

The third one is the one people skip. A week of notice is normal in this market, not an edge case.

The same week, the money side

On 30 October the FT reported that OpenAI had cut its latest round from up to $30 billion to about $20 billion, four days after lowering the valuation it was asking from $1.4 trillion to $1.25 trillion.

Read those two stories together and you get the quarter: capital became more careful, and small subscription products feel it first. A seed round announces the intention to build; it does not announce the runway.

Our maintenance mode is one boolean in a TypeScript file, and it taught us that our sign-in pages are not public

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Nakodo has a maintenance mode. The whole of it is this file, src/lib/maintenance.ts, 15 lines:

// On while the database is down for maintenance; off again, and deployed, once // it's back. While on: // - the app and the sign-in pages show /maintenance instead (src/proxy.ts), so // nobody can sign up, sign in or change anything; // - the crons skip their runs (src/lib/cron.ts); the first run after it's off // picks the work up where it was left; // - Stripe's and Resend's webhooks answer 503, so they send the event again // later, and the other endpoints that need the database answer 503 too; // - creators' opt-out and unsubscribe links say to come back later. // The public pages stay up: they never touch the database. export const MAINTENANCE = false; export function maintenanceResponse(): Response { return new Response("Down for maintenance", { status: 503, headers: { "Retry-After": "600" } }); }

No feature flag service, no environment variable, no row in a table. A constant, read in ten places, and changing it is a commit and a deploy.

It has been true once, on 12 October 2026, for 23 minutes. Both flips are in the history:

8efb633 2026-10-12 15:05:09 Add maintenance mode for database downtime 5bd22d3 2026-10-12 15:28:33 Disable maintenance mode

The ten call sites

Writing a kill switch is easy. Deciding what it means is the work, and it means something slightly different in each of the ten places that read it.

The proxy, first, because it covers everything a browser asks for:

if (MAINTENANCE) { const path = request.nextUrl.pathname; if (isPublicPath(path) && !isSignInPath(path)) return NextResponse.next(); if (path.startsWith("/api/")) return maintenanceResponse(); return NextResponse.rewrite(new URL("/maintenance", request.url)); }

Three answers in three lines: public pages carry on, API routes get the 503, everything else is rewritten to a page that says we will be back. A rewrite rather than a redirect, so the URL the visitor came for stays in the address bar and works when they reload it later.

The cron jobs, through one function:

export async function cronSkipReason(): Promise<string | null> { if (MAINTENANCE) return "maintenance"; try { await withTimeout(db.execute(sql`select 1`), 10_000, () => new Error("timed out")); return null; } catch (e) { console.error("cron: the database isn't answering, skipping this run", e); return "database unreachable"; } }

Two reasons to skip, and the flag is the cheap one: it costs no round trip. The important property is that a skipped run starts nothing, so nothing is left half done, and the queue is picked up by the next run as if the gap had not happened. Two of the four crons run every five minutes, so a 23-minute window is about nine skipped runs between them.

The two webhook endpoints, which get the 503 for a specific reason. Both Stripe and our mail provider retry a failed delivery with backoff, so a 503 means "ask again" and the event is not lost. A 200 with the body thrown away would be a lost subscription change or a lost bounce. This is the one place where answering with an error is the careful behaviour and answering successfully is the destructive one.

The two public pages behind email links, the opt-out page and the unsubscribe page, which render a different component:

if (MAINTENANCE) return <BackSoon />;

These are the ones I care most about, because the person clicking them did not choose a moment. They are 20 lines of "this link cannot do anything right now, open it again in a little while", with no form and no database read, and they are the reason the one-click endpoints answer 503 rather than pretending: a mail client that gets a 503 from a one-click unsubscribe will show the user that it failed, which is correct, instead of telling them they are unsubscribed when nothing was written.

The refactor it forced

The commit that added all this also changed a file that has nothing to do with maintenance, and that change is the most durable thing to come out of the feature:

const PUBLIC_PREFIXES = [ ... - "/login", - "/forgot-password", - "/reset-password", - "/auth/", ]; +const SIGN_IN_PREFIXES = ["/login", "/forgot-password", "/reset-password", "/auth/"]; + export function isPublicPath(path: string): boolean { - return PUBLIC_EXACT.has(path) || PUBLIC_PREFIXES.some((p) => path.startsWith(p)); + return PUBLIC_EXACT.has(path) || [...PUBLIC_PREFIXES, ...SIGN_IN_PREFIXES].some((p) => path.startsWith(p)); +} + +// The sign-in flow: public, but it needs the database. +export function isSignInPath(path: string): boolean { + return SIGN_IN_PREFIXES.some((p) => path.startsWith(p)); }

Before that commit, "public" meant one thing in this codebase: no session required. The auth boundary only ever asked that question, so one list was enough.

Maintenance mode needs a different question: does this page need the database. Those two sets overlap but are not the same, and the login page is exactly where they come apart. It is public, anybody can open it, and it is useless while the database is down, because signing in writes a session and reads a profile.

So now there are two predicates. isPublicPath answers the auth question and still includes the sign-in pages. isSignInPath carves out the four prefixes that are public but database-dependent. The proxy's maintenance branch is the only caller that needs both, and the line reads exactly like the sentence that justifies it: public, and not sign-in.

I would not have found that distinction by thinking about it. I found it by needing the app to be half up.

The page that refuses to exist while the app is up

export default function MaintenancePage() { if (!MAINTENANCE) redirect("/login"); ...

The maintenance page guards itself, which means the flag is checked twice on the same request: once in the proxy to decide to rewrite there, once in the page to decide whether it is allowed to render.

That is not belt-and-braces for its own sake. Without the guard, /maintenance would be a real, reachable, indexable page claiming the app is down, at all times, for anybody who guessed the URL. With it, the URL only means anything during a window.

You can check the current state from outside:

$ curl -sI https://nakodo.app/maintenance | grep -i location location: /login?next=%2Fmaintenance

That redirect is actually the other layer talking. /maintenance is not in the public path list, so a signed-out request to it is sent to the login page by the normal auth check before the page runs at all. A signed-in visitor gets there and the page's own guard sends them to the same place. Two different mechanisms, one destination, and either way the answer is "there is nothing here right now", which is the truth.

Why a constant and not a flag service

The obvious objection to a committed boolean is that turning it on requires a build. On this project that is a deploy of a couple of minutes, and it has a property I have come to want: the switch leaves a commit, with a timestamp and a message, in the same history as everything else. The two lines above are a complete incident record.

The alternatives I did not take, and why:

  • an environment variable would also need a redeploy on our host for a running deployment to see it, so it costs the same couple of minutes and leaves no record;
  • a flag in Redis would be instant, and would put the switch in a second service that can have its own bad day, while the thing we are defending against is the database being unavailable;
  • a flag in the database is obviously circular.

What a constant cannot do is be flipped by somebody who cannot deploy, or be flipped automatically when the database goes down on its own. The second one is a real gap, and it is partly covered by the select 1 in cronSkipReason above: the background work stops by itself when the database stops answering, whether or not anybody set the flag. The pages behind the login do not, and a visitor during an unplanned outage gets an error boundary rather than the polite page. If I were to extend this, that is the piece I would build next, and it would not be a nicer switch: it would be the proxy noticing.

What stays up

The 76 pages in the sitemap are unaffected by any of this, and the schema comment that promises it is checkable:

$ grep -rl "@/db" "src/app/(marketing)" src/content | wc -l 0

Not one file under the marketing routes or the content directory imports the database. The guides, the pricing page, how it works and every niche page are built from arrays in the repository at deploy time, so they are not "up because the database is up", they are up because they have never needed it. That is what the proxy's first branch is protecting: during the 23 minutes in October the public half of the site was serving exactly what it serves now.

That is the real shape of a maintenance mode, and it is mostly not the switch. It is knowing, per route, which of the two questions you are asking: does this need a user, or does this need the data.

The Role of Artificial Intelligence and Inkjet Printers in Smart 3D Panel Manufacturing: From Quality Control to Production Accuracy

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Introduction

The construction industry is gradually moving toward smarter and more efficient manufacturing methods. As demand for lightweight, durable, and energy-efficient building materials increases, manufacturers are looking for technologies that can improve production accuracy while reducing waste and operational costs. One product that has gained attention in modern construction is the 3D wall panel, also known as the 3D panel.

These panels typically consist of an expanded polystyrene (EPS) foam core positioned between two layers of welded steel wire mesh. Diagonal steel connectors pass through the foam core, creating a three-dimensional structure. Once installed, the panels are generally covered with concrete or shotcrete to form a strong building element.

Although the basic manufacturing process is well established, modern technologies can make it more precise and easier to manage. Artificial intelligence (AI) and industrial inkjet printers are two technologies that offer valuable opportunities in this field. AI can support production monitoring and quality inspection, while inkjet printing provides an efficient method for marking products with important manufacturing information.

Understanding how these technologies work together helps explain the potential of smart manufacturing in the 3D panel industry.

Understanding the 3D Panel Manufacturing Process

Before exploring the role of AI and inkjet printing, it is important to understand how 3D panels are manufactured.

The production process usually begins with preparing the EPS foam core. The foam is cut into specific dimensions according to the required panel design. Steel wire mesh is then positioned on both sides of the foam, and diagonal connectors are installed to connect the two mesh layers.

The final structure must meet several technical requirements, including accurate dimensions, appropriate wire spacing, reliable connections, and consistent foam density. Even small manufacturing variations can affect installation, material consumption, and the overall performance of the finished panel.

For this reason, quality control is an essential part of the production process. Manufacturers need reliable methods to monitor each stage, identify defects, and maintain consistent product specifications.

How Artificial Intelligence Improves 3D Panel Production

Artificial intelligence can help manufacturers analyze production data, recognize patterns, and identify potential problems more efficiently. Instead of relying exclusively on manual inspections, production facilities can use AI-supported systems to monitor different manufacturing stages.

1. Automated Quality Inspection

One of the most promising applications of AI is automated visual inspection. Cameras installed along production lines can capture images of finished panels and send them to computer vision systems for analysis.

AI models trained on relevant production images may identify visible issues such as:

  • Irregular foam dimensions
  • Missing or misplaced wire connections
  • Uneven wire mesh positioning
  • Damaged foam surfaces
  • Inconsistent panel geometry

These systems can help operators detect defects earlier and reduce the likelihood of defective products reaching the packaging stage.

However, AI inspection should complement, rather than replace, technical testing and established quality control procedures. Structural performance still requires appropriate engineering assessments and material testing.

2. Improving Cutting Accuracy

The EPS foam core must be cut according to specific measurements. Inaccurate cutting can create difficulties during mesh installation and lead to unnecessary material waste.

AI-based production systems can analyze cutting data and identify patterns associated with measurement errors. When integrated with suitable sensors and automated cutting equipment, these systems can support more consistent production.

For example, a smart manufacturing system may detect repeated dimensional deviations and alert operators before the problem affects a large batch of panels.

3. Predictive Maintenance for Manufacturing Equipment

Unexpected equipment failures can interrupt production and increase operational costs. Cutting machines, welding equipment, conveyors, and other production components require regular maintenance.

AI-powered predictive maintenance systems can analyze information such as machine temperature, vibration, operating speed, and production history. By recognizing unusual patterns, they may help maintenance teams identify potential equipment problems before a breakdown occurs.

This approach can improve production planning, reduce unexpected downtime, and support more stable manufacturing operations.

4. Reducing Material Waste

Efficient material consumption is important for manufacturers working with EPS foam and steel wire. Incorrect cutting, defective welding, and repeated production adjustments can increase waste.

AI can analyze production records and help identify the stages where material losses occur most frequently. Manufacturers can use these insights to adjust production settings, improve cutting plans, and reduce avoidable errors.

The result can be a more controlled manufacturing process with better resource utilization.

The Role of Industrial Inkjet Printers in 3D Panel Manufacturing

While AI focuses on data analysis and production optimization, industrial inkjet printers provide a practical solution for product identification and traceability.

During manufacturing, panels need to be identified using information that helps operators, distributors, and construction teams distinguish between different products and production batches.

Industrial inkjet printers can print variable information directly onto suitable product surfaces, labels, or packaging materials without requiring physical contact with the printing surface.

Depending on the production requirements, manufacturers can use these systems to print:

  • Production dates
  • Batch and serial numbers
  • Product codes
  • Manufacturer identification
  • Technical specifications
  • QR codes and barcodes

This information makes it easier to organize inventory, track production batches, and manage product distribution.

1. Printing Production Dates and Batch Numbers

Production date and batch identification are important for maintaining manufacturing records. When each batch receives a unique identification code, manufacturers can connect finished products to their corresponding production information.

For example, if a quality issue is discovered after delivery, the manufacturer can use the printed batch number to identify the relevant production period and investigate the manufacturing records.

An industrial inkjet printer can automate this marking process, reducing the need for manual labeling and helping maintain consistent identification across large production volumes.

2. Contactless Printing on Different Materials

One advantage of industrial inkjet technology is its ability to print without direct contact with the product surface. This can be useful when marking packaging materials or suitable surfaces that are difficult to process using traditional contact-based printing methods.

Manufacturers can select an appropriate ink and printing system according to the material, surface condition, production speed, and environmental requirements.

For 3D panels {تری دی پنل }, printing on packaging, protective films, labels, or designated marking areas may be more practical than printing directly onto the foam or steel mesh.

3. Supporting Product Traceability

Traceability allows manufacturers to follow products throughout the supply chain. A printed barcode or QR code can connect a physical panel or package to a digital record containing information such as product dimensions, production batch, manufacturing date, and inspection status.

This approach can make warehouse operations more organized and help construction companies verify product information before installation.

Combining AI and Inkjet Printing for Smart Manufacturing

The real potential of these technologies becomes clearer when they are integrated into a connected production system.

Imagine a manufacturing facility where AI-supported cameras inspect each panel after assembly. The system checks visible characteristics and sends inspection results to a production management platform. Once a panel passes the required checks, an industrial inkjet printer marks its packaging with a unique batch number or QR code.

The printed code can then connect the physical product to its digital manufacturing record.

This workflow offers several advantages:

Improved production tracking: Each batch can be associated with manufacturing and inspection information.

Faster identification: Operators can identify products without manually checking multiple documents.

Better quality management: Inspection records can help manufacturers investigate recurring defects.

More organized inventory: Product codes make warehouse classification and distribution easier.

Reduced manual operations: Automated marking and inspection can reduce repetitive tasks.

However, successful integration requires compatible software, reliable communication between equipment, suitable printing hardware, and a clearly defined data management process.

Important Factors When Choosing an Industrial Inkjet Printer

Selecting the right industrial inkjet printer depends on the specific requirements of the production facility. Not every printing system is suitable for every surface or manufacturing environment.

Several factors should be considered before purchasing a machine.

Printing Speed

The printer must operate at a speed compatible with the production line. A system that cannot keep up with manufacturing output may create bottlenecks.

Printing Surface

Manufacturers should determine whether information will be printed on packaging film, cardboard, labels, or another material. Ink compatibility and adhesion are important considerations.

Print Quality

Small production codes, barcodes, and QR codes must remain readable. The printer should provide sufficient resolution and consistent printing performance for the intended application.

Maintenance Requirements

Industrial environments may contain dust, vibration, and changing temperatures. Choosing equipment with appropriate protection, accessible maintenance components, and reliable technical support can help reduce operational interruptions.

Integration with Production Systems

For smart manufacturing, the printer should be compatible with production management software and other automation equipment where required. This makes it easier to synchronize product identification with manufacturing data.

The Future of Smart 3D Panel Manufacturing

The development of smart manufacturing is creating new opportunities for producers of construction materials. Artificial intelligence, machine vision, industrial automation, and digital identification systems can help manufacturers improve visibility across their production operations.

In the future, AI systems may become more capable of analyzing production conditions, identifying complex defects, and recommending adjustments based on historical manufacturing data. At the same time, industrial inkjet printers and digital coding systems can make product identification faster and more reliable.

These developments do not eliminate the importance of skilled operators, engineering standards, or physical quality testing. Instead, they provide additional tools that help manufacturers make better decisions and maintain more consistent production processes.

For companies producing 3D panels, investing in suitable automation technologies can be a practical step toward improving efficiency and strengthening quality management.

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence and industrial inkjet printing serve different but complementary purposes in modern 3D panel manufacturing. AI supports production analysis, automated inspection, predictive maintenance, and material optimization. Industrial inkjet printers simplify product marking by printing production dates, batch numbers, product codes, and other identification information.

When these technologies are connected through a well-designed manufacturing system, they can improve traceability, reduce manual work, and provide better control over production operations.

As construction material manufacturing continues to evolve, the combination of intelligent software and reliable industrial equipment is likely to become increasingly valuable for companies seeking greater accuracy, efficiency, and consistency.

Aggressor-Side Classification in Order-Flow Indicators: Quote Rule, Tick Rule, and Unknown Trades

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A trade print tells us that a transaction occurred at a particular price and size. It does not always tell us whether the buyer or seller initiated it. Yet footprint charts, bid/ask volume displays, and cumulative delta all depend on assigning trades to a side. A classification error made at ingestion can propagate into every downstream visualization.

There is no universally correct fallback for missing or stale quote data. The useful engineering question is not simply “Was this a buy or a sell?” It is “What evidence supports this classification, and what do we do when that evidence is incomplete?”
Aggressor side is not trade direction
Every executed trade has a buyer and a seller. “Buyer-initiated” generally means an incoming order crossed the spread to execute against resting sell liquidity; “seller-initiated” means the analogous action against resting buy liquidity. It does not mean that the buyer knows the future direction of the market or that a buy signal has appeared.

Some feeds provide an aggressor-side indicator. Others provide trades and quotes that let you infer one. Verify the feed's documented semantics before treating either as ground truth. Equities may require a consolidated best bid/offer; a futures feed may supply venue-specific inside quotes. These inputs are not interchangeable without a data-policy decision.
Method 1: The quote rule
When an appropriate bid/ask snapshot can be aligned with the trade, a common classification rule is:

  • Trade at or above the contemporaneous ask: buyer-aggressed candidate.
  • Trade at or below the contemporaneous bid: seller-aggressed candidate.
  • Trade inside the spread, or with inconsistent quotes: unknown. “Contemporaneous” does a lot of work here. Quote and trade messages may be delivered on different channels, arrive out of order, or use timestamps with different precision. Comparing a trade with the quote that happens to be most recently received can silently contaminate the result. Validate source, ordering, quote age, and whether the quote was actually applicable at execution time.

If both comparisons appear true because the market is locked or crossed, do not let branch order arbitrarily pick a winner: flag the quote as unusable under your documented policy.
Method 2: The tick rule
When reliable quote context is unavailable, the tick rule uses the change from the previous trade price:

  • Higher than the previous print → buy candidate.
  • Lower than the previous print → sell candidate.
  • Same price → apply an explicit zero-tick policy, such as retaining the previous nonzero price-change direction, or mark unknown. This is a heuristic, not a substitute for a contemporaneous quote or an exchange-provided aggressor flag. It can behave poorly when spreads are wide or the tape contains gaps or reordering. In a report or indicator settings panel, name the method rather than simply labeling the output “true buy volume.” A classification function with an honest third state The example below is standalone C#-style application logic, not a drop-in NinjaScript indicator. It illustrates a deliberately conservative quote policy, an optional tick-rule fallback, and a separate unknown outcome. enum Side { Buy, Sell, Unknown }

sealed class TradePrint
{
public double Price;
public DateTime TimeUtc;
}

sealed class Quote
{
public double Bid;
public double Ask;
public DateTime TimeUtc;
}

static Side Classify(
TradePrint trade,
Quote quote,
double? previousTradePrice,
Side previousTickDirection,
TimeSpan maxQuoteAge)
{
bool quoteUsable = quote != null
&& quote.Bid > 0
&& quote.Ask > quote.Bid
&& trade.TimeUtc >= quote.TimeUtc
&& trade.TimeUtc - quote.TimeUtc <= maxQuoteAge;

if (quoteUsable) { if (trade.Price >= quote.Ask) return Side.Buy; if (trade.Price <= quote.Bid) return Side.Sell; return Side.Unknown; // Inside spread: no forced side } if (!previousTradePrice.HasValue) return Side.Unknown; if (trade.Price > previousTradePrice.Value) return Side.Buy; if (trade.Price < previousTradePrice.Value) return Side.Sell; return previousTickDirection; // Configurable zero-tick policy

}
The time threshold is a policy input, not a universal constant. Timestamp checks cannot fix unsynchronized providers or missing quote changes. Also, previousTickDirection must be maintained from the previous nonzero price change, not accidentally overwritten with Unknown on every tied print.

A production pipeline should preserve a classification method and ideally a confidence/provenance field alongside the side label. Otherwise, a chart may combine exchange flags, quote-rule guesses, and tick-rule guesses while making them look equally certain.
What should delta do with unknown volume?
For each print, aggregate its size into exactly one of three buckets: buy, sell, or unknown. Then define your displayed delta explicitly:
classified_delta = buy_volume - sell_volume
classified_volume = buy_volume + sell_volume
all_traded_volume = classified_volume + unknown_volume
Unknown volume should not disappear from total traded volume. Conversely, forcing all unknown prints into buy or sell overstates the certainty of delta. Showing an “unclassified %” beside delta can be more honest than a falsely precise histogram.

A useful diagnostic is to compare classification rates by instrument, session, feed, and replay/live mode. A sudden rise in unknown volume may indicate a quote-availability change or timestamp alignment issue—not a change in market sentiment.
Separate ingestion, classification, aggregation, and rendering
Keep these stages independent:

  1. Ingestion: validate timestamps, ordering, trade sizes, and quote provenance.
  2. Classification: apply a selected policy and record the method used.
  3. Aggregation: map prices to integer tick indices and maintain buy/sell/unknown counters without floating-point price-key drift.
  4. Rendering: consume consistent snapshots rather than mutable accumulation buffers. This separation lets you replace a fallback method without rewriting the footprint renderer. It also makes feed comparisons testable: run the same recorded prints through two classification policies and inspect where they disagree.

For wider context on order-book, tape, and delta concepts, see the ICF Market volume and order-flow education series. The classification policy described here is an educational design example, not a claim about any proprietary ICF Market implementation.
The test I would run before shipping
Build a small replay fixture with: normal at-bid and at-ask prints, an inside-spread trade, a locked quote, a stale quote, the first print of a session, and a sequence of same-price trades. Assert that all volume remains accounted for and that the output names its source policy.

Discussion: If you have both a feed-supplied aggressor flag and the quote rule, do you treat one as authoritative, record the disagreement, or surface both?
