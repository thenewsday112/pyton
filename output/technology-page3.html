Digital office communication affects stress, creativity, and perceived tone

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<figure class="wp-block-embed is-type-video is-provider-youtube wp-block-embed-youtube wp-embed-aspect-16-9 wp-has-aspect-ratio"><div class="wp-block-embed__wrapper"> </div></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Office messages used to move on paper.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">People used to type, copy, and file them by hand, but now they arrive in seconds via <a href="https://www.earth.com/lifestyle/email-phrases-annoy-everyone/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">email</a>, Slack, Microsoft Teams, <a href="https://www.earth.com/science/most-people-misjudge-their-own-text-messaging-habits/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">text</a>, and more. Trading these messages can feel like a <a href="https://www.earth.com/science/what-makes-a-conversation-engaging-its-not-what-you-think/">conversation</a>, but that feeling misleads people because the writer hears a tone of voice the reader often does not.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nicholas Epley, a behavioral scientist at the <a href="https://www.uchicago.edu/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">University of Chicago</a>, described the problem when email was a new, fast tool.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;But what&#8217;s different in this medium is…the ease with which we can fire things back and forth. It makes text-based communication seem more informal and more like face-to-face communication than it really is,&#8221; said Epley.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Researchers in <a href="https://cas.okstate.edu/psychology" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">psychology</a>, <a href="https://business.vanderbilt.edu/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">management</a>, and <a href="https://www.vanderbilt.edu/communications/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">communication</a> at universities in the United States have since tested the changes new tools bring to work.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most of their studies are small. Together, they cover how well tone comes through on a screen, how stressed workers feel, and how teams come up with ideas when they aren&#8217;t in the same room.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-memos-were-built-for-filing">Memos were built for filing</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">JoAnne Yates, a management professor emerita at <a href="https://www.mit.edu/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">MIT</a> <a href="https://mitsloan.mit.edu/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Sloan School of Management</a>, studied how American companies built their first internal communication systems. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Her book Control Through Communication follows typewriters, carbon copies, and filing systems as they changed business writing in the late 1800s and early 1900s.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Cabinets that sorted papers by subject worked best for documents covering one subject each. The internal memo grew up alongside them, a one-topic document a company could copy, file, and find again.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Speed came much later. Yates worked with Wanda Orlikowski of MIT and Melissa Mazmanian of the <a href="https://uci.edu/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">University of California, Irvine</a>, on a study of professionals who carried mobile email devices. At first, the devices gave those workers flexibility and peace of mind.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Mobile email also raised everyone&#8217;s expectations for how quickly someone should respond. The team wrote that professionals chose to work &#8220;anywhere, anytime,&#8221; but in practice they ended up working &#8220;everywhere, all the time.&#8221;</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-tone-gets-lost-in-text">Tone gets lost in text</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Epley and psychologist Justin Kruger tested how well tone travels in an experiment with 30 pairs of college students. Each student received a list of 20 short statements and gave each one a sarcastic or a serious tone.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">One student in each pair read the statements into a tape recorder, and the other emailed them. Both groups expected their partners to read the tone right about 78% of the time.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Listeners got it right about 75% of the time. Email readers managed 56%, not much better than a coin flip.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The voice messages came close to what the senders hoped, and the emails fell well short. Still, readers and listeners alike guessed they judged the tone correctly 90% of the time.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Kruger and Epley blamed egocentrism, the difficulty people have seeing things from someone else&#8217;s point of view. A writer hears the intended tone, but the reader sees only words.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;E-mail is fine if you just want to communicate content, but not any emotional material,&#8221; said Epley.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Short replies now carry signals of their own. &nbsp;In a <a href="https://www.binghamton.edu/index.php" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Binghamton University</a> study led by psychologist Celia Klin, 126 undergraduates rated one-word replies such as &#8220;Okay&#8221; and &#8220;Sure.&#8221; A period made those replies seem less sincere in a text message, but not in a handwritten note.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-email-turned-into-a-stress-signal">Email turned into a stress signal</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Stephen Barley and Debra Meyerson of <a href="https://www..stanford.edu" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Stanford University</a> and Stine Grodal of <a href="https://www.bu.edu/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Boston University</a> studied workers at high-tech companies. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Engineers, technical writers and marketers logged every phone call, email and face-to-face conversation over a series of workdays. The team <a href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220520979_E-Mail_as_a_Source_and_Symbol_of_Stress" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">reported in <em>Organization Science</em></a> that people who spent more time handling email felt more overloaded.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">In later interviews, Grodal noted that participants spent about 90% of their discussion on email. Questions about phone calls and meetings drew little emotional response.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;You can actually see it on the screen; it has material properties. And so email becomes your to-do list of the day,&#8221; said Grodal.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Gloria Mark, an informatics professor at the University of California, Irvine, tested the opposite. Her team cut off email for 13 civilian employees at an Army research center outside Boston for five workdays. Heart rate monitors and computer software tracked the workers the whole time.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">With email, the workers switched computer windows about 37 times an hour. Without it, they switched about 18 times, and Mark&#8217;s team used that switching to measure <a href="https://www.earth.com/science/multitasking-can-make-the-brain-blur-useful-and-irrelevant-information/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">multitasking</a>. Their heart rates also varied more during the email break, which the researchers read as a sign of lower stress.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-video-narrows-people-s-focus">Video narrows people&#8217;s focus</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">As more people started <a href="https://www.earth.com/lifestyle/working-from-home-changes-your-daily-habits-in-ways-you-dont-notice/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">working from home</a>, many meetings moved onto video. Melanie Brucks of <a href="https://business.columbia.edu/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Columbia Business School</a> and Jonathan Levav of Stanford University tested what that does to <a href="https://www.earth.com/science/ai-boosts-brainstorming-but-may-slow-the-creative-process/">brainstorming</a>.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The researchers split 602 people into pairs and gave each pair five minutes to list creative uses for a Frisbee or bubble wrap. Some pairs sat together in a rooms, and others worked over video. The video pairs came up with <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04643-y" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">fewer ideas</a>, and fewer creative ones.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Eye tracking pointed to a reason. People on video spent more time looking at their partner and less time looking around the room. They also remembered fewer of the props the researchers had set out, such as a large houseplant.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The same pattern appeared among 1,490 engineers at company sites in five countries, although video didn&#8217;t hurt the next step: picking the best idea.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;When you&#8217;re visually narrowing yourself… you&#8217;re putting blinders on, basically,&#8221; said Brucks.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Video can also wear people out. In a Stanford survey of more than 10,000 people, about 13.8% of women and 5.5% of men said video calls left them very to extremely tired. The team found that the subjects seeing their own faces on screen for hours helped explain that gap.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-off-hours-email-raises-anxiety">Off-hours email raises anxiety</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The pressure doesn&#8217;t end when the workday ends. William Becker of Virginia Tech, Liuba Belkin of <a href="https://www2.lehigh.edu/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Lehigh University</a> and Samantha Conroy of <a href="https://www.colostate.edu/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Colorado State University</a> surveyed full-time employees and their partners about checking email <a href="https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206319890655">outside work hours</a>.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Workers felt more anxious when employers expected them to monitor email at night, even if the workers spent little time actually working, and their partners reported worse health and less relationship satisfaction. After-hours email is a source of <a href="https://www.earth.com/lifestyle/job-stress-is-quietly-disrupting-sleep-for-millions-of-workers/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">job stress</a> that permeates the whole household.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;If the nature of a job requires email availability, such expectations should be stated formally as a part of job responsibilities,&#8221; said Becker.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most of this research predates Slack and Teams becoming everyday tools. The tone experiment used email between college students, and the punctuation study used text messages, so these studies didn&#8217;t test workplace chat apps directly.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Grodal has said her team needs to return to workplaces and test changes such as letting workers skip after-hours replies. For now, those changes remain untested in the field.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The full study was published in the <a href="https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.89.6.925" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank"><em>Journal of Personality and Social Psychology</em></a>.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-how-to-get-involved">How to get involved</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Read the <a href="https://centerforplainlanguage.org/learning-training/tools-training/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Center for Plain Language&#8217;s free guides</a> if you write messages to coworkers. &nbsp;After the General Services Administration redirected the government&#8217;s plain language website in 2025, the nonprofit reposted the Federal Plain Language Guidelines</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Explore the <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/twh/programs" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">NIOSH Total Worker Health</a> program if you help shape workplace policy. The federal program works to make jobs safe and to support workers&#8217; health and well-being.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Follow the <a href="https://www.siop.org/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology</a> for research behind workplace practices. Its members study how people work, and the society shares that research with policymakers.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Try the <a href="https://www.nationaldayofunplugging.com/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">National Day of Unplugging</a> each March. NASA’s Curiosity rover photographed Martian cliffs at dawn, but its project scientist says one layer is the wrong color

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">On August 11, 2026, at 8:30 a.m. local Mars time, NASA&#8217;s Curiosity rover took six pictures of pale crags on the horizon of Mount Sharp. They were stitched together on Earth into one panorama.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The crags are part of the yardang layer. Project scientist Ashwin Vasavada says that layer looks out of place, with the wrong color and oddly tilted rock layers.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.nasa.gov">NASA</a> released the image on October 6, 2026, almost two months after it was taken. Nobody knows yet how the layer formed, and the team hopes to drive up to its base sometime in 2027.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-six-frames-without-white-balance">Six frames without white balance</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">It was sol 4,982 of the mission, the 4,982nd Martian day since landing. The camera was the <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/msl-curiosity/science-instruments/#h-mastcam" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Mastcam</a>, built and still operated by Malin Space Science Systems (<a href="https://www.msss.com/">MSSS</a>) in San Diego.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Unlike most Mastcam images, this panorama was released without white balancing. NASA says that was an artistic choice to keep the early morning look. The ground in front of the rover has a blue cast, and bright light falls on the crags behind.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The agency calls it the most detailed view yet of these distant cliffs. In June, the same hills were only a row of pale shapes at the back of a sol 4,916 frame from Navcam, one of the rover&#8217;s navigation cameras.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Allison Dries-Padilla, a mission operations specialist at Malin Space Science Systems, was on the Mastcam shift for the plan that started that sol. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Her <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-4982-4987-back-to-our-regularly-scheduled-programming/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">mission blog</a> lists a close mosaic of an eroded ridge called &#8220;Los Toldos.&#8221; A second mosaic covered bedrock farther away, called &#8220;Los Ladrillos.&#8221;</p> <div class="wp-block-image"> <figure class="aligncenter size-full"><a href="https://cff2.earth.com/media-2026/2026/10/26074744/mount-sharp-yardang-layer-location.jpg"><img alt="Artist's concept of Gale Crater and Mount Sharp with an ellipse marking the yardang layer on the mountain's northwestern shoulder" class="wp-image-94119" height="300" src="https://cff2.earth.com/media-2026/2026/10/26074745/cropped-mount-sharp-yardang-layer-location.webp" width="300" /></a><figcaption class="wp-element-caption"><em>Artist&#8217;s concept of Mount Sharp, the 3-mile-tall (5-kilometer-tall) mountain Curiosity has been climbing since 2014. The ellipse marks the yardang layer on its northwestern shoulder. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/ESA/DLR/FU Berlin/MSSS. Click image to enlarge.</em></figcaption></figure> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-ten-miles-of-pale-crags">Ten miles of pale crags</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Mount Sharp stands 3 miles (5 kilometers) tall in the middle of Gale Crater. Its yardang layer, a band of ridges carved by wind, runs about 10 miles (16 kilometers) across the northwestern side.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Curiosity has been going up the mountain since 2014. On August 26, 2026, it reached <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/mars/curiosity-reaches-1-kilometer-elevation-gain/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">0.6 miles (1 kilometer) of elevation gain</a> above the crater floor. NASA counts that as the most ever climbed on Mars.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">On August 1 and 2, from a valley nicknamed Valle Grande, the rover shot 323 Mastcam images looking downhill. Those became one panorama of the crater floor and its northern rim, seen through dusty summer air.</p> <div class="wp-block-image"> <figure class="aligncenter size-full"><a href="https://cff2.earth.com/media-2026/2026/10/26091141/valle-grande-gale-crater-floor-crop.jpg"><img alt="Curiosity rover tracks running across sandy ground in Valle Grande toward the hazy floor and northern rim of Gale Crater on Mars" class="wp-image-94285" height="300" src="https://cff2.earth.com/media-2026/2026/10/26091142/cropped-valle-grande-gale-crater-floor-crop.webp" width="300" /></a><figcaption class="wp-element-caption"><em>Crop from the center of the 323-image Mastcam panorama Curiosity took from Valle Grande on Aug. 1 and 2, 2026, looking down Mount Sharp. The rover&#8217;s tracks run into the distance toward the floor and northern rim of Gale Crater, seen through dusty summer air. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/MSSS. Click image to enlarge.</em></figcaption></figure> </div> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The yardangs had been photographed before as well, in an 18-image Mastcam mosaic on November 2, 2024. NASA&#8217;s caption then said the color, texture and tilt of these layers set them apart from the rock lower down.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Catherine O&#8217;Connell-Cooper, of the <a href="https://www.unb.ca/">University of New Brunswick</a> in Canada, is a strategic planner for APXS, the rover&#8217;s Alpha Particle X-ray Spectrometer. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">In a June <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-4913-4919-planetary-explorers-freewheeling-to-the-yardang-unit/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">blog post</a>, she called the yardang unit the next major stop, &#8220;a series of intriguing wind sculpted, pale coloured hills.&#8221; Some rocks on the way stick out at strange angles, she wrote, and that makes drives harder to plan.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-a-gap-in-the-climate-record">A gap in the climate record</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Each layer of Mount Sharp holds a different stretch of Martian history. In the lower ones, Curiosity&#8217;s team found signs of lakes and streams from billions of years ago. Later the water dried up and left salty minerals behind.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">According to NASA, once new sediment stopped settling on the mountain, wind may have stripped some of it away and shaped the yardangs.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">On the shoulder of the mountain where Curiosity is climbing, the yardang layer is the last one at the top. The <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/nasas-curiosity-captures-dawn-breaking-on-wind-carved-cliffs/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">photojournal entry</a> for the panorama says the mountain may have begun eroding before that layer was laid down.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">If that happened, part of the climate record is missing between the yardangs and the rocks beneath them. Curiosity has been reading that record on its way up.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-ash-from-ancient-eruptions">Ash from ancient eruptions</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Vasavada is based at NASA&#8217;s <a href="https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/">Jet Propulsion Laboratory</a> in Southern California, which built the rover and leads the mission for NASA.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;The yardang layer looks out of place. It&#8217;s the wrong color, the layers tilt at an odd angle, and it almost appears plastered on,&#8221; Vasavada said.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;But that&#8217;s what makes it exciting to reach. No one is sure exactly what created this layer, but one idea is it may be ash deposited by ancient volcanic eruptions,&#8221; he added.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The photojournal text points to piles of volcanic ash along the Martian equator as a possible match. It also says outright that scientists are not entirely sure how the layer formed.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-sulfate-rocks-before-the-cliffs">Sulfate rocks before the cliffs</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is the second year of Curiosity&#8217;s fifth extended mission, which began at the <a href="https://www.earth.com/space/mysterious-ridges-on-mars-hint-at-an-unexpected-past/">boxwork ridges</a> that hint at ancient underground water. Those were the <a href="https://www.earth.com/space/strange-spiderweb-rocks-on-mars-hint-at-hidden-groundwater/">spiderweb-shaped rock patterns</a> tied to the timing of water loss on Mars.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Over the next year or so, the rover will cross layers with sulfates and carbonates. NASA treats both as signs of the surface drying out. Lower down on the same mountain, Curiosity had already turned up <a href="https://www.earth.com/space/sulfur-stones-and-spiderwebs-curiositys-latest-discoveries-on-mars/">sulfur stones</a>.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">In the week of the panorama, Curiosity drove 100 feet (30 meters) to a new stop, Dries-Padilla wrote. Its left-front wheel was sitting on a small rock there. The team decided it was not safe to brush the bedrock with the arm.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The next drive was planned at about 154 feet (47 meters) to the southwest. On July 31, a few days before the 14th landing anniversary on August 6, the steep slope tilted the rover to 24 degrees at one point. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Susanne Schwenzer, a planetary mineralogy professor at <a href="https://www.open.ac.uk/">The Open University</a> in the UK, reported it in the blog.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-no-rock-sample-until-2027">No rock sample until 2027</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Every yardang image so far has been taken from far away. The pictures show shape, color and the tilt of the layers, but not what minerals are in the rock.</p> <div class="wp-block-image"> <figure class="aligncenter size-full"><a href="https://cff2.earth.com/media-2026/2026/10/26091144/yardang-unit-mastcam-mosaic-2024.jpg"><img alt="Mastcam mosaic of the pale, layered Yardang Unit ridges on Mount Sharp, Mars, seen from a distance by Curiosity in November 2024" class="wp-image-94287" height="300" src="https://cff2.earth.com/media-2026/2026/10/26091145/cropped-yardang-unit-mastcam-mosaic-2024.webp" width="300" /></a><figcaption class="wp-element-caption"><em>An 18-image Mastcam mosaic of the Yardang Unit, the wind-carved layer high on Mount Sharp, taken by Curiosity from a distance on Nov. 2, 2024. Color is adjusted to match how the scene would look under Earth lighting. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/MSSS. Click image to enlarge.</em></figcaption></figure> </div> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The volcanic ash idea has not been tested on the rock itself, and NASA has given no exact date for the arrival. Designing Cloud-Native Extensions on SAP BTP with CAPM: Patterns for Clean-Core Architectures

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<h2 id="h-key-takeaways">Key Takeaways</h2><p>●&nbsp; Clean core extensibility is when we, as a design choice, decide to go that way per use case: synchronous OData for transactional use cases that require immediate response, asynchronous events for all other use cases.</p><p>● &nbsp; At the beginning of each CAPM project, multi-tenancy issues should be addressed; retrofitting tenant isolation in a single-tenant already deployed extension is a much more disruptive affair than to include the feature from the word go.</p><p>● &nbsp; Principal propagation does not feature in the design of which extensions preserve core system authorization and audit trails’ integrity versus which ones allow them to degrade silently.&nbsp;</p><p>●&nbsp; In CAPM extensions the great production risk is not in the framework at all but in the peripheral issues: destination config, XSUAA scope mapping, CDS to core field mapping which go out of sync across environments.</p><p>●&nbsp; Clean core, which reduces upgrade risk in the ERP core but does not remove operational burden; it shifts versioning, monitoring, and authorization complexity into the BTP environment, which in turn requires the same operational care as the core itself.</p><h2 id="h-executive-summary">Executive Summary</h2><p>SAP’s clean-core strategy tells engineering teams to leave S/4HANA itself largely untouched. Custom logic should run beside it as applications on SAP Business Technology Platform, usually built with the Cloud Application Programming Model (CAPM). That change is not merely structural. Teams now have to deal with distributed-system problems—network requests instead of local function calls, tenant isolation, token-based authorization, and eventual consistency—in a domain long shaped by monolithic customization. This article examines recurring design choices in production CAPM extension projects, including how extensions compose services with the core, how tenants are separated, how SAP Event Mesh carries integration events, and how authorization relies on principal propagation. The discussion comes from hands-on experience designing and building an extension from start to finish, with architects and senior engineers in mind—people who need to make these tradeoffs correctly on the first attempt.</p><h2 id="h-introduction">Introduction</h2><p>For most of the past two decades, extending SAP meant changing the ERP system itself. Teams added ABAP reports, user exits, and Z-tables alongside the vendor’s standard code. The approach worked, but the trade-off was familiar: each custom object could fail during the next upgrade, and the line separating SAP’s code from the company’s own gradually disappeared.</p><p><br /></p><p>Keep the ERP core to stock standards, and put custom logic into a separate cloud native runtime, SAP BTP, which can be stood up or taken down independent of the core’s release cycle [1]. CAPM is what SAP uses for that outer layer: a domain model-first, convention over configuration approach which compiles Core Data Services (CDS) models into OData services, with persistence, authorization and multitenancy mainly handled by generated code [2].</p><p><br /></p><p>The problem is simple: “move the logic to BTP” is a slogan, not an architecture. Accepting it leaves a team to settle the details that improvised projects often mishandle: connecting the extension to the core, serving several business units from one codebase without exposing their data to one another, carrying a user’s identity across the network, and monitoring a service beyond the familiar SAP Basis toolchain. Without a deliberate design, teams tend to rely on synchronous calls, technical users rather than propagated authorization, and single-tenant deployments. The second consumer then arrives, and the design has to be rebuilt. These are the same distributed-systems errors organizations make during a first microservices project—only they reach the SAP ecosystem later.</p><p><br /></p><p>Service design, multi-tenancy, event based integration, and authorization of principals we also look at what goes wrong in production. This article looks at the most important issues:&nbsp;</p><h2 id="h-background">Background</h2><p>Standard configuration and key-user (in-app) extensibility is what we have in S/4HANA; for anything that requires custom code or a new data model we go to side by side extensions on BTP [1], [5]. The RESTful ABAP Programming Model (RAP) is the clean core compatible way to expose logic that must stay in ABAP, which we build on CDS views and which generates OData services with transactional (draft, lock, validation) handling [5], [9].</p><p><br /></p><p>In the world of side-by-side half we have CAPM which is the model-driven framework. A CAPM project puts forth entities and services in CDS; the framework in turn puts out an OData v4 (or v2) service with default persistence and generic CRUD handlers which also has in it hooks for custom logic in Node.js or Java [2], [4]. Also it is typical for CAPM apps to run on Cloud Foundry or Kyma, which in turn is backed by SAP HANA Cloud, and we also see Fiori elements UIs generated from the same CDS annotations.</p><p><br /></p><p>SAP Event Mesh is the managed version of the event-broker pattern which we have in BTP middleware that puts together event delivery between producers and subscribers so their availability and load are put out of each other’s business. It is the typical means for asynchronous integration between the core (which is publishing business events) and extensions that may react without polling.</p><p>XSUAA, the SAP Authorization and Trust Management service, is BTP’s OAuth2 auth server: it issues and issues JWTs which have in them scopes from role collections and is what CAPM apps use for auth and auth requests [6], [7].</p><h2 id="h-problem-analysis">Problem Analysis</h2><p>Four problems recur across CAPM extension projects, independent of industry:</p><p><br /></p><p>Where does the transaction boundary sit? A parallel extension is not in the same unit of work as the S/4HANA tables it depends on. What used to be a single local transaction now requires a synchronous call with its own error handling, or an asynchronous flow which includes eventual consistency. Teams that ignore this design step implement synchronous calls into every operation which in turn caps the extension’s availability to that of the core system.</p><p><br /></p><p>How many tenants does the extension actually serve? In many cases, a single business unit’s extension is also required by a second unit or country within the first year. If tenant isolation was not a primary design issue from the start, then as we go in to retrofit it we are looking at re-provisioning infrastructure and re-testing authorization, which at this point the first tenant is already in production.&nbsp;</p><p><br /></p><p>Whose identity is on the wire? Calling S/4HANA through a broad-privilege technical user is easier than setting up identity federation, which explains its popularity. But it creates a serious authorization flaw: everyone shares one credential, so individual checks disappear and the audit trail points to the technical account rather than the person who made the change.</p><p><br /></p><p>Who is watching the extension once it is live? A CAP service does not inherit the monitoring practices that come with the ABAP stack; a Basis team’s muscle memory for ST22 reports does not translate to Cloud Foundry logs, which in turn must be deliberately wired into an observability pipeline.&nbsp;</p><h2 id="h-architecture-and-design">Architecture and Design</h2><p>In the case of S/4HANA, we maintain minimal custom logic (standard objects, ABAP CDS views, RAP exposed business objects), which in turn has a side-by-side CAPM extension in a BTP subaccount for UI, extended logic, and any data model that does not fit in the ERP schema.</p><p><br /></p><p>Three decisions define this shape.&nbsp;</p><p><br /></p><p>Service composition: The extension connects to the core in two ways. Requests that need an immediate response use OData v2/v4 through the Destination and Connectivity services. Notifications go through SAP Event Mesh instead. In practice, asynchronous communication should be the default; use synchronous calls only when the caller cannot continue without the result, because they tie the extension’s SLA to a system outside its control.</p><p><br /></p><p>Multitenancy: CAPM's @sap/cds-mtxs package implements SAP's standard pattern — one provider subaccount running the extension, an MTX sidecar handling tenant onboarding/upgrade, and one isolated database container per tenant subaccount [3], [10], [12]. This we see a shared application, isolated data model which is more economical than one deployment per tenant and we keep tenant data separate which is a point of note for the compliance reviewers.</p><p><br /></p><p>Authorization boundary: XSUAA-issued JWTs authorize the extension's own endpoints; the Destination service, configured for principal propagation, carries the caller's identity through to the core so OData calls execute — and are authorization-checked and logged — as the actual end user [6], [7], [8].</p><p><img alt="" src="https://cdn.hackernoon.com/images/chowa-ediotor_cf4o4na.png" /></p><p>The tradeoff: this trades in-core simplicity for operational surface area — a second deployable, security model, and monitoring domain, in exchange for upgrade independence. That trade is worth making for most net-new logic; it is not automatically worth making for a small, stable customization cheaper to maintain in place, a point returned to below.</p><h2 id="h-implementation">Implementation</h2><p>The following snippets illustrate the patterns above at the level of real project code.</p><p>Domain model keyed against a core identifier, not a replica of core data:</p><pre class="language-javascript"><code class="language-javascript">// db/schema.cds namespace com.example.extension; using { cuid, managed } from '@sap/cds/common'; &nbsp; entity RiskAssessments : cuid, managed { &nbsp; salesOrder &nbsp; : String(10) @title: 'S/4HANA Sales Order'; &nbsp; riskScore : Decimal(3,0); &nbsp; reviewStatus : String(20) default 'OPEN'; &nbsp; reviewedBy &nbsp; : String(256); &nbsp; notes&nbsp; &nbsp; : String(1000); } </code></pre><p><br /></p><p>The entity carries a plain salesOrder string rather than an association into a replicated copy of the order table — replicating core master data reintroduces exactly the coupling clean core is meant to remove. The extension resolves the current state on demand instead.</p><p><br /></p><p>Synchronous read-through to the core via a remote OData service:</p><pre class="language-javascript"><code class="language-javascript">// srv/external/S4SalesOrder.cds using { API_SALES_ORDER_SRV as external } from './S4SalesOrder.csn'; service S4Service { &nbsp; entity SalesOrders as projection on external.A_SalesOrder; } // srv/risk-service.js const cds = require('@sap/cds'); module.exports = cds.service.impl(async function () { &nbsp; const { RiskAssessments } = this.entities; &nbsp; this.on('READ', RiskAssessments, async (req, next) =&gt; { const results = await next(); const s4 = await cds.connect.to('S4Service'); for (const row of results) { &nbsp; const order = await s4.run( &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; SELECT.one.from('SalesOrders').where({ SalesOrder: row.salesOrder }) &nbsp; ); &nbsp; row.orderStatus = order?.OverallSDProcessStatus; } return results; &nbsp; }); }); </code></pre><p><br /></p><p>cds.connect.to('S4Service') resolves, via configuration, to a destination that carries the inbound user's principal — propagation is configuration, not application code, so the same handler runs correctly whether the destination uses principal propagation or, in a lower environment, a technical user.</p><p><br /></p><p>Reacting to a core event asynchronously, instead of polling:</p><pre class="language-javascript"><code class="language-javascript">// srv/event-listener.js const cds = require('@sap/cds'); module.exports = cds.service.impl(async function () { &nbsp; const messaging = await cds.connect.to('messaging'); &nbsp; messaging.on('sap.s4.beh.salesorder.v1.SalesOrder.Changed.v1', async (msg) =&gt; { const { SalesOrder } = msg.data; await UPDATE(cds.entities.RiskAssessments) &nbsp; .set({ reviewStatus: 'NEEDS_REVIEW' }) &nbsp; .where({ salesOrder: SalesOrder }); &nbsp; }); }); </code></pre><p><br /></p><p>This handler updates the extension's own state and lets a downstream process act on it — decoupling that keeps the extension available even during a core maintenance window.</p><p><br /></p><p>Authorization declared next to the model it protects:</p><pre class="language-javascript"><code class="language-javascript">// srv/risk-service.cds service RiskService @(requires: 'authenticated-user') { &nbsp; entity RiskAssessments as projection on extension.RiskAssessments; &nbsp; annotate RiskAssessments with @( restrict: [ &nbsp; { grant: ['READ'], to: 'RiskViewer' }, &nbsp; { grant: ['*'], to: 'RiskManager' } ] &nbsp; ); } </code></pre><p>RiskViewer/RiskManager map, via xs-security.json, to XSUAA scopes and role collections assigned to real users:</p><pre class="language-javascript"><code class="language-javascript">{ &nbsp; "scopes": [ { "name": "$XSAPPNAME.RiskViewer", "description": "Read risk assessments" }, { "name": "$XSAPPNAME.RiskManager", "description": "Manage risk assessments" } &nbsp; ], &nbsp; "role-templates": [ { "name": "RiskViewer", "scope-references": ["$XSAPPNAME.RiskViewer"] }, { "name": "RiskManager", "scope-references": ["$XSAPPNAME.RiskManager"] } &nbsp; ] } </code></pre><p><br /></p><p>These role templates are what an administrator turns into actual role collections and assigns to users or groups in the BTP cockpit — CDS declares intent, this file is the contract USAA enforces against, and the role collection assignment is the step most likely to drift out of sync with the other two, as the Production Lessons Learned section below discusses.</p><h2 id="h-performance-and-scalability">Performance and Scalability</h2><p>CAPM applications scale the way most stateless Cloud Foundry/Kyma services do: horizontally, with session state kept out of the application tier. The design questions that matter are elsewhere. Read-through calls to the core are the dominant latency cost, not the extension's own logic — a short-lived cache for infrequently changing master data reduces both, with a TTL reflecting how stale the UI may be for that field. One container per tenant which reduces cross-tenant contention but at the same time does require capacity planning against the largest tenant’s needs. Event consumption throughput is a function of the consumer which in turn means that for bursty events there must be sufficient number of consumer instances to handle the volume within the business’s freshness requirement. For bulk operations use OData’s $batch instead of individual calls in a loop which in turn results in one round trip as opposed to hundreds.</p><h2 id="h-security-considerations">Security Considerations</h2><p>Security in this architecture is a matter of the full request path maintaining the same guarantees which in turn each individual element of that path is responsible for and not of any single control.&nbsp;</p><p><br /></p><p>●&nbsp; Token validation is automatic; scope design is not. At each point of entry we require a valid XSUAA JWT, also it is CAPM’s job to check signature, expiry, and audience which we do without custom code but still we have to write out xs-security.json scope definitions carefully because a very wide default scope renders CDS @restrict annotations useless [6].</p><p>● Principal propagation should be the default backend authentication mode, Not an issue that is addressed only as an after thought [7], [8]; any technical or user fallback should be put in place as a documented reviewed exception, not a silent default.</p><p>●&nbsp; Authorization granularity matters more than authentication. OWASP’s API Security Top 10 puts out broken object level authorization and broken function level authorization as its top priority issues [14] which in turn map directly to CDS @restrict annotations that are too broad, or which are absent at all on a generated navigation property.</p><p>●&nbsp; Secrets live in the platform, not the codebase. Destination credentials and API keys go into BTP’s credential store and injected service bindings which also means they should not be in the CDS model or a checked in config file.&nbsp;</p><p>● Transport security needs attention at both ends. BTP by default enforces TLS 1.2+ on inbound traffic; for outbound connection via the Cloud Connector that is the team’s responsibility as far as certificate rotation goes, not the platform’s.</p><p>●&nbsp; Zero-trust framing fits this architecture naturally. NIST SP 800-207 puts forth zero trust access in terms of per session and identity which is a different approach from that of network location [19] we present this model which by design has the application logging and the core no longer on the same trusted network segment.</p><h2 id="h-operational-excellence">Operational Excellence</h2><p>A CAP service is only as operable as the observability wired around it, and BTP does not wire that in for free.</p><p><br /></p><p>●&nbsp; Structured, correlated logging. The Application Logging feature provides for search of logs across instances, but only if correlation IDs travel with each request through every downstream call which includes outbound OData calls to the core [24] without that a single reported issue from a user may not be traced across the full request path.</p><p>●&nbsp; Distributed tracing matters more here than in a typical microservices system, because a meaningful share of "slow" requests are slow in the core, not the extension, and only a trace spanning both sides of the boundary proves which one [21].</p><p>●&nbsp; Alerting should be scoped to actionable symptoms — We see that for elevated error rate, queue backlog, destination authentication failures not all anomalies the platform is technically able to emit; alert fatigue, which leads to practice defeat much faster than any one missed incident.</p><p>● &nbsp; Multitenant upgrade rollout needs a staged path. MTX-powered schema upgrades go out to all enrolled tenants at the same time by default, which is why we require a canary tenant (or a small set) for an upgrade to go through before it is made available to all.</p><p>●&nbsp; HA/DR is the team's own responsibility. For critical business extensions, we need to see a defined RTO/RPO and a proven out-of-band recovery method; living on a cloud platform does not, in and of itself, guarantee resilience.</p><p><br /></p><p><img alt="" src="https://cdn.hackernoon.com/images/chowa-ediotor_uajmgldo.png" /></p><h2 id="h-trade-offs-and-limitations">Trade-offs and Limitations</h2><p>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Clean core, side-by-side extension architecture is what we should go with for new custom logic implementation, but that is not a hard and fast rule. It introduces a network hit and an availability issue which core customizations did not have; in a use case which requires the same transactional consistency as native ABAP logic, forcing it side by side actually trades off upgrade risk for distributed transaction complexity which may not be a clear win. Also, it is not a free ride; each extension is a second thing to secure, patch, and staff. Multi-tenancy issues are a waste of effort for a tool which is very scoped and truly single-tenant, and event-driven integration trades off speed for resilience, which is the wrong trade-off when the business is looking for a sync answer. Small, stable, rarely used custom codes are often better left as minimal in core extensions, then re-platformed for strategic conformity -- clean core is a direction to move logic towards over time, not a reason to do a big bang migration of everything at once.</p><h2 id="h-production-lessons-learned">Production Lessons Learned</h2><p>In many cases we saw two failure patterns which reported back often enough in real CAPM extension builds to be worth going into in detail; we thought at first they were application bugs in either of these and in fact they were not.</p><p><br /></p><p>The "it works in every environment except the one that matters" destination bug. A CAP service may read from S/4HANA without trouble in development and quality assurance, then fail silently—or return a misleading error—when it reaches production. The application code is often fine. More commonly, production has a destination with the wrong authentication setup: basic authentication was configured instead of principal propagation, or a certificate that works in QA was never replaced with the production certificate. Development and QA tests still pass because each one uses its own correctly configured destination. The problem appears only in the environment that no one tests again before go-live. A lasting fix means managing destination configuration like versioned application code. Each change needs review, and every promotion should include a checklist confirming the authentication method and certificate validity for each landscape—not a cockpit setting configured once and then forgotten.</p><p><br /></p><p>The silent authorization drift between the CDS model and the role collections that were actually assigned. A CDS @restrict is a statement of intent which the actual behavior a user sees is based on the role collection which an admin put together in the identity provider and which two which are edited in different tools by different people at different times. Early in an extension's life the mapping between CDS scopes and role collections is usually correct as one team designed both at the same time. Months down the line after a business request for a new field or operation which a developer then adds to the @restrict annotation which he extends the CDS scope for -- the corresponding role collection update is easy to miss what breaks is not immediate but the new feature just does not show up for anyone which is reported as a "bug in the new feature" instead of being identified as an authorization gap. The best fix is a single mapping document which is owned by someone and reviewed with the security team on a set cadence not just at go live so that a CDS change and its corresponding role collection change are always done together instead of independently.</p><p><br /></p><p>An issue we see is that what began as a solution for one team grew to require a second and by that point the migration was much more difficult we had wished we had put tenant awareness in from the beginning. Also we note that Fiori elements really do speed up UI delivery when the backend CDS @UI annotations are used as designed teams which ignore this and go to manual UI coding see no benefit at all out of the framework which they may have thought would be the solution for them.</p><h2 id="h-best-practices">Best Practices</h2><p>●&nbsp; Default to async, event based integration for notifications; use sync OData for interactions that require an immediate response.&nbsp;</p><p>●&nbsp; Multitenancy model to be determined before writing the first line of extension code, which in turn may be for a single tenant launch.</p><p>●&nbsp; Principal propagation to be the default back-end auth mode out of which technical user fall back will be a documented exception.</p><p>● &nbsp; Write out CDS @restrict annotations for what the security team has approved in terms of role collection design, and at a set interval go over that mapping.&nbsp;</p><p>●&nbsp; For destination and connectivity config, version and review the same care that we put into application code.&nbsp;</p><p>● &nbsp; Put in place distributed tracing that spans the extension-to-core boundary from the very first production deployment, not only after the first latency issue.</p><p>●&nbsp; &nbsp; Cache core master data reads with an explicit, business-justified TTL.</p><p>● &nbsp; Treat HA/DR posture as an explicit requirement with a stated RTO/RPO, sized to the process's business criticality.</p><h2 id="h-future-outlook">Future Outlook</h2><p>SAP reports that it is presenting RAP (which is for logic that must stay in ABAP Cloud) and CAPM (which is for logic that belongs side by side) as embodiments of the same clean-core philosophy which we see in the use of CDS as the model language. As cross-system communication trends towards event-based models, extensions which are put together in a synchronous-first approach will see growth in the need to include asynchronous elements. Also, these trends do not change the base principles on which we operate; they, in fact, put into higher value the issues of service composition and multitenancy from the start.</p><h2 id="h-conclusion">Conclusion</h2><p>In a side-by-side layer, a team owns end-to-end, not scattered through a vendor-maintained core. CAPM makes that layer a material improvement over hand-rolled middleware, but it does not do away with the underlying distributed systems issues: service composition, tenant isolation, identity propagation, and observability still have to be designed into the layer. Teams which approach those design issues with the same care as any other production distributed system do in fact end up with extensions that are truly upgrade independent; teams which see CAPM as a drag-and-drop solution tend to learn the hard way that these issues did not go away.</p><h2 id="h-references">References</h2><p>[1] SAP SE, "What Is a Clean Core?" France’s AI Strategy: Turning Models, Compute, and Public Investment Into National Capability

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p>France's potential role in AI rests on its ability to organise a chain of capabilities. Model developers can produce useful technologies, computing institutions can provide infrastructure, and public administrations can establish sustained demand. National capacity grows when these activities become connected through arrangements that work under practical constraints. The decisive question is whether French institutions can secure the resources they need, adapt them to their purposes and remain responsible for the resulting decisions.</p><p>That question matters because the visible scale of an AI strategy can obscure its operational content. In February 2025, the Élysée reported €109 billion in announced investments associated with AI infrastructure and deployment projects. [1] This signalled commitments by investors. It was neither a measure of installed computing capacity nor a statement that the French state had already spent that amount. Assessing France requires following projects and access arrangements beyond the announcement.</p><h2 id="h-models-that-users-can-adapt">Models that users can adapt</h2><p>In the Invention layer, an important distinction concerns what users can actually obtain and modify. Mistral's documentation lists Ministral 3 8B, released in December 2025, as generally available under Apache 2.0. [2] An available model with permissive licensing gives developers a different set of options from a service accessible only through a proprietary interface. It can support adaptation and deployment decisions closer to the institution using it.</p><p>That possibility is not equivalent to a complete operating capability. An administration still needs suitable infrastructure, technical staff, evaluation procedures and authority to use the relevant data. A model that performs well on a general benchmark may require considerable work before it can support an administrative task reliably. The French opportunity is to develop those supporting capacities around models that institutions can understand, test and replace.</p><p>Mistral's August 2026 announcement provides a useful illustration of access becoming more specific. The company said its regional endpoints were generally available, allowing customers to select inference in Europe or the United States. It also disclosed that limited, safeguarded transfers to subprocessors outside the selected region could occur. [3] This is evidence of a defined service offering, with stated limits. A European processing location alone cannot establish complete control over the surrounding technical and contractual relationships.</p><h2 id="h-computing-infrastructure-under-shared-authority">Computing infrastructure under shared authority</h2><p>Industrialisation requires infrastructure that can support demanding work at useful scale. Alice Recoque illustrates both the ambition and the institutional complexity. EuroHPC signed its procurement contract in November 2025. The planned system will be owned by EuroHPC and hosted in France, with GENCI managing the project and CEA operating the machine. The Jules Verne consortium includes the Netherlands and Greece, and computing resources are to be allocated jointly in proportion to investment. [4]</p><p>France therefore occupies a significant organisational position without acquiring exclusive national disposal of the system. The planned architecture also combines European components with AMD processors and accelerators. [4] European coordination can enlarge access and influence while retaining external technological dependencies. The relevant policy issue is whether those dependencies remain manageable through procurement, support and continuity arrangements.</p><p>Project timing is equally consequential. GENCI's current project page identifies the end of 2027 as the expected operational date. [5] This profile therefore treats Alice Recoque as contracted future capacity. It does not add its promised performance to a present French inventory. The distinction affects decisions by laboratories and public organisations that need resources before the new system becomes available.</p><h2 id="h-the-state-as-an-organised-user">The state as an organised user</h2><p>Operationalisation becomes more tangible in Albert API. DINUM, France's interministerial digital directorate, operates this shared gateway to generative models. Its official description covers access to open-weight and proprietary models, alongside functions such as document classification, retrieval and optical character recognition. It identifies Albert API as an instance of the state-developed OpenGateLLM software. [6]</p><p>A shared interface can help administrations avoid rebuilding the same connections and can make changing providers less disruptive. Its strategic value depends on the surrounding institutional arrangements: which data may be processed, who evaluates performance, how errors are corrected and who can suspend a failing service. DINUM's page states that an approved version for internal state data is still being finalised. [6] Claims about universal readiness for sensitive public workloads would therefore exceed the evidence.</p><p>This is where governance connects Invention, Industrialisation and Operationalisation. The state can shape demand through procurement specifications and common infrastructure, while retaining responsibility for each public use. An architecture that permits model substitution, independent evaluation and continuity of service would make national capability less dependent on the fortunes of a single supplier. Achieving this requires administrative expertise as well as technical investment.</p><h2 id="h-the-evidence-to-watch">The evidence to watch</h2><p>Four developments would make France's trajectory easier to judge. First, documented acceptance and access milestones for Alice Recoque would establish when contracted infrastructure becomes usable. Second, reports on completed and energised facilities would distinguish delivered investment from announcements. Third, Albert API's authorisation status and service records would clarify which workloads it can responsibly support. Fourth, evaluations of public deployments would reveal whether AI improves accuracy, processing times or service quality at an acceptable cost.</p><p>France could play a substantial European role by connecting model development, shared computing and public demand into dependable arrangements. The strongest evidence would be institutions that can continue to operate, evaluate and adapt their systems when suppliers, prices or political conditions change. They include limb darkening, the dimming toward a star&#8217;s edge, which in these stars also changes the color.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">In the image of WD J1330+6435, the disc center is #D0FFB4, and the green fades toward the rim until the outermost pixels turn orange. WD J2356-2054 stays blue to the edge.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Warwick&#8217;s captions note that real stars would be many millions of times brighter than a screen. Only the hue is right.</p> <div class="wp-block-image"> <figure class="aligncenter size-full"><a href="https://cff2.earth.com/media-2026/2026/10/26091138/white-dwarf-colour-accurate-images-panel.jpg"><img alt="Color-accurate simulated discs of eight white dwarfs, from pale blue Sirius B to orange, pink, salmon, white, yellow, green and blue stars, each with its spectrum" class="wp-image-94281" height="300" src="https://cff2.earth.com/media-2026/2026/10/26091138/cropped-white-dwarf-colour-accurate-images-panel.webp" width="300" /></a><figcaption class="wp-element-caption"><em>Color-accurate simulated images of eight white dwarfs, each shown with its spectrum and temperature. The 2021 model, with the older sodium data, also gave a green, #EAFFA1.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The carbon-rich and metal-rich stars were chosen to show the range of colors. Microduck, the Robot Pet That Imitates Life: Can We Really Grow Attached to a Machine?

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p>Imagine a small robotic duck walking around your home, reacting to its surroundings, and even picking up small objects with its beak. When it simulates sadness, it lowers its head and makes soft, melancholic sounds. When it expresses happiness, it produces higher-pitched, lively sounds accompanied by body movements. Its name is <strong>Microduck</strong>.</p> <p></p><p>Standing just 25 centimeters tall and weighing less than 800 grams, it illustrates how advances in artificial intelligence and robotics are making increasingly sophisticated interactions between humans and machines possible.</p><p></p><p>One of its most curious features is <strong>its voice</strong>. Every Microduck has a unique sonic identity generated from its processor's serial number. As a result, no two units produce exactly the same sounds. It may seem like a minor detail, but it makes each robot recognizable, almost as if it had its own personality. Through different sounds and postures, Microduck can also <strong>simulate emotional states</strong> such as sadness and happiness.</p><p></p><p>Of course, the robot does not actually experience these emotions: it reproduces their outward expressions. Yet, the effect can be surprisingly convincing to anyone watching. There is also another interesting aspect: <strong>social behavior</strong>. When two or more Microducks come together, they can interact and, when appropriately configured, even perform a kind of chorus. Their behavior resembles that of animals that live in groups and communicate with one another.</p><p></p><p>But behind this playful appearance lies an enormous technological challenge. Microduck's software is <strong>open source</strong>, allowing developers to study how it works, modify it, and create new behaviors. Moreover, thanks to a <strong>digital twin</strong> and reinforcement learning, the robot can be trained in a virtual environment.</p><p></p><p>There, it can experiment with thousands of movements, fall, correct its mistakes, and try again. Cloud computing makes it possible to run large numbers of experiments in parallel, something that would require considerable time, money, and resources in the physical world. Once learned, these behaviors can be transferred to the real robot, an approach commonly known as <em>sim-to-real transfer</em>. This is how it can acquire unusual skills, such as learning to move on skates.</p><p></p><p>Another major challenge is <strong>efficiency</strong>. Microduck is equipped with fifteen motors, sensors, and a compact computing system. A neural network helps control its movements, continuously adjusting its balance and position. All of this must run on a small battery, which provides limited autonomy. Nature has been dealing with similar challenges for millions of years: animals perceive their environment, react, and move using energy obtained from food.</p><p></p><p>We tend to take these abilities for granted, yet reproducing them in machines remains extraordinarily difficult. Consider the complexity of developing increasingly autonomous robots capable of understanding instructions, reasoning about what to do, and translating decisions into precise physical movements. <strong>Combining artificial intelligence, perception, and physical action in small, affordable devices with limited energy consumption is one of the major technological challenges of the coming years.</strong></p><p></p><p>This brings us to an interesting question: why did Microduck's developers choose these particular characteristics? Its distinctive voice, simulated emotions, interactions with other robots, and amusing movements seem directly inspired by the animal world. The goal is probably not just to make the product appealing, but also to encourage an emotional connection with its users.</p><p></p><p>A small robot like Microduck could anticipate <strong>a new generation of artificial pets</strong>, somewhere between intelligent toys and future household assistants.</p><p></p><p>It could keep us company, entertain us, and learn new behaviors through our interactions. It would also offer several practical advantages: it does not make a mess, does not need to be taken outside, does not get sick, and never requires a visit to the veterinarian. If we leave home for a week, we can simply turn it off and find it exactly where we left it when we return.</p><p></p><p>But a real pet is something profoundly different. A dog or a cat does not simply simulate emotions through a computer program. <strong>Animals are living beings, with instincts and biological needs, capable of experiencing pleasure, pain, and fear.</strong> They explore, seek companionship, and spontaneously react to what happens around them. They build relationships through experience, learning, and living together. The bond between humans and animals also develops through everyday care, shared habits, and trust built over time.</p><p></p><p>Above all, having a pet means accepting responsibility. An animal can become sick, grow old, and suffer. It needs us even when we are tired, busy, or would rather be doing something else. A robot, on the other hand, may offer many of the enjoyable aspects of companionship without requiring the same sacrifices. And perhaps that is precisely one of the reasons why devices like Microduck could become successful.</p><p></p><p>Yet there is a fundamental difference: <strong>simulating life is not the same as living it</strong>. An animal is not merely a collection of needs and responsibilities. It gives us affection, companionship, and moments of happiness. It gets to know us, recognizes our habits, and develops a relationship that changes over time.</p><p></p><p>Caring for it, facing difficulties together, and even knowing that one day we will have to say goodbye are all part of that relationship. We cannot simply turn off an animal when it becomes inconvenient, because it is not an object at our disposal. It is a living being with whom we share part of our existence.</p><p></p><p><strong>But our relationship with an animal is only one part of its life.</strong> Every living organism belongs to a much larger cycle, in which different forms of life are interconnected and contribute to the balance of nature. Millions of years of evolution have shaped organisms capable of performing extraordinarily complex functions, often using surprisingly small amounts of energy and resources.</p><p></p><p>A robot can imitate an animal's voice, movements, and even its emotional expressions, but it does not participate in this biological cycle as a living organism. However sophisticated it becomes, it remains a collection of circuits, motors, and silicon chips.</p><p></p><p>Microduck is nevertheless an interesting technological achievement, showing how rapidly robotics is evolving, partly thanks to advances in artificial intelligence. I am convinced that similar devices will become successful and occupy an increasingly important place in our homes.</p><p></p><p>Yet, I must admit that this prospect also raises some concerns, particularly about the relationships we may develop with them. <strong>Perhaps the most interesting question is not how closely robots will come to resemble animals, but how willing we will be to grow attached to something that appears alive without actually being alive.</strong></p><p></p>
