Scientists find microplastics falling with snow in the Sierra Nevada

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists measuring microplastic deposition across the Sierra Nevada during snowstorms found plastic particles at every single site they sampled. 

The sites included the most remote locations, accessible only by chairlift or on skis, far beyond any road. 

The findings matter well beyond the mountains. The Sierra Nevada snowpack supplies roughly 30% of California’s freshwater. 

What falls with the snow eventually makes its way into that supply.

Skiing to the sampling sites

Lead researcher Aleksandra Karapetrova, an environmental toxicologist at the University of California, Riverside, collected snow at seven locations across the Sierra Nevada during active storms. Getting there was difficult. 

“One site was accessed by chairlift. I had to ski to the rest of the remote sites. That’s how isolated they are,” she said. “But no matter how far out the site, I always found microplastics there.”

The expectation had been that at least some of the most isolated sites would show little or no contamination. That expectation did not survive contact with the data. 

Earlier research had already established that microplastics occur in Western US snow. What this study added was a storm-by-storm accounting. 

The scientists measured exactly how much plastic was being deposited during individual weather events. Such a method makes it possible to connect specific particle loads to specific atmospheric conditions.

How snow sweeps plastic from the sky

Microplastics enter the atmosphere through mundane routes.

Clothing sheds fibers during daily wear or through dryer vents, wind lifts small fragments from the ground, and plastic products break down into particles small enough to become airborne. 

Once suspended, they can travel considerable distances before anything brings them down.

Snow is particularly effective at collecting them. As snowflakes fall, they scavenge particles from the air around them. 

This process helps explain why the researchers measured substantially more plastic arriving with snowfall than during dry weather. 

The storm is not just bringing water. It is sweeping the atmosphere clean of whatever is suspended in it, plastic included.

What the analysis revealed

At remote sampling locations during the height of the storms, snow deposited between 270 and 3,200 plastic particles per square meter per day. 

At one site in the Mammoth Mountain ski area, where human activity is more concentrated and snowfall accumulation greater, the figure ran between 2,600 and 7,500 particles – roughly three times the remote-site range. 

“I didn’t think the plastics would stay suspended so much in the town,” Karapetrova said.

Even at that upper end, the actual mass of plastic is small. At the highest estimated deposition rate, snowfall over an acre would deliver about 1.5 grams of plastic per day. 

The particle counts are what make the numbers striking, not the weight.

The findings suggest that even the Sierra Nevada’s most isolated locations are not beyond the reach of airborne plastic pollution.

Where the plastic comes from

To trace the origins of the particles, the researchers followed the air masses that brought each sampled storm into the Sierra Nevada. 

Most had passed over Fresno and Madera counties in the Central Valley before arriving at the mountain sites.

That suggests the valley may be a significant source of the airborne plastic, though computer models could not pinpoint exact origins. 

The Central Valley is one of the most intensively farmed and heavily trafficked regions in the American West. Plastic is used in agriculture, packaging, and transport, generating abundant material for the wind to pick up and carry.

Identifying the particles required more than visual inspection under a microscope. 

The team used infrared spectroscopy to read each particle’s chemical signature. They then matched those signatures against a reference library to distinguish plastic from soil and plant material. 

The most common types found were polyethylene, polypropylene, and polyester, or PET. Cellulose derivatives, chemically modified plant-based materials used in consumer products, also turned up.

Beyond the mountains

The study didn’t assess what fraction of this plastic reaches drinking water, nor did it evaluate health effects directly. 

Although some plastic-associated chemicals have been linked to harmful biological effects, the study did not determine whether the particles found in Sierra Nevada snow pose risks to human health or aquatic ecosystems.

What the study makes clear is that atmospheric microplastic deposition is not confined to cities or coastlines or industrial zones.

It is happening continuously, across an entire mountain range, carried by weather systems that have no regard for how remote a location is.

“We are at a point where microplastics are in our atmosphere, not just the ocean, not just rivers. It comes down with the snowfall, making its way to places humans typically don’t go,” Karapetrova said. 

“We’ve reached a point where plastic pollution is falling out of the sky every day, across this entire region, even when it isn’t snowing.”

The research is published in the journal Environmental Science & Technology.

Darkbloom, which allowed Mac users to rent out their GPUs for AI inference, warns of low payouts

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Declining token prices have prompted Darkbloom, a distributed inference network, to reassess its business plans.

Darkbloom, a project from Seattle-based tech biz Eigen Labs, has developed software that allows anyone with a sufficiently capable macOS device to rent their GPU through the Darkbloom network for interference jobs. In return, they get paid.

Through this network, 800 or so participants – including as of two days ago this reporter – have been able to collect a few cents to a few dollars per day by having their hardware handle AI model prompts submitted through OpenRouter, an AI model gateway.

In theory, someone running one of Apple's recently released Mac minis with an M5 Pro chip and 64 GB of RAM could earn as much as $192 per month handling incoming inference jobs, per the company's estimate.

That assumes full 24/7 utilization during this period on a sought-after model like Qwen3.6 35B A3B, which sells for $0.065/M input tokens, $0.75/M output tokens. But not every network participant has fully surrendered their Mac hardware to the network.

The median earnings of the 50 people on the leaderboard amount to about $5.26 per day, $159.86 per month, or $1,918.50 per year. So the best case estimate presented by Darkbloom looks plausible. But it may not be sustainable.

The price of tokens – the unit of AI work – has declined, reportedly down 54 percent since May. Ramp's AI Token Price Index, which tracks the price of tokens from Anthropic and OpenAI, shows similar slippage. For Anthropic, the price per million tokens went from $2.65 on May 1, 2026 to $0.62 on October 22, 2026, a decline of 77 percent.

In a message shared with Darkbloom participants on the company's Slack channel, Kydo, one of the Eigen Labs researchers working on the distributed inference network, celebrated the network's processing of its trillionth token while attempting to moderate expectations about the project's viability.

"Earnings have been low," said Kydo. "For most providers, I don't expect that it is going to recover soon."

Pointing to the computational prowess of DeekSeek's DeepInfer framework, he explained that structural changes in the inference market have affected the supply of tokens.

"Data centers have become cheaper," he said. "Output per NVIDIA cluster is up ~20x this year, and their margins are wide enough to cut prices 90 percent and still profit at 50 percent."

The flood of tokens in turn has washed away the price advantage Darkbloom had previously. "The market caught up to our price," Kydo explained. "We could go lower, but then most of you would earn even less nothing."

Finally, he said, the inference routing market is changing, with OpenRouter moving away from price-based routing and putting more emphasis on decode speed, quantization, and prefill speed.

For network participants with older or less powerful Mac hardware – those without top of the line Max or Ultra Apple Silicon – "inference won't be worth running," he said.

Kydo went on to outline how Darkbloom aims to adapt to the commoditized token market. This involves analyzing model demand to steer participants toward more sought-after models, and looking at ways to provide other kinds of compute work, like distributed CI/CD jobs.

For Mac hardware, the focus now appears to be on clusters of high-end Mac Studios (now available with up to 512 GB of RAM), using Apple's Remote Direct Memory Access (RDMA) over Thunderbolt.

The CI/CD work, based on the observation that AI agents now make pull requests faster than CI/CD pipelines can handle them, will require sandboxing — something the biz expects to begin testing soon. And Darkbloom also aims to improve the performance of its network (in terms of time to first token) and to look for other token buyers beyond OpenRouter.

"The next stretch will be a fight, and I expect many machines will leave and Slack will get quieter," Kydo concluded. "But those who stay will be first in line when new earning opportunities come along. We are a driven team and well capitalized. We will not give up." ®

Bayern Munich are reportedly interested in signing Arsenal captain Martin Odegaard, and the Norwegian international may be open to joining the German champions.

According to a report from Sky Germany, the 27-year-old could consider a move to the Bundesliga, although Arsenal are keen to secure his long-term future with a new contract.

Martin Odegaard attracts interest from Bayern Munich

The attacking midfielder has established himself as one of Arsenal’s most influential players under Mikel Arteta, playing a crucial role in their recent success. His creativity, technical ability and leadership qualities have made him an integral part of the team’s midfield.

Bayern have kept in touch with the player’s camp over the years, but a transfer has never materialised. The German giants could have another chance to pursue him if uncertainty around his contract continues.

Odegaard’s current agreement at the Emirates Stadium expires in 2028, and Arsenal have already held initial informal discussions over an extension. The Gunners are determined to avoid allowing their captain to enter the final year of his contract without resolving his future.
