DraftKings can continue operating in the state under the interim arrangement.</p> <p>The company’s existing Louisiana license expires November 10, 2026. The board has scheduled a renewal hearing for February 18, 2027, with a final decision expected afterward; the conditional extension is set to keep DraftKings authorized through February 19.</p> <h2><span id="ai_allegations_and_a_shareholder_review">AI allegations and a shareholder review</span></h2> <p>The Louisiana Gaming Control Board’s review follows a New York Times report alleging that DraftKings used AI to direct promotions and bonus inducements toward customers considered more likely to bet more. The reported practice was referred to internally as “elasticity.” The allegations are part of a broader <a href="https://readwrite.com/sportsbook-ai-marketing/">regulatory examination of AI in sportsbook marketing</a>.</p> <p>There is also a separate ownership issue. The Louisiana State Police Gaming Enforcement Division found information indicating that some DraftKings shareholders with stakes of 5% or more had not completed customary suitability reviews. Assistant Attorney General Houston told the board the investigation was incomplete and that regulators needed additional time to examine the allegations and shareholder question.</p> <p>The board has not found evidence that DraftKings violated a licensing or moral obligation. Its decision leaves both matters under review while the company operates under conditional authorization.</p> <h2><span id="draftkings_disputes_the_reported_ai_use">DraftKings disputes the reported AI use</span></h2> <p>DraftKings President of Operations and cofounder Paul Liberman appeared before the board to address the concerns. He denied that the company used AI in the manner described in the reporting or used the technology to harm consumers. Liberman also said DraftKings keeps records showing whether customers are winning or losing.</p> <p>Legal Sports Report reported that the conditional renewal includes deadlines for DraftKings to provide shareholder suitability materials and written responses about AI use, prediction-market activity, digital offerings to Louisiana residents, and other regulatory questions. The company must also review and certify its marketing materials, commercials and digital ads for responsible-gambling compliance.</p> <p>The conditions require a senior company representative to handle communications with the board within seven days. DraftKings must submit several materials within 30 days and provide monthly compliance reports covering areas such as new digital products, promotions, responsible-gambling systems, customer complaints and unresolved compliance issues.</p> <p>Board Chairman Chris Hebert described the conditional approval as a way for regulators to complete their oversight and give DraftKings an opportunity to respond. The board’s action is not a finding that the company engaged in wrongdoing.</p> <h2><span id="february_hearing_will_set_the_next_decision_point">February hearing will set the next decision point</span></h2> <p>DraftKings will remain authorized to operate while state investigators continue their work and the company responds to the board’s conditions. Introducing Amazon ECS Gateway: A fully-managed ingress solution for ECS services

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p>When platform teams manage containerized applications at scale on <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/ecs/?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">Amazon Elastic Container Service (Amazon ECS)</a>, configuring ingress networking for each service often becomes a bottleneck. For every new service, teams must provision and connect <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/application-load-balancer/?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">Application Load Balancers (ALBs)</a>, <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/certificate-manager/?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">AWS Certificate Manager (ACM)</a> certificates, <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/waf/?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">AWS WAF</a> web ACLs, and security groups, then keep those resources in sync as services scale, update, or operate. This per-service overhead slows down deployment velocity and increases the risk of configuration drift across an organization.</p> <p>Today, we’re announcing <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/ecs/?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">Amazon ECS Gateway</a>, a new account-level resource that simplifies ingress networking for containerized workloads by dynamically provisioning and managing the necessary infrastructure on your behalf, with all resources created directly in your account.</p> <p><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-106076" height="977" src="https://d2908q01vomqb2.cloudfront.net/da4b9237bacccdf19c0760cab7aec4a8359010b0/2026/10/20/2026-ECS-Gateway-architecture.png" width="1675" /></p> <p>ECS Gateway introduces a clear separation of responsibilities between platform teams and application teams.</p> <ul> <li><strong>Platform teams</strong> create and configure a gateway, defining organizational networking standards including subnets, security groups, TLS certificates, listener settings, and WAF web ACLs, in a single place. They then associate the gateway with one or more ECS clusters, making it available to services running on those clusters.</li> <li><strong>Application teams</strong> opt their services in by adding a gateway connector parameter when creating or updating a service. ECS provisions the required per-service resources automatically: an ACM certificate, a unique DNS hostname, listener rules, target groups, and a service security group that permits inbound traffic only from the ALB. No networking expertise required.</li> </ul> <p>ECS Gateway offers host-based routing using ALBs and enables up to 25 services to share a single ALB, optimizing infrastructure costs. ECS manages the full lifecycle of the underlying infrastructure, provisioning the first ALB when your first service connects, adding more ALBs as capacity is reached, and removing them when no longer needed. Each service receives a unique DNS hostname, giving your teams a ready-to-use endpoint the moment provisioning completes.</p> <p><strong><u>A first look at ECS Gateway</u></strong><br /> In the <a href="https://console.aws.amazon.com/ecs/?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">ECS console</a>, platform team can create a gateway and sets its configuration. The infrastructure role is an IAM role that ECS assumes to provision and manage networking resources such as ALBs, security groups, and certificates in your account. To create a gateway, provide a gateway name and select an infrastructure role. You can optionally associate the gateway with ECS clusters during creation or later.</p> <p>Choose a load balancing scheme, either public or VPC-internal traffic routing. Under <strong>Additional Settings</strong>, you can also set up a listener, port, maximum count of load balancers the gateway can scale to, and a web ACL lets you protect your web applications. Some settings, including the name, scheme, VPC, subnets, IP address type, listener protocol and port cannot be changed after gateway creation.</p> <p><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-105491" height="2560" src="https://d2908q01vomqb2.cloudfront.net/da4b9237bacccdf19c0760cab7aec4a8359010b0/2026/09/02/2026-ecs-gateway-1-create.jpg" style="border: solid 1px #ccc;" width="1719" /></p> <p>Using <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/cli/?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">AWS Command Line Interface (AWS CLI)</a>, call <code>CreateGateway</code> with a name (unique per account), an infrastructure role, and a configuration object. No infrastructure is provisioned until a service connects with a gateway connector.</p> <pre><code class="lang-bash">aws ecs create-gateway \ --name my-team-gateway \ --infrastructure-role-arn arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/ECSGatewayRole \ --configuration '{ "HostSharedAlb": { "networkConfiguration": { "subnets": [ "subnet-080779f9bd887757a", "subnet-03d5b2fead6bc6258", "subnet-0861210a397d31252", "subnet-06539e194023cb77a" ], "ipAddressType": "ipv4" }, "scheme": "internet-facing" } }' </code></pre> <p>Once the gateway is created, you can take a look at the detailed configuration, gateway routes, load balancers, and associated clusters.</p> <p><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-105492" height="978" src="https://d2908q01vomqb2.cloudfront.net/da4b9237bacccdf19c0760cab7aec4a8359010b0/2026/09/02/2026-ecs-gateway-1-create-2.jpg" style="border: solid 1px #ccc;" width="1800" /></p> <p>Now associate the gateway with one or more clusters (many-to-many), to make it available to ECS services on those clusters.</p> <p><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-105493" height="1148" src="https://d2908q01vomqb2.cloudfront.net/da4b9237bacccdf19c0760cab7aec4a8359010b0/2026/09/02/2026-ecs-gateway-2-connect.jpg" style="border: solid 1px #ccc;" width="1800" /></p> <p>Use <code>AssociateGatewayWithCluster</code> to make the gateway available to services on a cluster:</p> <pre><code class="lang-bash">aws ecs associate-gateway-with-cluster \ --gateway my-team-gateway \ --cluster arn:aws:ecs:us-east-1:123456789012:cluster/my-cluster</code></pre> <p>To create and manage the ECS Gateway programmatically, including calling APIs and searching documentation, try the <a href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/agent-toolkit/latest/userguide/getting-started-aws-mcp-server.html?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">AWS MCP Server</a> and <a href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/agent-toolkit/latest/userguide/plugins.html?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">plugins</a> with your preferred AI coding tool. To learn more, visit the <a href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonECS/latest/developerguide/gateway-overview.html?trk=d8ec3b19-0f37-4f8c-8c12-189f913e205c&amp;sc_channel=el">Amazon ECS Gateway documentation</a>.</p> <p><strong>ECS Gateway:Behind the scenes<br /> </strong>A gateway can be associated with up to 10,000 clusters, and a single cluster supports up to five gateway associations. This many-to-many relationship gives platform teams the flexibility to share a gateway across environments or assign multiple gateways (for example, public and private) to a single cluster.</p> <p>Platform teams can control access by choosing which gateways to associate with which clusters. For additional control, use IAM policies to restrict who can deploy services to specific clusters.</p> <p>Application teams add the gateway connector to each service definition in <code>CreateService</code> or <code>UpdateService</code>. If you have an existing ECS service with its own load balancer, you can adopt ECS Gateway incrementally by calling <code>UpdateService</code> to add a gateway connector. Both ingress paths remain available, allowing you to validate the gateway connection before removing the existing load balancer configuration.</p> <pre><code class="lang-bash">aws ecs create-service \ --cluster my-cluster \ --service my-api-service \ --task-definition my-api-service:1 \ --gateway-connectors '[{ "portName": "api", "filters": {"accessTypes": ["PUBLIC"]}, "healthCheck": {...} }]'</code></pre> <p>At deployment time, Amazon ECS evaluates the gateways associated with the cluster and connects the service to every compatible gateway. A gateway is compatible when its access type matches the service’s connector filter, it is in the same VPC as the service, and it covers all Availability Zones used by the service’s configured subnets.</p> <p>For each connection, Amazon ECS assigns the service to an ALB with available capacity and provisions the per-service resources: an ACM certificate, a unique DNS hostname, a listener rule routing traffic for that hostname to the service target group, and a service security group allowing inbound traffic only from the ALB security group. If no existing ALB has capacity, ECS creates a new one (up to <code>maxAlbCount</code> referring to quota limit). Once provisioning completes, the gateway route becomes <code>ACTIVE</code>, and the service URL is ready to receive traffic.</p> <p>ECS Gateway handles common operational scenarios automatically:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Redeployments</strong>: On each deployment, ECS re-evaluates gateway associations. If new compatible gateways have been associated since the last deployment, your service connects to them. If a gateway has been disassociated, your service disconnects and the route is removed. Your service always reflects the current state of the cluster gateways at deployment time.</li> <li><strong>Service deletion</strong>: ECS automatically disconnects the service from every gateway, removes all gateway routes, and cleans up all per-service resources. If the service held the last route on an ALB, that ALB is deprovisioned.</li> </ul> <p><strong>Things to Know</strong><br /> Here are a few important details to keep in mind:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Networking</strong>: ECS Gateway is available for ECS services using the <code>awsvpc</code> network mode across all launch types (Amazon ECS EC2, AWS Fargate, and ECS Managed Instances). Your service Availability Zones (AZs) must be a subset of each gateway’s AZs on the cluster. A gateway ALB only routes traffic to tasks in AZs where it has subnets. We recommend creating gateways and services for a given cluster with the same set of AZs.</li> <li><strong>Capacity</strong>: If <code>maxAlbCount</code> is reached and no ALB has available capacity, the deployment fails. Default quota limit of <code>maxAlbCount</code> is 10. Plan your <code>maxAlbCount</code> setting according to the number of services you expect to connect.</li> <li><strong>Observability</strong>: Use ALB metrics in CloudWatch with the <code>TargetGroup</code> dimension to monitor per-service traffic, and <code>LoadBalancer</code> dimension to monitor traffic across the shared ALB. Use <code>describe-target-health</code> to check real-time target status. Configure CloudWatch alarms for metrics such as <code>HealthyHostCount</code>, <code>TargetResponseTime</code>, and <code>HTTPCode_Target_5XX_Count</code>, using gateway ARN-based tagging so monitoring scales automatically with your <code>maxAlbCount</code>.</li> </ul> <p><strong><u>Now available</u></strong><br /> Amazon ECS Gateway is available now in all commercial AWS Regions where ECS is available at no additional charge. You pay only for the underlying infrastructure provisioned in your account when services connect to a gateway, including applicable ALB charges, CloudWatch logs and metrics, ACM certificates, and data transfer charges. Embracing Playful Imperfection With The CSS `random()` Function

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p>In design, we love spending hours obsessing over consistency, grids, and alignment. But when I heard that the native <strong>CSS <code>random()</code> function</strong> landed in Safari and is rolling out in Chrome 156, I started wondering: where does <strong>randomness</strong> actually make sense on the web? Is it something we want in our design system? To find out, I did what I always do in doubt: I built a bunch of playful CodePen demos exploring organic typography, bouncing letters, splayed cards, and a punk fanzine.</p> <p>As someone who loves building design systems and tinkering with CSS architecture, a big part of my day job revolves around control. We set up spacing scales, lock down typography, set up color tokens, and make sure everything aligns on a grid. <strong>Consistency</strong> is usually our bread and butter as design engineers and a proud badge of skill when creating design systems. If a card suddenly jumps 20 pixels to the left on a refresh, it’s not creative expression; it’s a bug that the project manager will log.</p> <p>So when I first heard that the native <code>random()</code> function from the CSS Values and Units Level 5 spec was shipped in Safari, and is currently rolling out in Chrome 156 (with positive signals from Mozilla on Firefox as well), my first reaction was: <em>that’s pretty cool!</em></p> <p>And my second reaction was: <em>wait... where would I actually use this without making a mess? How can I convince designers that random might be a good idea for a design system?</em></p> <p>Because let’s be honest: why on earth would we want randomness in our stylesheets when we’ve spent the last decade trying to make the web more predictable? Even when using flexbox or fluid typography, we’re still trying to make things as aligned and uniform as possible.</p> The Charm Of Things Not Being 100% Straight <p>If you step away from the browser for a second and look at physical things around you, nothing is ever truly uniform.</p> <p>Take an old letterpress print: the ink bleeds slightly into the paper grain, some letters have a microscopic tilt, and the baseline isn’t always that straight. Or think about a deck of Polaroid photos dropped on a coffee table; those photos aren’t perfect, probably not straight either. Or what about lights flickering in a street at night? That little bit of analog messiness is what makes things feel <strong>alive</strong>, <strong>organic</strong>, and <strong>human</strong>.</p> <p>On the web, we’ve gotten so good at pixel perfection that sometimes our interfaces end up feeling a bit sterile. We notice this even more now with rapidly created designs by an unguided LLM; we usually end up with the same sort of design we had 10 years ago. Everything is squared off, aligned, neat. It works, but frankly, it does get a bit boring.</p> <p>Randomness in CSS doesn’t have to mean throwing your design system out the window. It’s about little imperfections and playfulness. Maybe let’s even call it fun.</p> <p>To see what this actually looks like in practice, I spent a few hours tinkering in CodePen. The demos might feel a bit isolated, but I’m sure that many designers could make the best of this <strong>controlled chaos</strong>. But before we look at those demos, let’s take a look at the syntax.</p> How The CSS <code>random()</code> Function Works <p>The <code>random()</code> function is specified in <a href="https://drafts.csswg.org/css-values-5/#random">CSS Values and Units Module Level 5</a>. It generates a pseudo-random value inside your CSS without needing JavaScript or a build-time Sass loop.</p> <p>The formal specification looks like this:</p> <pre><code>random( &lt;random-key&gt;?, &lt;min&gt;, &lt;max&gt;, &lt;step&gt;? )</code></pre> <p>In practice, and across current browser implementations, you can just write comma-separated arguments:</p> <pre><code>/* Random angle between -3deg and 3deg */ rotate: random(-3deg, 3deg); /* Random width between 20px and 80px in steps of 10px */ inline-size: random(20px, 80px, 10px); /* Random animation duration */ animation-duration: random(1.5s, 3s, 0.2s); </code></pre> <h3>Matching Units</h3> <p>It won’t be a shocker for most people, but I thought I should at least mention that <strong>your minimum and maximum bounds must share the exact same unit type</strong>.</p> <p>The CSS parser needs to know what dimension it’s dealing with. You can use lengths, angles, time, percentages, or plain unitless numbers:</p> <pre><code>/* Good: both are angles */ rotate: random(-4deg, 4deg); /* Good: both are unitless numbers */ opacity: random(0.3, 1); /* Nope! You cannot mix units */ inline-size: random(2rem, 100px); /* Invalid */ </code></pre> <p>If you ever need to combine units, you’ll have to reach for <code>calc()</code> inside the arguments so each side resolves properly.</p> <h3>Stepping And Rounding</h3> <p>The optional third argument is the step size. If you don’t add it, the browser picks from a continuous range. But if you provide a step, it rounds the random value to discrete increments:</p> <pre><code>/* Only produces 0px, 15px, 30px, 45px, or 60px */ top: random(0px, 60px, 15px); </code></pre> <p>This can be helpful in cases where you want things to jump around a bit, but you still want them to land on your pre-defined grid.</p> <h3>Caching Keys: Keeping Things In Sync</h3> <p>Normally, every time <code>random()</code> is evaluated on a property, it creates an independent random value. But there could be an example where you create a box that sets <code>inline-size</code> and <code>block-size</code> at random, but at the same time you need them to match so the element stays a perfect square.</p> <p>That’s where the <strong>caching key</strong> comes in:</p> <pre><code>.card-thumbnail { /* Both properties share the key --square-size */ inline-size: random(--square-size element-scoped, 60px, 120px); block-size: random(--square-size element-scoped, 60px, 120px); } </code></pre> <p>In the latest spec, a bare dashed ident like <code>--square-size</code> shares its seed globally across every element in the document (which would make all cards identical). To keep width and height linked on a single element while giving each card its own independent size, the spec requires pairing it with the <code>element-scoped</code> keyword (e.g. <code>--square-size element-scoped</code>).</p> <p>To see this in action, I created a little example. In the top row, the tiles have uncached dimensions, which distorts each tile into an unpredictable, mismatched rectangle. In the bottom row, both <code>inline-size</code> and <code>block-size</code> share <code>--square-size</code>, guaranteeing that each tile remains a pristine 1:1 square. Refresh the CodePen a couple of times and notice the bottom cards keeping the same randomness:</p> <p>See the Pen <a href="https://codepen.io/editor/smashingmag/pen/01a15e91-4bac-765e-b04e-a53636759163">Uncached vs cached random() [forked]</a> by <a href="https://codepen.io/utilitybend">@utilitybend</a>.</p> <div> <pre><code>/* Uncached: width and height resolve independently, creating random rectangles */ .tile-uncached { inline-size: random(4.2rem, 8.5rem); block-size: random(4.2rem, 8.5rem); rotate: random(-3.5deg, 3.5deg); } /* Cached: shared key guarantees a clean 1:1 square */ .tile-cached { inline-size: random(--square-size element-scoped, 4.2rem, 8.5rem); block-size: random(--square-size element-scoped, 4.2rem, 8.5rem); rotate: random(--tilt element-scoped, -3.5deg, 3.5deg); } </code></pre> </div> Browser Support And Progressive Enhancement <p>Where can you test this today?</p> <ul> <li><strong>WebKit / Safari</strong>: Safari was the first to land support.</li> <li><strong>Chrome / Edge</strong>: Shipped and rolling out right now with <strong>Chrome 156</strong>.</li> <li><strong>Firefox</strong>: Positive signals on the standards track, so hopefully won’t be too far behind.</li> </ul> <p>Because we’re still in the rollout phase, you’ll want to wrap your experiments in <code>@supports</code>:</p> <pre><code>/* Baseline for all browsers */ .badge { rotate: 0deg; } /* Enhanced for browsers with random() */ @supports (rotate: random(-2deg, 2deg)) { .badge { rotate: random(-2.5deg, 2.5deg); } } </code></pre> <h3>The Future <code>random-item</code> Function</h3> <p>There’s also another function in the spec called <code>random-item()</code>. While <code>random()</code> handles numerical ranges, <code>random-item()</code> lets you pick from a list of predefined tokens or keywords:</p> <div> <pre><code>/* Picking a random color from your palette */ background-color: random-item(--seed, oklch(75% 0.18 150), oklch(65% 0.22 350), oklch(85% 0.16 85)); </code></pre> </div> <p>This can be handy to randomise from a set of design tokens. This one does not have any browser support at the moment, but it certainly is something to look forward to.</p> <p>And now for the practical part.</p> Demo 1: Let’s Just Randomize Letters <p>When I started playing with the feature, I was also doing some typography tricks with animations. So that’s where my first thought went: Typography! <em>What if we just add a tiny bit of imperfection to a word?</em></p> <p>Think of vintage book covers. The letters aren’t misaligned by miles, but they have small imperfections.</p> <p>See the Pen <a href="https://codepen.io/editor/smashingmag/pen/01a15e92-157f-708d-a704-fa067114aaca">Imperfection random() [forked]</a> by <a href="https://codepen.io/utilitybend">@utilitybend</a>.</p> <p>Here is the CSS powering the letters:</p> <pre><code>@layer demo { .char { display: inline-block; /* A tiny tilt */ rotate: random(-2.8deg, 2.8deg); /* A micro nudge on X and Y */ translate: random(-1.5px, 1.5px) random(-2.8px, 2.8px); /* Very subtle ink variance */ opacity: random(0.8, 1, 0.02); transition-property: rotate, translate, opacity; transition-duration: 0.3s; transition-timing-function: cubic-bezier(0.34, 1.56, 0.64, 1); } /* Snap straight on hover */ .word:hover .char { rotate: 0deg; translate: 0 0; opacity: 1; } } </code></pre> <p>I also added a little hover effect. While you’re reading, the word feels playful. But the second you hover over it, every character snaps back.</p> <h3>Please Don’t Break The Screen Reader</h3> <p>One quick <strong>accessibility warning</strong> here: If you break a word into separate <code>&lt;span&gt;</code> elements so you can style each letter, screen readers will often read it out as individual characters: “i - m - p - e - r - f - e - c - t - i - o - n”, or pause awkwardly between each one. That’s a terrible user experience.</p> <p>Always hide the visual spans from assistive tech and provide the full word:</p> <pre><code>&lt;h1 class="word" aria-label="Imperfection"&gt; &lt;span class="word-inner" aria-hidden="true"&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;i&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;m&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;p&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;e&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;r&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;f&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;e&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;c&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;t&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;i&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;o&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class="char"&gt;n&lt;/span&gt; &lt;/span&gt; &lt;/h1&gt; </code></pre> <p>Screen reader users get the clean text, and sighted users get the fun visual quirks.</p> Demo 2: Animating The Letters: Pure Procedural Bounce <p>Once I had those letters sitting there with their little tilts, I couldn’t resist: <em>what if we animated it?</em></p> <p>I started tinkering with a bounce keyframe. I was just goofing around, but the end result gave me a little smile. If you animate every letter with the exact same timing, you get that clean sine wave we’ve all seen before. But if every letter gets its own jump height, its own duration, and its own landing squash, suddenly the word looks like a bunch of little characters jumping around on a trampoline.</p> <p>See the Pen <a href="https://codepen.io/editor/smashingmag/pen/01a15e92-c117-729f-9844-ddf19fc1fc96">Bouncy imperfection with CSS random() [forked]</a> by <a href="https://codepen.io/utilitybend">@utilitybend</a>.</p> <p>Here is how the bounce parameters are wired up:</p> <div> <pre><code>@layer demo { .char { display: inline-block; transform-origin: center bottom; /* Procedural bounce variables */ --jump: random(element-scoped, -35px, -55px, 2px); --tilt: random(element-scoped, -8deg, 8deg, 0.5deg); --dur: random(element-scoped, 1s, 1.2s, 0.1s); --delay: random(element-scoped, -3s, 0s, 0.1s); --rebound-squash: random(element-scoped, 1.08, 1.18, 0.02); animation: letter-bounce var(--dur) linear var(--delay) infinite; } @keyframes letter-bounce { /* apex */ 0% { translate: 0 var(--jump); rotate: var(--tilt); scale: 1 1; animation-timing-function: cubic-bezier(0.65, 0.05, 0.85, 0.35); } /* touchdown */ 44% { translate: 0 0; rotate: calc(var(--tilt) * 0.2); scale: 0.96 1.05; animation-timing-function: ease-out; } /* squash */ 52% { translate: 0 0; rotate: 0deg; scale: var(--rebound-squash) calc(2 - var(--rebound-squash)); animation-timing-function: cubic-bezier(0.2, 0.8, 0.2, 1); } /* back up */ 62% { translate: 0 calc(var(--jump) * 0.2); rotate: calc(var(--tilt) * -0.4); scale: 0.98 1.03; animation-timing-function: cubic-bezier(0.15, 0.85, 0.35, 1); } 100% { translate: 0 var(--jump); rotate: var(--tilt); scale: 1 1; } } } </code></pre> </div> <p>Notice the negative animation delay (<code>--delay: random(-3s, 0s)</code>).</p> <p>If you set a positive delay, the letters sit frozen on page load, waiting for their timers to expire before jumping. By using a negative delay, the browser pretends the animation has already been running for a few seconds. The moment the page loads, every letter is already bouncing at its own rhythm.</p> Demo 3: Tilting Cards And Dynamic Shadows <p>We’ve built cards. Lots of cards. So many cards... Portfolio grids, blog summaries, feature lists. And most of the time, they sit in three or four neat, boring columns.</p> <p>For dashboards, that’s exactly what you want. But if you’re building a lifestyle portfolio, a children’s website, or a collection of vacation photos, we can be a bit more playful. In the following example, I did just that:</p> <p>See the Pen <a href="https://codepen.io/editor/smashingmag/pen/01a15e93-849d-72a9-9254-3f4305e58a34">Random card tilt [forked]</a> by <a href="https://codepen.io/utilitybend">@utilitybend</a>.</p> <p>Here’s how I put that together:</p> <pre><code>@layer demo { .card { /* A gentle random tilt and vertical shift */ --tilt: random(-3.5deg, 3.5deg); --shift-y: random(-6px, 6px); rotate: var(--tilt); translate: 0 var(--shift-y); /* Shadow follows the tilt angle! */ box-shadow: calc(var(--tilt) * 1.5) calc(10px + abs(var(--tilt) * 1px)) 28px oklch(20% 0.02 260 / 0.07); transition: rotate 0.4s cubic-bezier(0.2, 0.8, 0.2, 1), translate 0.4s cubic-bezier(0.2, 0.8, 0.2, 1), box-shadow 0.4s ease; /* Straighten up on hover or keyboard focus */ &amp;:hover, &amp;:has(.button:focus-visible) { rotate: 0deg; translate: 0 -10px; z-index: 10; box-shadow: 0 20px 44px oklch(20% 0.02 260 / 0.12), 0 2px 6px oklch(20% 0.02 260 / 0.04); } } } </code></pre> <p>In the example, playing around with the shadow was a fun experiment. I will probably try more effects with that in the future.</p> Demo 4: The Splayed Deck With <code>sibling-index</code> And <code>scroll</code> <p>Here is where modern CSS features start getting really interesting. In the previous demo with tilting cards, we used pure randomness. But what if you have a stack of cards that needs to fan out across the screen as you scroll?</p> <p>In this example, I decided to use <strong>math for the structure</strong> and <strong>randomness for the human imperfection</strong>.</p> <p>See the Pen <a href="https://codepen.io/editor/smashingmag/pen/01a15e94-79c6-70b1-97d4-66b7c2d736b0">Scroll drivin animations with random() tilted cards [forked]</a> by <a href="https://codepen.io/utilitybend">@utilitybend</a>.</p> <p>Here is the setup:</p> <div> <pre><code>@layer demo { .deck-card { position: absolute; /* Mathematical fan base via sibling-index() (centered around middle card) */ --elevation: sibling-index(); --base-rot: calc((sibling-index() - (sibling-count() + 1) / 2) * 12deg); --base-x: calc((sibling-index() - (sibling-count() + 1) / 2) * 60px); --jitter-rot: random(-3.5deg, 3.5deg); --jitter-y: random(-12px, 12px); --jitter-x: random(-8px, 8px); transform-origin: center 130%; /* SDA */ animation: splay-fan linear both; animation-timeline: scroll(root); animation-range: 0% 80%; transition: scale 0.3s cubic-bezier(0.2, 0.8, 0.2, 1); &amp;:hover { scale: 1.05; /* Hovered item on top */ z-index: calc(sibling-count() + 1); } } @keyframes splay-fan { from { rotate: 0deg; translate: 0 0; box-shadow: 0 1px 3px oklch(20% 0.02 260 / 0.1), 0 2px 8px oklch(20% 0.02 260 / 0.07); } to { rotate: calc(var(--base-rot) + var(--jitter-rot)); translate: calc(var(--base-x) + var(--jitter-x)) var(--jitter-y); /* Splayed: 1. Sharp inter-card contact edge shadow 2. } } } </code></pre> <p><code>calc(pow(-1, sibling-index()))</code> gives us alternating directions (<code>1</code>, <code>-1</code>, <code>1</code>, <code>-1</code>), and <code>random(15%, 44%)</code> decides how fast each row travels. As you scroll, each typographic line moves at its own tempo.</p> Demo 6: Blurry Randomness <p>I love photography, and one of my favorite things about prime lenses is shallow depth of field. Others blur out to <code>6px</code> or <code>8px</code> and fade into the background. And when you hover over any blurry item in the back, it rack-focuses right into sharpness. It’s a great little effect for decorative hero sections or tag clouds.</p> Where Randomness Makes Sense (And Where It Doesn’t) <p>Now that we’ve played with the wild side, let’s talk about where this actually belongs in our daily work.</p> <h3>Good Use Cases</h3> <ul> <li><strong>Subtle micro-imperfection on decorative UI</strong>: Tilting polaroids, slight angles on sticky notes, or typographic hero titles.</li> <li><strong>Desynchronizing ambient loops</strong>: Status beacons, gentle floating badges, or background particle grids where perfect unison looks robotic.</li> <li><strong>Generative backgrounds</strong>: Star fields, confetti celebrations, floating bokeh circles, or texture grain.</li> <li><strong>Editorial art direction</strong>: Magazine spreads, event flyers, brutalist layouts, and playful landing pages.</li> <li><strong>Layering on top of math</strong>: Using <code>sibling-index()</code> for the structural grid and adding small random offsets for human warmth.</li> </ul> <h3>Where You Should Avoid It</h3> <ul> <li><strong>Core layouts and grids</strong>: Don’t randomize column gutters, form field widths, or container padding. People need predictable layout geometry to navigate.</li> <li><strong>Body copy</strong>: Don’t touch font sizes, line heights, or letter spacing on long-form paragraphs. Reading fatigue is real.</li> <li><strong>Interactive targets</strong>: Don’t leave small buttons or links tilted or shifted without resetting them on hover and focus. If a user has to chase a moving or crooked button, you’ve failed them.</li> </ul> <p>But! I am happy to be proven wrong here. I encourage you to challenge the status quo and experiment with this.</p> <h3>Always Respect <code>prefers-reduced-motion</code></h3> <p>Any time you use animations, you have to respect user preferences for reduced motion:</p> <pre><code>@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) { .char { animation: none; rotate: 0deg; translate: 0 0; } } </code></pre> <p>If someone prefers reduced motion, it could also be a good idea to flatten the angles, not just the animations. Design should be fun, but never at the cost of accessibility.</p> Wrapping My Head Around <code>random()</code> <p>Tinkering with <code>random()</code> over the last couple of days was a lot of fun. How to continue a GitHub Copilot app session in VS Code

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The agent has finished the bulk of your change. Now you want to inspect one implementation detail, adjust a few lines by hand, and run the project locally before you ship it.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">But the work is sitting in the <a href="https://github.com/features/ai/github-app">GitHub Copilot app</a>&hellip; and your editor is where you review diffs, debug code, and make final adjustments. Do you have to choose between staying in the session and starting over in Visual Studio Code?</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">You don&rsquo;t. You can open the Copilot app session directly in VS Code and continue with its conversation, code changes, and working context intact. That means Copilot can handle the repetitive or time-consuming work, then hand control back to you for closer review, hands-on editing, and local testing.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">In this tutorial, you&rsquo;ll learn how to:</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li>Open a Copilot app session in VS Code.</li> <li>Review the agent&rsquo;s changes line by line.</li> <li>Continue the same conversation from your editor.</li> <li>Modify and test the code locally.</li> <li>Push your updates to the pull request associated with the session.</li> </ul> <aside class="wp-block-group post-aside--large p-4 p-md-6 is-style-light-dimmed has-global-padding is-layout-constrained wp-block-group-is-layout-constrained is-style-light-dimmed--2" style="border-top-width: 4px;"> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-prerequisites-nbsp">Prerequisites&nbsp;</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Before you begin, make sure you have:&nbsp;</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li>The GitHub Copilot app installed and signed in.&nbsp;</li> <li><a href="https://code.visualstudio.com/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Visual Studio Code installed</a> and connected to the same GitHub account.&nbsp;</li> <li>A Copilot app session with code changes you want to review or continue.&nbsp;</li> <li>Permission to work in and push changes to the session&rsquo;s repository.&nbsp;</li> </ul> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">This tutorial assumes you are already comfortable navigating VS Code, running commands in its integrated terminal, and working with Git branches and pull requests.</p> </aside> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-1-start-with-a-session-in-the-copilot-app">1. Start with a session in the Copilot app</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Open the session you want to continue. Before handing it off, take a moment to review the task and the work Copilot completed.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">A session contains more than a collection of modified files. It also records the task, the plan, and the conversation that produced those changes. That context is important when you want to understand why Copilot chose a particular implementation or ask it to revise part of the work.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">You do not need to summarize that history or copy it into a new chat before moving to VS Code.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-2-open-the-session-in-vs-code">2. Open the session in VS Code</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">From the session, select <strong>Open in VS Code</strong>. If the app presents more than one supported editor or VS Code installation, choose the one you want to use.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">VS Code opens the project using the worktree associated with the session. It also displays the existing session in the Copilot sidebar.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That gives you three connected pieces of the original work:</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li><strong>The session context:</strong> The task, plan, and conversation remain available.</li> <li><strong>The diff:</strong> The files and lines changed by the agent are ready for review.</li> <li><strong>The worktree:</strong> VS Code opens the same isolated working tree the session used.</li> </ul> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">You are continuing the existing session, not reconstructing it in a new editor window.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-3-review-the-agent-s-changes">3. Review the agent&rsquo;s changes</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Before editing, inspect the diff in VS Code. Open the Source Control view to see the changed files, then select a file to review its changes line by line.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Review the implementation as you would any other proposed change. In particular, check that:</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li>The code solves the requested problem.</li> <li>The implementation follows the repository&rsquo;s conventions.</li> <li>Error states and edge cases are handled appropriately.</li> <li>Tests cover the behavior that changed.</li> <li>No unrelated files or behaviors were modified.</li> </ul> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The preserved conversation can help explain how Copilot arrived at the implementation. If something is unclear, continue prompting Copilot in the sidebar. Make a hands-on edit</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Once you understand the proposed changes, edit any file directly in VS Code.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is useful for adjustments that are faster or easier to make by hand, such as:</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li>Refining a label or error message.</li> <li>Renaming a variable for clarity.</li> <li>Adjusting layout or styling.</li> <li>Adding a missing test case.</li> <li>Applying a repository-specific convention.</li> </ul> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">You are editing the session&rsquo;s existing worktree, so your manual changes build directly on the agent&rsquo;s work. You do not need to copy files between directories or manually re-create the branch.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-5-run-and-verify-the-project-locally">5. Run and verify the project locally</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Use the VS Code integrated terminal to run the project&rsquo;s normal validation workflow. The exact commands depend on the repository, but some web app examples may include:</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li><code>npm install</code></li> <li><code>npm test</code></li> <li><code>npm run lint</code></li> <li><code>npm run dev</code></li> </ul> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Treat agent-generated changes the same way you would treat code written by a person: run the relevant tests, inspect failures, and verify the behavior yourself.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">If the development server prints a localhost URL, open it to test the change in a browser. When supported by your VS Code setup, you can open the URL in VS Code&rsquo;s integrated browser and inspect the result without leaving the editor.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For a user-interface change, verify both the requested behavior and nearby states. For example, check the modified component at different viewport sizes and confirm that loading, empty, and error states still work.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-6-continue-with-copilot-or-keep-editing-yourself">6. Continue with Copilot or keep editing yourself</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">At any point, you can choose the interaction that best fits the next step:</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li>Prompt Copilot when the change requires repository-wide analysis or repetitive edits.</li> <li>Edit directly when you know the exact change you want.</li> <li>Use both approaches iteratively as you test and refine the implementation.</li> </ul> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For example, after making a manual UI adjustment, you might ask Copilot:</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Add a test for the updated label and run the relevant test file. Managing open-weight model risk in enterprise deployments

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The growing AI landscape is giving enterprises more choice in how they build, customize, and deploy increasingly capable models. Open-weight models are an important part of that landscape. They give enterprises access to model weights and greater flexibility to inspect, evaluate, modify, and run models within their own environments.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">While that flexibility can be valuable, it also affects how enterprises evaluate models, where they implement safeguards, and who is responsible for maintaining those safeguards over time.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">As enterprises choose which models to deploy, the relevant question is broader than whether a model is open-weight or closed-weight. Enterprises need to understand what they are using the model for, what could go wrong in that context, and what evidence and controls they need to manage risks.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Today, Microsoft and NVIDIA are publishing Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise, the first in a planned series of joint resources intended to help enterprises address those questions. The paper introduces a shared risk taxonomy and an approach to managing model risks grounded in established standards and frameworks, including International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standards and the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The paper reflects a core idea: weight openness alone does not determine an AI system’s risk. Risk depends on the model, use case, deployment, and safeguards.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Read the whitepaper: Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise<br />Understanding risk in the deployment context<br />Open- and closed-weight models can share many of the same underlying risks, but how those risks materialize depends on the deployment. A model used to help an employee draft a document presents different considerations than the same model used in a customer-facing workflow, just as a standalone inference endpoint differs from a system connected to external data or tools.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The architecture matters too. A risk may originate in the model, while the most effective control may sit in the inference environment, application, or surrounding operational processes. The joint guidance looks beyond the model label to factors including provenance, intended use, users, deployment conditions, and surrounding controls. This first paper focuses primarily on model-layer risks. Evaluating a model alone does not establish the safety of an agent or application: teams must also assess its permissions, data access, tools, runtime, and operational controls.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Establishing a common language<br />Managing these risks consistently also requires a common vocabulary across engineering, security, legal, safety, and business teams.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The initial Microsoft and NVIDIA taxonomy organizes risks into five broad categories: Cybersecurity, Data Protection, Content, Human-AI Interaction, and Bias. It is not a policy or deployment checklist, but instead it should be used by enterprises to adapt it to their own systems, use cases, and risk criteria, then connect relevant risks to evaluations, mitigations, and accountable owners.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Using evaluations as risk evidence<br />Evaluations help turn the taxonomy into something enterprises can use. The paper maps evaluations to a selection of risks across the five categories, giving teams a way to start with a risk that matters for their deployment and identify evaluations that can produce relevant evidence about it.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Microsoft Foundry helps enterprises assess quality, groundedness, and safety with built-in and custom evaluations and automated red teaming. Its configurable content-safety controls help detect and mitigate harmful inputs and outputs and prompt attacks. Teams should validate control availability for their model and deployment, including preview status, and combine these capabilities with application-specific testing, monitoring, and human oversight.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Evaluations still need to be interpreted in the context of a particular deployment and the goal is to make it easier for teams to connect the risks they identify with useful evidence, while building a broader set of evaluation resources that can evolve alongside models, deployment patterns, and emerging risks. Microsoft and NVIDIA plan to use the joint repository to share additional evaluations and point to existing resources that can help enterprises assess specific risks.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Accounting for changes in control and responsibility<br />Open-weight access gives enterprises greater control over how a model is evaluated, modified, and deployed. Model access and deployment are separate choices: open-weight models can also be used through managed platforms. Responsibility for safeguards depends on the service and deployment configuration; enterprises that operate their own model deployments may take on controls otherwise provided by a managed service.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Those safeguards can operate at different layers, and (depending on the risk) enterprises might use model changes, access controls, input and output filtering, monitoring, or human review. The important step is connecting each identified risk to controls that can effectively address it within the deployed system. Those decisions need to be revisited as models and deployments change.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Building on this work<br />Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise focuses primarily on the model layer. Future work from Microsoft and NVIDIA is intended to extend this approach across the runtime, harness, and application layers, with additional technical guidance on evaluation and mitigation.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">We also expect the resources supporting this work to evolve as new models, evaluations, and deployment patterns emerge. The joint repository will provide a place to share additional evaluation resources, including contributions from the broader community.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For enterprises deploying open-weight models today, the paper provides guidance they can begin applying to their own use cases. We encourage developers, security practitioners, researchers, and others working in this space to read the paper, explore the evaluations, and contribute evaluations that can help strengthen this shared body of work.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Read the joint Microsoft-NVIDIA paper, Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Explore the joint repository and technical resources.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise was developed jointly by Microsoft and NVIDIA, with Andrew Berkley and April Chen of Microsoft and Michael Boone, Eileen Long, and Arun Rao of NVIDIA leading the development and editing of the paper.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Explore Microsoft Foundry evaluations and content-safety controls for implementation guidance.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">To learn more about Microsoft Security solutions, visit our website. Bookmark the Security blog to keep up with our expert coverage on security matters. Is Your Organization Messing Up AI Adoption?

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p>The way I see most organizations roll out AI feels more like a farce than a strategy. If it were turned into a movie, reviewers would say it was unbelievable. Nobody could be that stupid.</p> <p>Yet here we are. And what makes it all the more frustrating is that <strong>we have been here before</strong>.</p> <p>Back in 2014, I wrote a book for Smashing Magazine called <a href="https://shop.smashingmagazine.com/printed-books/digital-adaptation/"><em>Digital Adaptation</em></a>, looking at the mistakes being made in how organizations were approaching digital transformation and what they should be doing instead. The similarities between then and now are striking.</p> <p>So in this post, I want to look back at <em>Digital Adaptation</em> and see how the lessons in that book apply to the AI era and what we can learn from the past to help us navigate the challenges of AI today.</p> <p>The biggest mistake, both then and now, is approaching each as a technological challenge.</p> It’s About People, Not Technology <p>Sure, both digital transformation and AI are technological advances, and yes, some level of technological implementation is required. But that is the tip of a very large iceberg.</p> <p>Back in the midst of the digital transformation era, IT departments were rolling out CMSs, CRM systems, intranets, and so on. Meanwhile, on the customer-facing side, organizations were rolling out websites, apps, and personalization. But they were putting almost no emphasis on <em>how</em> people were going to use these systems.</p> <p>Internal systems were hard to use, and customers either didn’t care what was being built or wanted something else entirely.</p> <p>The same is happening with AI. Senior management are buying into the hype, and IT teams are rolling out Copilot Studio licenses company-wide and considering the job done. Sure, there is a bit of organization-wide generic training, but that’s about it.</p> <p>Just like before, staff are left wondering what to do with these new systems and feeling under pressure to use them and deliver results.</p> <blockquote>Organizations need to recognize that AI is a <strong>cultural shift as much as a technology</strong>, and that shift has enormous implications for their staff.</blockquote> <p>After all, this time around, it is even worse, because this time, staff fear for their jobs. They have read the same headlines that you have, and no amount of promises from senior management is going to make them feel any better.</p> Why Would Staff Want To Adopt AI? <p>I mean, think about it from a staff member’s point of view. AI offers them very little personally, even setting aside the fact that management may be intending to use it to replace them.</p> <p>The main reason senior management are excited about AI is that it provides additional <strong>productivity</strong>. People can do more in less time. But that does not benefit staff. They are still required to work 9 to 5, but now they are expected to do more in the same time. They don’t get any time back, and they don’t get paid more, so why would they embrace AI?</p> <blockquote>So before asking staff to embrace AI, organizations need to ask themselves how they intend to <strong>motivate</strong> them to do so.</blockquote> <p>One strategy is to help staff identify the parts of their job they hate and how AI can help them do those tasks more quickly and less painfully. People tend to be a lot more receptive to change when it is presented as an <strong>opportunity to improve their working lives</strong>.</p> <p>But, that should just be the beginning. Organizations may need to consider providing incentives for staff to adopt AI in terms of career progression, compensation, or shorter working hours.</p> Staff Need Time And Support To Adopt AI <p>Also, adopting AI is not a simple task. It involves challenging every working practice they have built up over (in some cases) decades. All of that <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_knowledge">procedural knowledge</a> has to be rebuilt. That is a lot to ask of anybody in even the best of circumstances, and most employees do not find themselves in the best of circumstances.</p> <p>Most are overworked and struggling to stay on top of their to-do lists. They don’t have time to sit down, learn a new tool, and figure out how it fits into their workflow.</p> <p>That doesn’t just take time. It also takes a lot of <strong>consideration</strong> and <strong>experimentation</strong>. It is not enough to just send a member of staff on a training course and expect them to be able to use AI to its full potential.</p> <p>At a minimum, staff need training specific to their roles, such as the <a href="https://better-ux-with-ai.boagworld.com/">AI training I provide to UX teams</a>. But organizations should be going even further.</p> <p>They need to develop sophisticated training programs that help staff from different departments understand how to use AI in their specific roles. They need ongoing support and the time to experiment with AI to find the best ways to use it.</p> <p>But formal training will only take you so far. In my experience, the most effective support comes from <strong>colleagues</strong>, which is why I would start with the enthusiasts rather than the resisters. Every organization has a handful of people who are already experimenting with AI, often without anybody asking them to. Bring those people together, give them the time and space to share what is working for them, and let their enthusiasm spread to everyone else.</p> <p>I saw this work during the digital transformation era too. In <em>Digital Adaptation</em>, I described an internal conference where staff showed their colleagues what they had built, and it did more for adoption than any memo from the top ever could. People are far more likely to try something when they see a colleague in a similar role using it successfully than when they are told to by senior management.</p> <blockquote>Rather than trying to win over the most resistant staff first, organizations should find the people already experimenting with AI, give them time to share what they have learned, and let social proof do the heavy lifting.</blockquote> <p>Unfortunately, experimentation comes with a big downside… mistakes will be made.</p> It Needs To Be Safe To Fail <p>Many organizations have, to be frank, a culture of fear where mistakes are seen as a sign of incompetence. However, by its very nature, learning a new tool like AI will involve making mistakes, and this can be a massive barrier to adoption.</p> <p>I saw this during the era of digital transformation. Senior management would demand digital transformation and were confused when it didn’t happen.</p> <p>What they didn’t realize was that they were sending mixed messages to staff. Sure, they were asking for digital transformation, but they were still demanding that the same key performance indicators (KPIs) be met, and they still pointed out any mistakes in their annual reviews.</p> <p>There was <strong>no space to fail</strong>, as the pressure to deliver was relentless.</p> <p>This time around, it seems to be even worse. I hear horror stories of staff being reprimanded for using too many tokens just weeks after being told to adopt AI. Or staff being lectured so much about what they should or should not do with AI that they are afraid to try anything. It is safer to just do what they have always done.</p> <blockquote>If management want staff to adopt AI, then they need to make staff <strong>feel safe to experiment</strong>. They need to adjust KPIs, change how staff are reviewed, and relax some of the governance while staff are learning.</blockquote> Handle Governance With Care <p>Look, I get it. AI cannot be rolled out without some governance in place, but again, this is an area that organizations have typically handled poorly.</p> <p>In the midst of digital transformation, governance would either be non-existent, or it would be so strict that it would stifle innovation, and unfortunately, I see the same pattern emerging with AI adoption.</p> <p>Staff need some governance to help them know where they stand. It helps them understand what is expected of them and what they can and cannot do.</p> <p>But too often organizations have been so strict that it is easier not to do anything at all. Pages of instructions about what people should and should not share with AI create that sense of fear that I mentioned earlier.</p> <blockquote>Organizations need a short set of principles that would fit on a handful of posters, similar to <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles">gov.uk’s design principles</a>. Beyond that, they need to demonstrate a degree of trust in their staff to use common sense.</blockquote> <p><img src="https://files.smashing.media/articles/is-your-organization-messing-up-ai-adoption/design-principle-tumblr.jpg" /></p> <p>Of course, mistakes will get made, but that is the cost of change, and so organizations need to accept that and do what they can to mitigate that in how they roll out the technology.</p> Adaptive Technological Rollout <p>That said, organizations will need to be careful in how they roll out AI from an IT perspective. Once again, it is easy to lock everything down and stifle the adoption of AI.</p> <p>Again, this is something I highlighted back in 2014 with digital transformation. Overstretched IT teams would pick a set of technologies and roll them out organization-wide, often with little or no consultation with the individuals who would be using them. Anything outside of that set of technologies had to go through a complex approval process, normally involving a steering committee. The result was a glacial pace of change that was one of the major contributing factors to the failure rate of digital transformation projects.</p> <p>Today we are seeing the same thing. IT teams are opting into a tool like Copilot and calling it a day, but that often falls massively short.</p> <p>Take, for example, a UX team I am working with at the moment. Management is pushing hard for AI adoption, and the team is excited about the potential of creating an AI-powered research repository and even AI personas. It sounds like a wonderful case study in AI adoption, with enthusiastic management, an excited team, and innovative ideas.</p> <p>Unfortunately, the reality is that nothing is happening because Copilot cannot deliver on the team’s vision, and so they are currently bogged down in bureaucracy as they try to persuade the AI steering group that they need to use a different toolset. Understandably, the team’s enthusiasm is waning. Why bother?</p> <p>And that team is one of the more patient ones. In many organizations, staff are not waiting for the steering group at all. Just as we saw shadow IT during the digital transformation era, with people signing up for Dropbox and Google Docs because the official tools could not do what they needed, we are now seeing shadow AI. When the in-house AI tool will not let people do their jobs, they open a personal ChatGPT or Claude account on their phone or home laptop and get on with it.</p> <p>That is far more dangerous than the problem the restrictions were meant to prevent. Company data ends up in tools the organization has no control over, with no oversight and no record of what has been shared. In trying to lock everything down, organizations end up pushing the risk somewhere they cannot see it.</p> <p>The instinctive reaction is to clamp down with stricter policies, blocked websites, and warnings about disciplinary action. But that rarely works, and it drives the behavior further underground. <strong>Shadow AI is a symptom</strong>, and a useful one, because it shows exactly where the official tools are falling short.</p> <blockquote>Instead of trying to stamp out shadow AI, organizations should learn from it. Talk to staff about what they are using personal accounts for and what they feel they cannot do with the AI they have been given, then use that to shape what gets rolled out next.</blockquote> <p>Look, I understand the cost of a messy, out-of-control tech stack. It is not ideal. However, in the early days of adoption, we may have to live with a bit of chaos while people explore the edges of what is possible and work out common use cases.</p> <p>Organizations need to allow staff to explore different uses of AI and even different AI apps to find what suits their specific needs in their specific roles. They can always start locking things down later as the real organizational needs become clearer.</p> A Dangerous Time <p>I admit all of this feels dangerous to some organizations. They want to control the AI adoption process centrally and make sure nothing goes wrong. I understand that. But change comes with a degree of risk, and the risk of moving too slowly is greater than the risk of moving too fast.</p> <p>However, a bigger danger still is when organizations see this as a technological challenge and overlook the human side of things. AI adoption is not simply about rolling out new technology. Just like digital transformation, it is about <strong>people</strong>, <strong>culture</strong>, and <strong>change management</strong>. The sooner organizations realize the scope of what they are dealing with, the better.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For most of my career, building software started with the same setup: keyboard, mouse, editor, terminal, browser, and an impressive number of tabs I was definitely going to look at later. The tools changed, but the relationship did not. Like most developers, I learned commands, memorized shortcuts, and adapted my thinking to the machine.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">AI is changing that relationship and not because I can prompt my way to some workable code. It&rsquo;s more because the system behind it can understand a repository, choose the right model, plan work, use tools, edit files, run commands, inspect failures, and keep going within boundaries the developer controls.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">We&rsquo;ve made it possible for a developer to describe an outcome, and GitHub Copilot can gather repository context, inspect symbols and dependencies, edit files, run tests, and revise the implementation. In the IDE, agent mode keeps that loop interactive. GitHub Copilot coding agent can take an issue into an isolated GitHub Actions-powered environment and return a pull request for review. This is not just faster coding. It is the shift from issuing commands to making intent executable. The developer still owns the outcome, but the work looks subtly different.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">AI-assisted development compresses that chain of events. We spend less time navigating tools and more time setting direction, supplying context, defining constraints, and reviewing evidence. The development workflow now is about understanding the structure of codebases, the customer, and what a good solution should feel like.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">This makes developers&rsquo; jobs even more important, and their time more valuable. It makes judgment more valuable. It makes taste more valuable. An agent can generate three implementations before you finish your tea, but someone still has to decide whether the abstraction belongs, the migration is safe, the tests prove anything useful, and the result solves the problem.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>That is the most important change in the craft: the developer remains accountable for the outcome, even as more mechanics are delegated.</strong></p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">So what does this mean for the emerging developer workflow? The goal is to embed AI into the workflow, not beside it. It&rsquo;s more of an ambient intelligence.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That requires portable context. MCP connects agents to tools and services; repository instructions and skills encode conventions; hooks enforce policy and validate output. Simpler interfaces need explicit context underneath.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">I see three layers: modality captures intent; the harness plans and acts; the control plane lets developers inspect, redirect, and approve. The best products connect all three without surrendering control.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Together, they let developers move across text, voice, and physical controls without losing context.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-why-physical-controls-are-interesting-again">Why physical controls are interesting again</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">I, and many others in the maker community, have been experimenting with ambient control devices, from inexpensive hackable picture frames to ESP32 devices with screens attached. Agentic coding is past just a keyboard and a terminal into alternative interfaces.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Getting this kind of technology into everyone&rsquo;s hands is something that I&rsquo;m very passionate about and so over the past 18 months we&rsquo;ve been working with Logitech on their MX Keypad and how it can help this new way of working.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The Logitech MX Keypad turns familiar developer actions into programmable inputs to accelerate workflows that are ambient and contextual to the work devs are doing. In GitHub Copilot app, a key can submit a parameterized prompt, open or resume a Copilot session, invoke an IDE command, or trigger a multi-step sequence. The hardware earns its place only when that shortcut removes enough friction to keep you in the code, but still gives you control.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Physical controls work when an action repeats often enough that removing a few steps preserves concentration. Not every developer needs another device on the desk, and not every interruption deserves a key. However, with this experience the developer decides which actions earn that shortcut and when the keyboard, command palette, or voice is better. Maintaining flow is the key, and a good interface gives some of that attention back.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">GUIs made software visual. Mouse made navigation easier. The web made it connected. Mobile made touch natural. Agents add intent and action. The interface now includes ambient control.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-interface-moves-into-the-background">The interface moves into the background</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That is why GitHub worked with Logitech to integrate the GitHub Copilot app directly into the experience. Developers can run agentic workflows in control, map a repeated prompt, start or resume a session, and decide through real use whether a physical control helps you stay in flow.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is still early, so the important questions are engineering questions. How much repository and organizational context should an agent retain? Which tool calls can run automatically? How should credentials, network access, and secrets be scoped? What evidence should accompany a change? How do multiple agents coordinate without duplicating work or creating conflicting pull requests? Products such as MX Keypad let us explore one practical part of that system: how developers invoke, steer, and resume agentic work without losing focus.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For decades, developers learned to speak computer: syntax, commands, APIs, shortcuts, and the occasional incantation copied from a seven-year-old issue comment. Now computers are getting better at meeting us halfway. The best interface will not have the most panels or AI buttons. It will understand enough context, preserve meaningful control, and get out of the way so we can build software that matters.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The goal is not to remove developers from the work. AI Agent Security Risks: What Can Go Wrong When Agents Act on Their Own

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The thing that makes an <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/what-are-ai-agents/" id="dkr_ai-agent-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">AI agent</a> useful, its freedom to act, is also what can introduce risk. An agent doesn&#8217;t just answer a question, it browses, runs code, and calls tools on its own, so a single bad or manipulated instruction can turn into real damage instead of just a wrong answer. That’s the shift traditional application security wasn’t built for, and it’s why <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/manufacturing-trust-for-ai-agents-keynote/" id="dkr_trust-has-to-be-built-into-the-system-around-the-agent-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">trust has to be built into the system around the agent</a>, not assumed.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">An agent decides its own path, picking tools at runtime, chaining actions together, and usually running with the credentials of whoever launched it. That autonomy is the source of most agent-specific risk, and it points to the two-part answer that runs through this post: contain what an agent can reach, and oversee what it does.</p> <div class="wp-block-ponyo-zeta organism toc-exclude"> <blockquote class="container"> <h2 class="wp-block-ponyo-heading text-lg"> Key takeaways </h2> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li>AI agents introduce risks traditional app security wasn&#8217;t built for: they choose tools, chain actions, and often run with your credentials.</li> <li>The main risks are prompt injection, tool misuse and poisoning, excessive autonomy, credential exposure, and data exfiltration.</li> <li>Most trace back to one thing: the agent can act, so containment and oversight matter more than the model.</li> <li>The two-part answer is isolation to limit blast radius and governance to oversee behavior.</li> </ul> </blockquote> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why agents break traditional security models</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">If you&#8217;ve secured web services, the model is familiar: requests arrive at known endpoints, deterministic logic handles them, and you design controls around that predictability. Agents break that assumption. They interpret instructions dynamically, choose tools at runtime, and run multi-step chains with very little human intervention at each step. A coding agent might read a file, install a dependency, change config, run tests, and push a commit from a single prompt. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Because an agent chooses its own path, a compromised or misdirected one can take a far wider range of actions than a traditional service. Every risk below has already produced <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/ai-coding-agent-horror-stories-security-risks/" id="dkr_documented-real-world-incidents-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">documented real-world incidents</a>, and they all follow from that shift.</p> <div class="wp-block-ponyo-image"> <img alt="Diagram of the top AI agent security risks including prompt injection, data exfiltration, tool misuse and poisoning, excessive autonomy, and credential and secrets exposure." class="fade-in" height="1218" src="https://www.docker.com/app/uploads/2026/10/docker_AI-Agent-Security-Risks-2320x1218.jpg" title="- docker AI Agent Security Risks" width="2320" /> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">1. Prompt injection</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Prompt injection is the risk that defines agent security. Because an agent acts on whatever text it reads, an attacker doesn&#8217;t need access to your systems. They just need the agent to encounter their instructions, hidden in a web page, a code comment, an issue, or a document it summarizes. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">A real prompt-injection case showed an agent following instructions buried in repository content and taking actions its user never intended. </p> <div class="wp-block-ponyo-table"> <table class="responsive-table"> <tbody class="wp-block-ponyo-table-body"> <tr class="wp-block-ponyo-table-row"> <td class="wp-block-ponyo-cell"> <span class="responsive-table-label"></span> <span class="responsive-table-value"> <span class="responsive-table-value-content"> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Incident highlight:</strong> A coding agent followed instructions <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/mcp-horror-stories-github-prompt-injection/" id="dkr_buried-in-a-repositorys-own-content-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">buried in a repository&#8217;s own content</a> and took actions its user never intended. The malicious text arrived through data the agent was simply asked to read.</p> </span> </span> </td> </tr> </tbody> </table> </div> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The agent has no reliable way to tell a legitimate instruction from an injected one, so the defense is to limit what a hijacked agent can reach.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">2. Tool misuse and tool poisoning</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Agents act through tools: API connectors, database queries, file operations, and code execution. Every tool is an access vector, and two things could go wrong. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">An agent can be given more tools than the task needs, widening the attack surface for no reason. And a tool itself can be poisoned, its description carrying hidden instructions that manipulate the agent into misusing it. The mitigations are to scope tools to the task at runtime, source them from <a href="https://www.docker.com/products/mcp-catalog-and-toolkit/" id="dkr_registries-with-verified-provenance-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">registries with verified provenance</a>, and review tool descriptions rather than trusting them by default.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">3. Excessive autonomy</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The more an agent can do without review, the more a single bad decision costs. Excessive autonomy is the risk of granting an agent broad, standing permission to act, most visibly when developers run <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/what-is-yolo-mode/" id="dkr_yolo-mode-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">YOLO mode</a>, where every action is auto-approved. Nothing in the setup gave a human a chance to catch it before it executed.</p> </span> </span> </td> </tr> </tbody> </table> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">4. Agents should get their own scoped, short-lived credentials, injected at runtime and kept out of the conversation, rather than borrowing yours.</p> <div class="wp-block-ponyo-table"> <table class="responsive-table"> <tbody class="wp-block-ponyo-table-body"> <tr class="wp-block-ponyo-table-row"> <td class="wp-block-ponyo-cell"> <span class="responsive-table-label"></span> <span class="responsive-table-value"> <span class="responsive-table-value-content"> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Incident highlight:</strong> An AWS engineer handed a small Cost Explorer bug to a coding agent running with their own operator-level access. The result was a thirteen-hour regional outage.</p> </span> </span> </td> </tr> </tbody> </table> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">5. Data exfiltration and untrusted handoffs</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">An agent with network access and sensitive information in its context can send that data somewhere it shouldn&#8217;t, whether through a compromised tool or an injected instruction.
