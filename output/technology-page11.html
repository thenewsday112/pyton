I Used AI to Fake a Scientific Paper. Here’s What Happened

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<img src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/man-in-glasses-at-night-his-face-reflected-in-a-window-beside-him.jpg?id=68515605&amp;width=1245&amp;height=700&amp;coordinates=0%2C1445%2C0%2C1446" /><br /><br /><p><strong>Science has a spam</strong> problem. And the spam filter is getting clogged up.</p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-rebelmouse-image" style="display: none;"> <img alt="“text”" class="rm-shortcode" id="623fc" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/text.png?id=67033315&amp;width=980" /></p><p>According to all reports, academic publishing is drowning in what has come to be called AI slop: low-quality, mass-produced content generated with AI. By 2025, an estimated <a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2605754123" target="_blank">57 percent of all published academic articles</a> exhibited detectable evidence of large language model (LLM) influence, a massive jump from 12 percent in 2023. What’s more, <a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00319v2" target="_blank">over 12 percent of <em><em>Nature Communications</em></em> reviews</a> were found to be completely AI-generated in 2025.</p><div class="rm-embed embed-media"></div><p><span>Given that for every 40 papers showing statistical evidence of AI usage in 2025, </span><a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2526734123" target="_blank">only one formally disclosed it</a><span>, these figures likely grossly underestimate LLM involvement today. Of course, evidence of AI use doesn’t necessarily mean a paper is slop. But some estimates suggest that hundreds of thousands of fake, often AI-generated, publications are now produced annually.</span></p><p>“It’s not just the [scientific] record under threat, it’s science as a whole,” warns <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/sulicz/" target="_blank">Amanda Sulicz</a>, IEEE’s research-integrity manager. “AI slop is completely eroding trust in science and every scientific process that we’ve worked for centuries to create.”</p><p>Yet statistics and statements claiming that the problem is big—perhaps even existential for science—don’t really get the message across. Just how easy is it to use AI to create a convincing but completely fake paper? And without adding even more to the burden on stretched peer reviewers, who do this work on a volunteer basis, do publishers have the tools to keep AI slop out of the scientific record?</p><h3></h3><br /><div class="rm-embed embed-media"><div class="flourish-embed flourish-pictogram"><noscript><img alt="pictogram visualization" src="https://public.flourish.studio/visualisation/30581736/thumbnail" width="100%" /></noscript></div></div><p><br /></p><div class="rm-embed embed-media"><div class="flourish-embed flourish-chart"><noscript><img alt="chart visualization" src="https://public.flourish.studio/visualisation/30581953/thumbnail" width="100%" /></noscript></div></div><h3></h3><br /><p>Borrowing from the idea of “vibe coding,” in which programmers use natural-language prompts to generate code, I decided to “vibe-research” a made-up paper by prompting LLMs to generate hypotheses, code, and text with minimal human input. Then I asked <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/jenny-mahoney-b03a683/" target="_blank">Jenny Mahoney</a>, managing editor of the multidisciplinary and open-access journal <a href="https://ieeeaccess.ieee.org/" target="_blank"><em>IEEE Access</em></a><em>,</em> if we could war-game an attempt to sneak my AI-generated monstrosity past her journal’s defenses. She and IEEE kindly agreed.</p><p>The experiment would become a two-part test. First, whether someone with no special AI expertise could use off-the-shelf AI tools to generate a convincing paper describing research that had never been performed, and do it well enough to fool a publisher’s defenses. Then I would raise the stakes: What happens when more advanced AI systems don’t just write the paper but conduct underlying research?</p><p>Along the way, the experiment became a window into how AI automation is transforming scientific discovery and forcing stakeholders to rethink the entire academic publishing system.</p><h2>Round 1: Creating the Forgery</h2><p>To give me a chance at creating a convincing fake, I gave myself permission to prompt multiple times (within reason). But to make the fight fair, I couldn’t access the most advanced paid versions of LLMs—only free ChatGPT (at the time, GPT-5.3/5.2 Mini) and Gemini (3 Flash). Moreover, I could not directly edit any output, nor ask for outside help from expert AI-prompt engineers. This needed to be an amateur effort, with the paper generated as much as possible by the LLMs alone.</p><h3></h3><br /><img alt="Person working at a desk in a lit garden office at night, seen through window" class="rm-shortcode" id="bb404" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/person-working-at-a-desk-in-a-lit-garden-office-at-night-seen-through-window.jpg?id=68516072&amp;width=980" /><h3></h3><br /><p>I assumed that using fake author names and affiliations would immediately raise a red flag, and I didn’t want to drag the names of established academics into the experiment, so the best bet was to name myself as the sole author. That choice limited me to subjects in which I had some academic experience. So I chose my Ph.D. topic from nearly two decades ago: optical solitons, which are individual light pulses that propagate without any change to their shape.</p><p>My first attempt—“Can you vibe-research me an original paper in optical solitons?”—left a lot to be desired. Though Gemini was happy to do the task (unlike ChatGPT, which was more forceful in not generating synthetic data or studies), the result was underwhelming. And it highlighted a recurring problem during the process: Without any real research to work with, the output was laughably thin, with the LLMs often struggling to fill four pages. However, as I became more adept at prompting the models, they loosened up and became more characteristically loquacious.</p><p>I broke the paper down into its constituent parts—abstract, introduction, methods, and so on—and tackled them one by one. If one LLM refused to go any further, switching to the other and asking what the next step would look like “hypothetically” would often do the trick. After a dozen or so prompts, I had a realistic-looking paper. A dozen or so more prompts generated and tidied up the figures and captions, produced references and citations, and converted the paper into <em>IEEE Access</em>’s required style.</p><p>At this point, I read the full paper carefully. To me, it looked pretty good. The science sounded convincing. I even had visions of returning to academia to conduct in real life the experiments that the LLMs had proposed.</p><p>What’s more, I couldn’t see any obvious telltale “AI-isms” that would immediately be flagged. For example, the LLMs didn’t produce any tortured phrases—unusual paraphrases of standard technical terms, like “profound neural organization” replacing “deep neural network.”</p><p>Other AI-isms were arguably present, involving stylistic tics like hedging language (“potentially, typically”), grouping things in threes (“The study provides a clear, concise, and actionable framework”), and inserting formulaic transitions (“In summary,…”). But as the previous sentence demonstrates, these tics are hard to spot because they are often used by unimaginative humans, too.</p><h2>Round 1: Attempt to Publish</h2><h3>Two Ways to Make a Paper With AI</h3><br /><img alt="Illustration of digital papers overlaid on a blue circuit board pattern." class="rm-shortcode" id="ce350" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/illustration-of-digital-papers-overlaid-on-a-blue-circuit-board-pattern.png?id=68516003&amp;width=980" /><p><strong>I took two very different routes to creating a scientific paper with AI. First, I coaxed free, general-purpose chatbots through the process step by step; then I turned to FARS, an autonomous research system designed to do much of the scientific work itself.</strong></p><p style="font-size: 22px;"><strong>ROUND 1: CHATGPT + GEMINI</strong></p><p><strong>1. Choose a research topic</strong> <br />I ask the free chatbots to create an original paper on optical solitons, my former Ph.D. field.</p><p><strong>2. Break the paper into pieces</strong> <br />The initial results are thin, so I prompt separately for the abstract, introduction, methods, and other sections.</p><p><strong>3. Keep steering the LLMs</strong> <br />When one model refuses or stalls, I switch models, rephrase prompts, and ask what the next step would look like “hypothetically.”</p><p><strong>4. Build and polish the paper</strong> <br />After about two dozen prompts, the LLMs have generated the text, figures, captions, references, and citations and formatted the paper for <em><em>IEEE Access</em></em>.</p><p><strong>5. Put it to the test</strong> <br />I send the fake paper to <em><em>IEEE Access</em></em>, where automated integrity checks flag fabricated and unverifiable references.</p><p>→ <strong>CAUGHT</strong> <br />IEEE’s integrity checks flag fabricated and unverifiable references.<br /><br /></p><div class="horizontal-rule"></div><p style="font-size: 22px;"><strong>ROUND 2: FARS</strong></p><p><strong>1. Give FARS a research topic</strong> <br />I enter an abbreviated version of the same optical-soliton topic. FARS generates a research proposal in 21 minutes.</p><p><strong>2. Authorize the experiment</strong> <br />With a click, I tell FARS to carry out the experiment described in its proposal.</p><p><strong>3. Let FARS do the research</strong> <br />FARS conducts the computational experiment and emails me the next day to say it’s complete.</p><p><strong>4. Tell FARS to write the paper</strong> <br />Without checking the results, I click “Write paper.” Two hours later, FARS produces a submission-ready article.</p><p><strong>5. Put it to the test</strong> <br />I remove FARS’s disclosure, put my name on the paper, and send it to <em><em>IEEE Access</em></em>.</p><p>→ <strong>CAUGHT</strong> <br />The references are real, but IEEE flags the paper’s heavy reliance on non-peer-reviewed preprints.</p><h3></h3><br /><p>Handing the fake paper over to <em>IEEE</em> <em>Access</em> left me torn. After putting in a fair amount of effort into making the AI-generated forgery, would I be happy if it passed through the checkers, or saddened that the only remaining line of defense for publishers is the evermore-burdened peer-review community? And if it was spotted, would I be angry that I didn’t think of a red flag or be glad the checkers did their job?</p><p>As it turned out, I was filled with relief when the paper got picked up quickly for having hallucinated references, otherwise known as ghost citations. When I ran the paper through Gemini afterward, without qualm it happily reported that seven “modern LLM-related research references have been fabricated to support the paper’s specific narrative.” Thanks for the heads up, Gemini!</p><p>In fact, Gemini underestimated how many ghost citations there were. When Mahoney ran the paper through a research-integrity checker called <a href="https://stm-assoc.org/what-we-do/strategic-areas/research-integrity/integrity-hub/" target="_blank">STM Integrity Hub</a>, she picked up 12, and another checker from <a href="https://molecularconnections.com/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">MolecularConnections</a> could not validate 24 references, including one that had the classic tell of “John Smith” as a single author (no offense to all the academically inclined John Smiths out there). “I am an experimental physicist by training, so this was not at all my area, but nothing seemed majorly scientifically <em>wrong</em>” about the paper, says Mahoney. “It was just that so many hallucinated references immediately cast doubt on the entire work.”</p><p>Feedback on the fake paper from <a href="https://optow.ele.uniroma3.it/assanto.php" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Gaetano Assanto</a>, an expert in optical solitons and a former collaborator of mine from Roma Tre University, in Italy, was similarly mixed. Concentrating on the paper itself, he thought it looked professionally written. But he noted that the mathematics was very dense (“too much math for me in a single shot!”) and would require specialized knowledge to evaluate. And, although the text looked legitimate, Assanto noted an excessive use of acronyms in the abstract, and he was suspicious of a couple of confidently proclaimed physics statements that were at best debatable.</p><h2>The AI Arms Race</h2><p>On one level, this outcome was positive: A complete novice using free tools couldn’t get an AI-faked paper past the publishers’ automated defenses or a potential peer reviewer’s eagle eye. But it was hardly a resounding victory for the scientific publishing system. With general-purpose LLMs and no special prompting expertise, I had managed to conjure a superficially plausible scientific paper out of research that had never happened. And more advanced LLMs are no longer making the kind of mistakes their free, dumber relatives are making.</p><h3></h3><br /><p>These tools aim to address a serious bottleneck to scientific discovery: us. </p><h3></h3><br /><p>It’s common knowledge in the academic publishing world that, in the cat-and-mouse game between bad actors and publishers, AI slop papers continue to slip through publishers’ safeguards. “It feels like building a detection tool today may not be effective with the AI-created paper of tomorrow,” says <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/luigi-longobardi-38390819/" target="_blank">Luigi Longobardi</a>, IEEE’s director of publishing ethics and conduct. This leaves the humble peer reviewer as a publisher’s last line of defense. Yet peer review is simply not fit to handle the volume of papers—both legitimate and slop—passing through.</p><p>Even before AI tools became ubiquitous, the peer-review system was being pushed to its breaking point, processing well <a href="https://ncses.nsf.gov/pubs/nsb202333" target="_blank">over 3 million papers</a> in 2022. Fundamentally, this problem stems from science’s publish-or-perish culture, which pressures academics to constantly publish papers in prestigious journals to secure funding and advance their careers. This culture not only forces many academics to regularly churn out incremental research, but has also long driven some to enlist the help of “paper mills”—unethical organizations that produce low-quality or fabricated papers—just to keep pace. AI only amplifies these existing dynamics.</p><h2>Burden on Academics</h2><p>Mahoney says <em>IEEE Access</em> alone receives about 60,000 submissions per year, accepting around 20 percent, or about 12,000 papers. Every accepted paper is reviewed by at least two independent reviewers. But despite the journal’s roster of tens of thousands of peer reviewers, it’s getting harder and harder to secure their services, with the average number of reviewer invitations being sent out for each article increasing by 50 percent since 2018. For these volunteers, peer review is a significant commitment on top of teaching and conducting their own research. Now they’re also being asked to spot AI slop.</p><p>Of course, peer review is not the end of scrutiny for any paper. Once it has entered the scientific record, it must then pass muster with the scientific community. This is where thousands of papers with telltale signs of inappropriate AI use (and other forms of research misconduct) have been exposed.</p><h3>What Gave Away My AI Papers </h3><br /><p><strong>Round 1: The Fake Paper</strong></p><p><em>Created with free versions of ChatGPT and Gemini</em></p><p><strong>Ghost citations<br /></strong><br />Many references were fabricated or couldn’t be validated.<br /><strong>Questionable scientific claims<br /></strong><br />An expert flagged confidently stated claims that were at best debatable.<br /><strong>Too many acronyms<br /></strong><br />The expert also noted excessive use of acronyms in the abstract.</p><p><strong>Round 2: The AI-Research Paper</strong></p><p><strong></strong><em><em>Created by the autonomous research system FARS</em></em></p><p><strong>Too many preprints<br /></strong><br />The references were real, but too many pointed to non-peer-reviewed preprints.<br /><strong>Nonsense in a figure<br /></strong><br />One technical figure contained a line labeled “lhewl.”<br /><strong>AI artifacts<br /></strong><br />Another figure’s depiction of an AI “brain” contained telltale random dots.</p><h3></h3><br /><p><strong></strong>There are two high-profile homes for this important and completely volunteer-led post-publication review work. <a href="https://pubpeer.com/" target="_blank">PubPeer</a> is a place where researchers, often called scientific sleuths, can anonymously identify flaws in the literature. And <a href="https://retractionwatch.com/" target="_blank">Retraction Watch</a> documents and publicizes corrections to the scientific record.</p><p>Even the people who track problems in the scientific literature can find themselves caught up in AI fabrications. <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/ivanoransky/" target="_blank">Ivan Oransky</a>, cofounder of Retraction Watch and a member of the PubPeer Foundation’s board of directors, has encountered papers containing fabricated references attributed to him. In one case, a <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40199-025-00580-z" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">journal article</a> criticizing post-publication peer review included a fabricated reference supposedly written by Oransky himself—“which was sort of funny,” he chuckles.</p><p>Oransky argues that AI has exposed long-standing weaknesses in the scientific publishing system rather than creating an entirely new problem: “What I liken it to is a fire, where publish-or-perish has always been there as kindling, paper mills are the gasoline, and AI is the accelerant.” But though concerning, the crisis could create an opportunity, he says. “If I’m trying to be optimistic, maybe AI slop and the volume of submissions could make more people aware of those problems and try and solve them at a fundamental first-principles level.”</p><h2>Blurred Lines</h2><p>My homemade forgery had failed for reasons that today’s publishers know how to spot. But that raised a more unsettling question: What happens when AI isn’t simply fabricating a paper but conducting research that blurs the line between legitimate science and AI fabrication?</p><p>Making my vibe-researched paper look like a cave drawing, new automated research systems are handing increasingly large parts of the scientific process over to AI. Tools including <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31651" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">FARS (Fully Automated Research System)</a>, <a href="https://github.com/aiming-lab/AutoResearchClaw" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">AutoResearchClaw</a>, <a href="https://github.com/sakanaai/ai-scientist-v2" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">The AIScientist-v2</a>, <a href="https://www.intology.ai/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Zochi</a>, <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18705" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">AI Researcher</a>, and <a href="https://deepscientist.cc/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">DeepScientist</a> all aim to address a serious bottleneck to scientific discovery: us.</p><h3></h3><br /><p><span>What happens when AI </span>isn’t simply fabricating a paper but conducting research that blurs the line between legitimate science and AI fabrication? </p><h3></h3><br /><p>Often with just a single prompt, these AI engines can conduct a comprehensive literature search, from which they can generate a completely reasonable research proposal. They can design and conduct experiments using digital datasets and simulations and then write a full research paper—all with no human input after the initial prompt. Very recent systems are even beginning to go beyond the digital realm. <a href="https://insilico.com/news/tmikccj2f1-advancing-drug-discovery-from-automation" target="_blank">LabClaw</a>, for example, now links to autonomous labs that can generate real-world data for tasks such as drug discovery.</p><p>The output is, to most intents and purposes, actual research, and therefore potentially worthy of publication. But with humans in the loop in only the most nominal sense, these tools bring into question the meaning of the term “legitimate science.”</p><p>FARS, which caused a stir this year when it created <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31651" target="_blank">166 papers in 417 hours</a>, was developed by the Singapore-based startup <a href="https://www.crunchbase.com/organization/analemma" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Analemma</a>. “We built FARS because research is still constrained by human attention, fragmented tools, scarce technical labor, and slow experimental iteration,” explains <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/suxiwen/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Xiwen Su</a>, Analemma’s manager of product operations and partnerships. “FARS can pursue many research threads simultaneously, 24/7, without fatigue, and at scale.” (The online service that I used to access FARS was discontinued after Analemma was <a href="https://baike.baidu.com/fr/item/Analemme/3430299" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">acquired by Baidu</a> while this article was being reported.)</p><p>Su and the team behind Analemma kindly agreed to let me loose on FARS and gave me enough credits to create a paper. Which gave me another opportunity to put Mahoney’s research-integrity checkers to the test.</p><h2>Round 2: Using an AI Science Engine</h2><p>FARS is not an LLM in and of itself, but rather an agentic AI-orchestration system connected to a computing cluster that has access to essentially any open- or closed-source LLM it needs. The system uses four specialized agents for ideation, planning, conducting the experiment, and writing, all designed to perform the main tasks of science autonomously.</p><p>Though FARS initially focused on AI research, Su told me it should be reasonably effective across other domains. As I wanted a topic similar to that of my first fake paper, I pasted in an abridged version of that paper’s title—“LLM-aided Optical Soliton Dynamics Modelling for Next-Gen Communications”—into the prompt box and hit “Send.” Just 21 minutes later, FARS generated a research proposal based on an extremely detailed literature review—far more than I or any other human could have achieved in that time.</p><p>With a click, I tasked FARS with conducting the experiment it detailed in its research proposal. The next day, I received an email saying my experiment was complete. Without checking the results, I pressed a button marked “Write paper,” and the final submission-ready article was produced in 2 hours.</p><p>At this point, I finally had a look at some of the output. At first glance, the paper looked convincingly human-written. The only giveaway was a line just below the abstract reading: “WARNING: This paper was generated by an automated research system.” I deleted that, put my name in its place, and sent it to Mahoney.</p><h2>Round 2: Attempt to Publish</h2><p>Because FARS is designed to spot gaps in the scientific literature and conduct real research to close them, I was more confident it would breeze through the publisher’s checks. But just as my fake paper fell down in the references, so too did the AI-generated real one.</p><p>“Your fake article had lots of hallucinated references,” says Mahoney. “While this AI-generated one had no fake references, our team would have rejected this paper because it did have a huge percentage of references to preprints, which likely has something to do with the dataset the AI has access to.” Because preprints haven’t yet undergone peer review, <em>IEEE</em> <em>Access</em> considers heavy reliance on them a problem.</p><p>In addition, although the research-integrity checking tools didn’t spot any issues with figures, Mahoney did. One figure included a red line labeled with the nonsense term “lhewl,” and an AI “brain” in another figure had some random dots in it, which ironically were a tell that it was AI generated.</p><h2>Automation Changing the Landscape</h2><p>Automated research systems are very much a work in progress, but they are already starting to make waves in the scientific community. For instance, an <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10265-5" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">AI Scientist academic paper</a> and <a href="https://www.autoscience.ai/blog/meet-carl" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">papers from the Autoscience Institute</a> were accepted through double-blind peer review in 2025 by the International Conference on Learning Representations. As these systems improve and start to produce research outputs at volume, the waves are likely to build to a tsunami that will threaten today’s scientific and publishing process.</p><p>“FARS doesn’t kill the paper, but it does challenge the paper as the<em> unit of knowledge</em>,” argues Su. “We believe the future looks more like a living knowledge graph—where individual, well-scoped contributions (some made by humans, some by machines) connect and build on each other dynamically.”</p><h3></h3><br /><img alt="Man in glasses working at a desktop computer in a dimly lit home office." class="rm-shortcode" id="42e61" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/man-in-glasses-working-at-a-desktop-computer-in-a-dimly-lit-home-office.jpg?id=68530754&amp;width=980" /><h3></h3><br /><p>In this future, humans will continue to have roles in the scientific and publication processes—just very different and limited ones. For example, instead of conducting experiments themselves, humans would sense-check and review automated output at key points. And traditional line-by-line peer-reviewing will be a thing of the past, though Su emphasizes that this change will not eliminate human judgment. “Automated systems can handle reproducibility checks, statistical validation, and novelty screening,” she says. “Humans can then focus on the harder question: Does this [research] matter?”</p><h2>A Human-Centric or AI-Driven Future?</h2><p>Though from a very different world than Su, Mahoney agrees that humans need to be freed to focus on the science, and that technology holds the key. “It’s promising to see the progress that has been made to automate some of the triage tasks, such as author checks, tortured phrases, duplicate submission, reference validity, et cetera,” she says. “During peer review, we’re also using AI semantic search tools to help editors match manuscripts to the most relevant reviewers, who are best placed to stop the slop before entering the scientific record.”</p><p>In addition, Mahoney thinks that AI cannot be banned or overly restricted, as this will stymie progress at a time when human knowledge could and should be growing at an unprecedented rate. “Researchers are increasingly using AI in their work, from literature discovery, to code generation, to data analysis, and this is not necessarily a bad thing,” she argues. But she disagrees that peer reviewers and humans more broadly will play a smaller part in scientific progress; Mahoney believes the future still requires humans at the wheel. The question is not just whether the research matters, she says. “The question should be: Can this work be trusted and reproduced?”</p><p><span></span>To this end, Longobardi argues that AI may ultimately require scientists to prove not just what they discovered but how they discovered it. One possibility, he says, is using technology to verify research at the source. “The system doesn’t really help because it’s not designed to not trust the paper,” he explains. “Maybe institutions can think about developing their own trust markers, automatically integrating the equipment researchers use with blockchain technology to say ‘Yes, this person was in the lab on this day and got this data.’”</p><p>Elements of this approach are already emerging. For instance, two departments within the Max Planck Institute of Colloids and Interfaces have implemented a sample database, assigning each and every sample created under their roofs with a unique ID and QR code. And the University of Southampton’s School of Chemistry and Chemical Engineering has rolled out an <a href="https://cches.co.uk/electronic-lab-notebooks/" target="_blank">electronic lab notebook</a> system. These innovative safeguards represent early efforts toward keeping humans front and center in scientific progress, and making the scientific process itself more transparent.</p><p>Whether this human-centric vision, Su’s AI-driven aspiration, or some other picture of the future wins out remains to be seen. The one thing my vibe-research adventure has clarified is that business-as-usual science is not an option. Scientists and publishers must adapt to a world in which it’s becoming increasingly difficult to spot the difference between real research and AI forgeries, and where deciding what counts as legitimate scientific work may become even harder than detecting outright fraud.</p><h3>See the AI-Generated Papers</h3><br /><p>Benjamin Skuse created two papers for this experiment, both screened by <em>IEEE Access</em> with the publisher’s cooperation. Neither was accepted for publication.</p><p><strong><a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/files/114035/AI-generated_paper_round_1.pdf" target="_blank">Paper 1: The AI-Generated Forgery</a><br /></strong>Created with ChatGPT and Gemini, this paper describes research that never happened.</p><p><strong><a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/files/114041/AI-generated_paper_round_2.pdf" target="_blank">Paper 2: The AI-Generated Research Paper</a></strong><br />Created with Analemma’s FARS system, this paper describes computational research conducted by AI.</p><p><em>Neither paper was peer reviewed, and their scientific claims have not been independently validated.</em></p>

Industrial Intelligence: The next growth opportunity for manufacturing

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Every manufacturer has its own industrial intelligence. Some is documented, and some resides in the knowledge of experienced workers.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Consider an experienced operator preparing to restart a line after a changeover. The machine settings matter, but so does knowing how a particular material behaves, what happened during the previous shift, and when an apparently minor variation deserves a closer look. A procedure can describe the sequence, but successful execution depends on the judgment that comes from experience and the organization’s accumulated know-how.</p> <figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img alt="" class="wp-image-1147564" height="1688" src="https://wp.technologyreview.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/iStock-1367303200.jpg?w=840" width="3000" /></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That combination is industrial intelligence: a manufacturer’s accumulated know-how, operating practices, production data, and understanding of its own business.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The opportunity now is to make more of that intelligence available wherever decisions are made, then connect it to the physical world of manufacturing. The strategic destination is not more agents, but rather to turn the existing business intelligence into an engine for profitable growth.</p> <h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Individual knowledge can be translated into organizational advantage</strong></h3> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Expertise has limited reach when it stays with one person, inside one system, or within one plant. Leaders now must determine how to extend that reach without stripping away the context that makes the knowledge useful.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For example, an engineer evaluating a process change needs more than a production record. They need to understand the circumstances behind it: the equipment configuration, the quality requirements, and the trade-offs an operator made. Connecting those elements can help people apply experience beyond the place where it originated.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">When individual knowledge becomes widely accessible, innovation can spread to the maintenance workshop, the planning office, the engineering team, and the production floor. More people can contribute because more people can work with what the organization knows.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">But access to knowledge is only part of the opportunity. Manufacturing ultimately is physical, and it depends on materials, machines, and products.</p> <h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>When intelligence participates in action</strong></h3> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Physical AI describes systems that can perceive conditions, reason about them, and take action within the physical world. By connecting machines and processes, manufacturers get a clearer view of what is happening on the factory floor. Agents can then reason on top of that information to identify patterns, predict problems, and determine the best course of action. Physical AI brings business intelligence into the physical process, allowing machines and systems to respond to changing conditions.</p> <figure class="wp-block-video"><video controls="controls" height="1080" src="https://wp.technologyreview.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/FINAL-Physical-AI-2026-09-17.mp4" width="1920"></video></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Consider a robotic-handling operation encountering variation in the parts arriving at its station. Vision can help the system assess a part’s position. A model informed by the material and task can help determine an appropriate movement. Connected controls can then execute an adjustment within validated limits. The value is not merely seeing the variation; it is responding appropriately.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The same principle applies to quality operations. When <a href="https://www.microsoft.com/en/customers/story/26367-toyota-industries-corporation-azure">Toyota Industries</a> connected previously fragmented paint shop data, engineering and operations teams were given the context needed to understand defect drivers. The company reports that analysis cycles fell from five days to under four hours, while its pilot reduced relevant paint defects by about 25%. The breakthrough was not simply identifying another signal; it was helping people understand why defects were occurring and respond before the outcome was locked in.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is the type of situation where industrial intelligence has become essential. Data may reveal what is happening, but industrial intelligence provides the context for what should happen, which constraints cannot be breached, and when uncertainty requires human judgment.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Simulation offers a way to examine proposed actions before applying them to equipment. Manufacturers can explore how a change might affect a process in a digital environment. That does not remove the need for physical validation, but it creates room to learn without treating the production line as the first experiment.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Responsible deployment, therefore, begins with operational discipline. Leaders need to establish clear authority or what systems may change, reliable connections to equipment, defined safety boundaries, and a record of decisions and actions. People must be able to recognize uncertainty, intervene, and bring equipment to a safe state. Trust rests on those practical arrangements, not on confidence in a model alone.</p> <h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>More capable machines, more capable people</strong></h3> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Manufacturing has long combined human ingenuity with increasingly capable tools. Physical AI extends that relationship. Intelligent equipment can take on suitable repetitive or physically demanding tasks, while agent-assisted operations can help people navigate information-intensive decisions and changing conditions.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The aim is not to make machines smarter; it is to see what people can build when knowledge can travel, intelligence can act, and ambition has more room to become reality.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://news.microsoft.com/source/features/digital-transformation/rockwell-automation-pairs-ai-with-decades-of-shop-floor-know-how-so-workers-can-solve-glitches-faster/">Rockwell Automation</a> illustrates the point. At its Singapore plant, technicians use a maintenance copilot that combines manufacturing data, machine manuals, and knowledge captured from experienced workers. This system has reduced the time required for new workers to learn how to troubleshoot hundreds of machines from nine months to three. An engineer could explore more process alternatives before committing to a change. An operator could help improve the system’s response rather than repeatedly managing the same exception.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">This type of progress requires investment in equipment but also in people: training, time to learn, and playing a meaningful role in shaping how systems work. Machines become more capable so people can become more capable.</p> <h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Industrial Intelligence is the answer to sustainable growth</strong></h3> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Industrial intelligence connects what a manufacturer knows: the experience of its people, the signals from its operations, and the lessons accumulated over time. Physical AI gives that intelligence a way to act in the physical world, turning knowledge into decisions and decisions into action.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The next challenge for industry is not simply to produce more, but to grow without adding complexity at the same rate. Intelligence can help manufacturers turn experience into scalable capability—improving productivity, accelerating innovation, expanding capacity, and enabling people to solve problems that were previously difficult to tackle.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The next frontier of industry is not smarter machines or more automated factories; it is businesses that can continuously learn, adapt, and act. The opportunity is to turn the intelligence embedded in every operation into a compounding advantage for growth.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"></p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em><a href="https://info.microsoft.com/ww-landing-industrial-intelligence?lcid=en-us">Watch the video series</a> to learn how industrial customers are unlocking intelligence across the value chain, and join Microsoft at <a href="https://ignite.microsoft.com/en-US/home">Microsoft Ignite</a> to see what’s next for Industrial Intelligence.</em></p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em>This content was produced by Microsoft. <img src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/a-medium-sized-box-with-an-open-lid-inside-is-a-lock-mechanism-a-battery-pack-and-some-circuit-boards.png?id=68493268&amp;width=1245&amp;height=700&amp;coordinates=0%2C385%2C0%2C386" /><br /><br /><p>Like many a nerd, I have a certain fascination with locks. This originates in reading the memoir<a href="https://archive.org/details/surely-you-re-joking-mister-feynman-richard-feynman/page/136/mode/2up" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank"> <em><em>Surely You’re Joking, Mr. Feynman</em></em></a> as a teenager, in which Richard Feynman recounts picking locks and cracking combinations while at his day job working on <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/trinity-nuclear-test" target="_self">the Manhattan Project</a> at Los Alamos. I currently have a couple of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locksport" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">locksport</a> pick sets and training locks that I purchased at hacker conferences over the years (although I’m still terrible at it), and I delight in the YouTube exploits of folks like the <a href="https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCm9K6rby98W8JigLoZOh6FQ" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">LockPickingLawyer</a>.</p><p>All of which left me wondering if I could try building an interesting lock. One dodge that lockmakers have long used to challenge would-be plunderers is <a href="https://www.facebook.com/watch/?v=421986253303149" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">to conceal the existence</a> of the lock itself. A modern variant of this is the magnetic lock, where a magnet is held or slid against an innocuous portion of a door or box, releasing a catch. The problem with this is that, once you guess the location of the lock, <em><em>any</em></em> strong magnet will open it.</p><p>So I started thinking about embedding an <a href="https://ibspot.com/us/products/rixiang-rfid-electronic-cabinet-lock-hidden-diy-for-wooden-cabinet-locker-drawer-cupboard-latch-lock" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">RFID reader under the surface</a> and resigning myself to keeping track of yet another access card. But then I stumbled across something much more interesting: SparkFun’s US $100 <a href="https://www.sparkfun.com/sparkfun-magnetic-imaging-tile-8x8.htm" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">magnetic imaging tile</a>. I realized I could use this to build a magnetic lock that opens with a programmable secret gesture.</p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-rebelmouse-image rm-float-left rm-resized-container rm-resized-container-25" style="float: left;"> <img alt="Major electronic components of the lockbox." class="rm-shortcode" id="6921a" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/major-electronic-components-of-the-lockbox.png?id=68493271&amp;width=980" /> <small class="image-media media-caption">I used a few discrete components to drive the servo motor and put the lock into program mode [top], controlled by a Teensy 4.1 microcontroller [middle]. An array of Hall-effect sensors detect magnetic fields [bottom].</small><small class="image-media media-photo-credit">James Provost</small></p><p>The magnetic imaging tile is a small-run SparkFun board <a href="https://github.com/sparkfun/SparkFun_Magnetic_Imaging_Tile" target="_blank">based on the work of Peter Jansen</a>. It features an 8-by-8 grid of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_effect" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Hall-effect</a> sensors, and uses an <a href="https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/serial-peripheral-interface-spi/all" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">SPI interface</a> to talk to the microcontroller of your choice. These sensors can detect both the magnitude and polarity of a magnetic field, and the board can report its measurements hundreds to thousands of times per second, depending on how much processing power your microcontroller has available. This is easily fast enough to capture the motion of a magnet being moved by hand across the board’s sensor area—my secret gesture.</p><p>An interesting thing about using magnetic fields is that if I was in extreme paranoia mode, I could shield the entire lock behind copper sheeting, something I couldn’t do with an RFID reader. The copper sheet would act as a <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_cage" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Faraday cage</a>, blocking any RFID signal, along with any other electromagnetic fields an attacker with an EMF meter might use to locate the lock.</p><h2>Choosing the Brains of the Box</h2><p>I chose a <a href="https://www.sparkfun.com/teensy" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Teensy 4.1</a> as my microcontroller. I like the Teensy because it offers loads of input/output, a 600-megahertz processor, a megabyte of RAM, and a built-in SD card, all in a slim package with an equally slim $32 price tag.</p><p>Wiring the Teensy to the magnetic-imaging tile was trivial, only complicated by the fact that one side of the board has a silk-screen error that misidentifies the SPI connections (SparkFun says this will be fixed in future runs of the board).</p><p class="pull-quote"><span>In extreme paranoia mode, I could shield the entire lock behind copper sheeting.</span></p><p>To create the gesture-recognition software, I once again turned to vibe coding. If you read my Hands On piece in the April 2026 issue of <em><em>IEEE Spectrum</em></em> on <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/reviving-teletext-for-ham-radio" target="_self">creating a teletext suite for ham radio</a>, you’ll know I’ve been grappling with how vibe coding should be used in DIY projects, if at all. So this time, instead of letting Claude write all the software, I confined it to writing just two support libraries, which you can find in our online repository at <a href="https://github.com/IEEESpectrumHandsOn" target="_blank">https://github.com/IEEESpectrumHandsOn</a>.</p><p>One library is in charge of recording a lock gesture and saving it to the Teensy’s nonvolatile storage. The other library monitors the tile and reports whether or not a newly detected gesture passes a selectable threshold to be considered a match.</p><p>I wrote the top-level code myself to keep it as DIY as possible. This code handles things like a button that puts the system in gesture-recording mode after a brief delay, the indicator LED for recording mode, and the code that operates the physical locking mechanism.</p><p class="shortcode-media shortcode-media-rebelmouse-image"> <img alt="A diagram showing negative and positive charge carriers being separated towards different edges in the presence of a magnetic field, and a grid of sensors readings beneath the stroke line of a gesture." class="rm-shortcode" id="c3a69" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/a-diagram-showing-negative-and-positive-charge-carriers-being-separated-towards-different-edges-in-the-presence-of-a-magnetic-fi.png?id=68493270&amp;width=980" /> <small class="image-media media-caption">Hall-effect sensors rely on a current passing through a p-type slab of silicon. By looking at the intensities of the voltages across an 8-by-8 grid of sensors, the microcontroller can reconstruct a gesture [right]. </small><small class="image-media media-photo-credit">James Provost</small></p><p>Of course, I needed something to lock to demonstrate my proof of concept. I bought a <a href="https://www.michaels.com/product/85-wood-trinket-box-by-make-market-10357776" target="_blank">wooden craft box</a> and removed the existing lid latch. I glued a notched beam inside the lid and built a mechanism that uses a servo motor to rotate another beam in and out of this notch. I will be the first to admit this mechanism goes beyond “simple” and into “simplistic,” but it’s sufficiently robust for my demo. The box has ample room for the magnetic imaging tile to be mounted against one wall, along with the Teensy and other supporting electronics. Marking the outline of the sensor area on the outside defeated the point of having a hidden lock, so instead I <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/watchy-the-hackable-50-smartwatch" target="_self">once again turned to mining my Irish heritage</a>. I created a <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_knot" target="_blank">Celtic knotwork pattern</a> that runs in a band across the front of the lockbox (I used an <a href="https://dmackinnon1.github.io/celtic/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">online knotwork generator</a> and then projected and traced the pattern onto the box). Using the pattern as a camouflaged reference grid I can make a repeatable gesture within the confines of the sensor area. (To make the gestures I’m using a magnetic stylus borrowed from <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/weave-your-own-apollo-era-memory" target="_self">a ferrite core-memory kit</a> we featured in 2022.)</p><p>There’s obvious room for improvement in any more serious installation. The biggest drawback currently is that if the box is locked when the battery runs out, you can’t open it again without breaking something. This could be solved by adding a hidden inductive charging coil. Nonetheless, the demo lockbox works well—now I just have to think what I should store in it!</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em>As Cybersecurity Awareness Month continues, the GitHub Bug Bounty team is excited to spotlight another top-performing security researcher from the GitHub Security Bug Bounty Program:</em> <code>@r31n</code><em>!</em></p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">As home to the world&rsquo;s developers, GitHub is dedicated to the security and reliability of the code behind millions of projects, and <a href="https://bounty.github.com/">our Bug Bounty Program</a> remains a cornerstone of that commitment. AI has lowered the barrier to entry and become a genuine force multiplier. We use it across our own internal security programs, and <a href="https://github.blog/security/raising-the-bar-quality-shared-responsibility-and-the-future-of-githubs-bug-bounty-program/">we welcome researchers who do the same</a>. It exposes me to different products, technologies, and architectures I probably wouldn&rsquo;t run into otherwise. GitHub is one of those programs I keep coming back to because there&rsquo;s just so much surface area. It&rsquo;s large, complex, and constantly changing. New features ship all the time, so it never really feels like a finished target. They also work closely with researchers, the bounty structure is competitive, and the swag is a nice bonus. It&rsquo;s simply a program I enjoy spending time on.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>What are your favorite classes of bugs to research and why?</strong></p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">I don&rsquo;t really have one favorite bug class. An open redirect is a simple example. By itself, the impact might be limited. But if you find an allowlisted-domain SSRF that follows redirects, suddenly that open redirect can become a useful part of a much bigger chain. That&rsquo;s probably the part of vulnerability research I enjoy most: finding those connections, looking at two or three separate bugs and realizing they can be combined in a way that wasn&rsquo;t intended. It takes some technical knowledge, but also quite a bit of creativity.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>How do you keep your skills sharp and stay on top of new vulnerability trends?</strong></p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">It&rsquo;s actually a mix of different things. Security blogs, vulnerability write-ups, conference talks, proof of concepts, research papers, courses. I try to learn from wherever useful work is being shared. But most of it only really sticks once I try it myself. I can read a vulnerability write-up, but working through it myself gives me a much deeper understanding. If I actually reproduce the technique, play around with it, break it, and figure out why it behaves the way it does, that&rsquo;s when it really stays with me. And later, when I see a similar pattern on a real target, it&rsquo;s much easier to recognize.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>What tools or workflows have been gamechangers for your research? Any lesser-known utilities you&rsquo;d recommend?</strong></p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">I tend to prefer manual investigation and small, purpose-built scripts over large automated toolchains. If I notice I&rsquo;m repeating the same task during research, I&rsquo;ll usually automate that specific part. AI has probably impacted my way of working more than any individual security tool.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Even then, I think mindset matters more than tooling. You can become extremely good with a particular tool and still miss interesting bugs because you&rsquo;re only looking at the target through the lens that tool gives you. So, I don&rsquo;t really have one tool I&rsquo;d recommend to everyone. The most useful tool is usually the one that helps answer the specific question I&rsquo;m investigating.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Walk us through your general approach. I tend to spend more time on complex functionality, integrations, trust boundaries, and anything that behaves a little differently from the rest. Experience and intuition matter too. A source is a point where attacker-controlled data enters the application.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Think of a request parameter, uploaded file, API input, or data coming from another service. You should understand exactly what&rsquo;s happening, which security boundary or control is being violated, and what the real impact is. Ideally, you should understand the issue well enough that you could write the report without AI at all. Build a clear PoC. Make sure the security team can follow it. Know why every step matters.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">AI has lowered the barrier to doing security research, which is great in a lot of ways. But you can also see the downside in the number of unverified reports teams now have to deal with. AI should make research better. Not just make more reports.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>As the industry ships more AI-powered features, does testing them require a different mindset than traditional web bugs?</strong></p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Yes. Conventional vulnerabilities still matter, but attackers may also be able to influence a probabilistic decision-making component through the data, instructions, or context it receives. But getting a model to say or do something unexpected isn&rsquo;t necessarily the interesting part. The more important question is what that influence allows an attacker to achieve. Can they access something they should not or cause the system to act with privileges the attacker does not have?
