The Newcastle United exit from the Carabao Cup leaves Matthias Jaissle facing a fair question: are we watching the uneven beginnings of something promising, or a team struggling to establish the foundations it needs?

For now, there is evidence for both views.

Everton should sharpen the scrutinybut it should not settle the verdict.

Jaissle was appointed on 5 August. He has had less than three months in charge, arriving shortly before the competitive season and succeeding a manager whose achievements gave supporters both wonderful memories and legitimate expectations. That is a demanding inheritance. It is also too short a period to judge a managerial appointment conclusively.

The league record offers some perspective. Newcastle have taken 11 points from eight matches, with three wins, two draws and three defeats. They have scored 12 goals and conceded 14. That is an uneven start, rather than evidence of either a breakthrough or a collapse.

There are genuine glimmers of hope. Winning 2–0 at Tottenham demonstrated that this side can deliver away from home. More recently, the victory by the same score at Crystal Palace provided an encouraging response to successive league defeats. Jaissle made five changes for Palace, and several of those introduced contributed directly to the result. That suggests a willingness to reconsider his selections and an ability to produce a response.

Availability also matters.

For the Hull game, Newcastle had nine players missing, while Joelinton, Anthony Elanga and Jacob Ramsey have been among those working their way back. A new coach trying to establish relationships and patterns deserves some allowance for that disruption. The allowance cannot be unlimited but it should be acknowledged.

Everton itself contained more NUFC attacking endeavour than the scoreline might suggest. Newcastle registered 28 shots against Everton’s 11 and more than twice as many touches in the opposition box. Those figures support the argument that the team continued to compete and create opportunities. They do not establish that Newcastle deserved to win: shot totals alone tell us too little about the quality of chances or control of a match.

The strongest concern is the recurrence of damaging defeats. Losing 4–1 at Leeds, 4–1 at Coventry and now 3–1 at Everton means conceding 11 goals across these three away matches. The clean sheets at Tottenham and Palace are encouraging but the contrast exposes how inconsistent Newcastle’s defensive performance has been.

At Coventry, Newcastle conceded an early penalty, failed to deal with a long throw for the second goal and then conceded twice after pulling themselves back into contention. At Everton, avoidable losses of possession and failures to defend decisive moments again proved costly. My concern is whether Newcastle United have sufficient collective security when something goes wrong.

Matthias Jaissle has emphasised individual mistakes and the need for greater maturity. That is understandable but repeated mistakes eventually become a coaching question too.

Are instructions clear? Are players receiving enough support? Does the approach suit those available? Players remain responsible for their decisions; the manager remains responsible for creating conditions in which good decisions become more likely.

The cup exit also represents a lost opportunity. Newcastle selected a side showing only one change from the Palace victory. This was a serious attempt to progress and it failed. Supporters are entitled to feel disappointed without being accused of impatience.

My assessment is that Matthias Jaissle deserves time, accompanied by clear expectations. Over the coming weeks, Newcastle United need fewer damaging errors, greater resilience after conceding and performances that can be sustained from one match to the next. Another impressive afternoon will encourage; a dependable run will persuade.

There is enough promise to support Matthias Jaissle. There is enough fragility to question him. The fairest position is to do both and judge whether the team is learning from setbacks rather than repeatedly explaining them.

10 of the Most Iconic Patrick Stewart Roles Beyond Captain Picard

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

We may earn a commission from links on this page.

Sir Patrick Stewart, an actor equally at home onstage at the Royal Shakespeare Company and on the bridge of the Starship Enterprise, has died at the age of 86. Unexpectedly for most of us, in part because he kept his final illness private, but more so because it once seemed as though nothing could fell the man who looms so large in our cultural consciousness.

The thoughtful, erudite performer who had a troubled childhood in working-class Yorkshire; the committed socialist who was eventually knighted by Queen Elizabeth; the advocate who put his money and his considerable voice toward the fights for LGBTQ+ acceptance and animal welfare and against domestic violence, his presence and performances meant the world (the galaxy, even) to myself and so many others. But those who knew him personally can offer better tributes; perhaps it's best left to Shakespeare's Player King, performed by Stewart himself opposite David Warner's Hamlet back in 1965:

Where joy most revels, grief doth most lament;Grief joys, joy grieves, on slender accident.

Much of Stewart's finest work was on the stage; other performances remain rather shockingly hard to come by (whither I, Claudius?). Nonetheless, he left a true legacy behind him, well beyond Captain Picard and Professor X. Here are 10 of his most iconic other roles.

Jeffrey (1995)

A goofy, feel-good gay rom-com with the HIV/AIDS crisis still very much in the foreground, Jeffrey was a near-impossibility in the mid-1990s: a movie that neither villainized its queers nor treated them as pitiable martyrs. Steven Weber and Michael T. Weiss are the leads, joined by a cast that includes Christine Baranski, Victor Garber, Nathan Lane, Olympia Dukakis, and Sigourney Weaver but, for all those names, Stewart is the undeniable standout as Sterling, the irrepressible gay best friend: a swishing, mincing, sassy, high-camp character who steals every scene without ever becoming a caricature. He's the leader of the "Pink Panthers," a squad of queers who defend locals from gay bashers, and that is the key to the character's power: He's as gay as they come, but not ashamed and not even a little weak. This movie, and the presence of Captain Picard himself in it, might have saved my life back in 1995. Stream Jeffrey on Prime Video, Tubi, and The Roku Channel.

Excalibur (1981)

A sprawling, epic, and appropriately lusty take on the King Arthur stories from John Boorman, Excalibur is a mixed bag full of stunning visuals and iconic moments that never quite coalesce into something entirely coherent. Standing out among the film's many characters, Stewart is entirely memorable as Leodegrance, the boisterous, boorish, but ultimately faithful father of Guenevere who is among the first to pledge his loyalty to Arthur. Rent Excalibur from Prime Video.

Green Room (2015)

Darcy Banker is erudite and intellectual, in his way, and even capable of charm, but otherwise has no redeeming qualities whatsoever. It's bad enough for the punk band Ain’t Rights that they've booked a last-minute gig at an Oregon bar without realizing that it's a haven for white supremacists. It's even worse when they witness a murder and become trapped in the title's green room, with Stewart as the lead skinhead. He's quite simply one of the all-time great (meaning absolutely terrifying) villains in this thriller that's pretty much a horror movie. Stream Green Room on Netflix.

Logan (2017)

Patrick Stewart's role as Professor Xavier in the X-Men is almost as pop-culture iconic as that of Captain Picard, spanning 26 years and nine movies, including the forthcoming Avengers: Doomsday. In most of those, he looks like he's having an awful lot of fun, but he gives his finest performance in the role in this elegaic installment, playing a dying Professor in profound cognitive decline, his dream in tatters and his bravado worn down to prickly dependence. We rarely get to see our superheroes at the ends of their days, and there's a lovely bravery to this depiction of the Charles' journey. Logan will likely be the toughest of his films to rewatch in the coming days. Stream Logan on Disney+.

A Christmas Carol (1999)

This stagey television movie isn't the most sumptuous of the many filmed versions of the Dickens story, but you won't be surprised to learn that there are few better portrayals of Ebenezer Scrooge. It's rather a shame that Patrick Stewart didn't do more Dickens adaptations, since his booming voice and ability to play thoroughly heightened characters without ever resorting to caricature is as valuable as in Shakespeare. Rent A Christmas Carol from Prime Video.

Hamlet (2009)

Famously referred to as that "unknown British Shakespearean actor" when starting out on Star Trek: The Next Generation, Stewart was an associate of the Royal Shakespeare Company for twenty years before his most famous role came along. As is the way of live theater, much of his career lives on as an essential link in the chain of Shakespeare performances in the twentieth century...but not in a way that we can conveniently stream. We do, however, have this impressively mounted modern-dress stage production, in which Stewart plays King Claudius with characteristic brilliance against David Tennant's melancholy prince. Buy Hamlet from Prime Video and Apple TV.

Match (2014)

As a cinematic actor, Stewart's greatest strength was bringing humanity to larger-than-life, even operatic roles, whether they be Arthurian knights, Dickensian villains, or superheroes. That ability is an absolute necessity if one is going to have any success doing Shakespeare, as Stewart did for decades. Match is something rather different, though, and it's rather lovely to see him play something just a bit more mundane—even if the film's Tobi Powell is a choreographer with a nearly legendary reputation. Teaching at Juilliard School, with his career winding down, he's confronted with the living embodiment of the past he left behind to pursue his career. The ensuing relationships grow more complex, with the film building to a climax that's surprising and deeply devastating, made all the more so by Stewart's muted, subtle performance. Stream Match on AMC+.

Robin Hood: Men in Tights (1993)

This is not the greatest Mel Brooks movie, not by a long shot, but it is a great comedic performance from Patrick Stewart. He loved to recount, with self-effacing embarrassment, the story of having shouted "We are not here to have fun" at his co-workers during a rowdy day on set at Star Trek: The Next Generation. By 1993, he was clearly past any such pomposity, and clearly having fun in his cameo as King Richard, a memorably silly take on a character he might well have played with seriousness elsewhere. Rent Robin Hood: Men in Tights from Prime Video.

Dune (1984)

Stewart's first real foray into science fiction was in this big, bold, divisive, and thoroughly bonkers Frank Herbert adaptation from director David Lynch. Stewart plays troubadour and warrior Gurney Halleck, and does what he does best: bringing gravitas and believability (and it must be said, bald sexiness) to even the wildest material. Stewart plays an aged Merlin in this rather shockingly good modern reimagining of the King Arthur legendarium. NASA ponders a Hubble servicing mission or a controlled deorbit

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

NASA has posted a contract opportunity notice, also known as a request for information, from commercial space orgs who can service the Hubble Space Telescope.

Hubble was always looked after by NASA astronauts flying on the Space Shuttle, yet the last Hubble Space Telescope (HST) servicing mission was the STS-125 Space Shuttle flight, launched on May 11, 2009. At the time, scientists hoped the mission would add another five years of operational life to the venerable spacecraft. Almost 17 years on, the HST (aka Hubble) is still delivering science.

However, time is running out for the observatory. NASA reckons there is a 10 percent chance it could reenter the Earth's atmosphere by September 2032, rising to 50 percent by December 2033. As the end of the Swift mission showed, increased solar activity can cause greater-than-anticipated atmospheric drag.

Also, like Swift, Hubble has no propulsion system, and so when it reenters (as it will, without intervention), the reentry will be uncontrolled. Hubble is sufficiently massive that there is a remote chance somebody on the ground could have a very bad day when that happens.

As such, one mission option suggested by NASA is a vehicle to grapple the observatory and perform a controlled reentry and disposal.

However, there are other options. Something to boost the telescope's orbit up to at least 600 km, or another to "enhance its ongoing performance and increase its resilience and expected on-orbit lifetime."

Both are intriguing options, and important since, as NASA acknowledges, no replacement for Hubble is expected before the 2040s, when the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) could become operational. Despite the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the Nancy Roman Space Telescope, Hubble remains in high demand, with NASA reporting a 7:1 oversubscription.

Before becoming NASA Administrator, Jared Isaacman advocated a SpaceX mission to boost the Hubble, but the idea was ultimately rejected. Similar plans could now be dusted off. NASA's requirement is for a mission to occur by 2032, and not harm the Hubble.

A soft-capture mechanism was fitted to Hubble during the STS-125 mission, which will mean docking with the spacecraft will be easier than the grappling planned for Swift. Then, the question is what to do with it. A controlled reentry is one option. The other options – boosting to a higher altitude or "enhance its ongoing performance and increase its resilience" are intriguing. Northrop Grumman's Mission Extension Vehicle (MEV), for example, is capable of on-orbit servicing and extending the lifetime of satellites. The Hubble is an order of magnitude more challenging, but the technological hurdles can be overcome with time and funding.

Last year, The Register spoke with Dr John Grunsfeld, one of the astronauts (now retired) who worked on Hubble during the STS-125 mission. Grunsfeld told us that the veteran spacecraft faced death by a thousand cuts as its budget was whittled away. The observatory itself recently switched from three to one gyro mode (the telescope uses gyros to control where it is pointed) to eke out a few more years of operational life.

At this point, NASA is looking for information and comment – this isn't a request for proposals. There is no indication of how much the agency expects to pay. However, the timescale is immutable. Without intervention, Hubble will make an uncontrolled reentry in the coming years. ®
