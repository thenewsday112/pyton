Sam Altman Thinks Your PC Is Wasting Precious RAM

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
"Looks like OpenAI CEO Sam Altman wants to own all the computing resources and rent out terminals to the world as a way of propping up his business plan, now that debt-ridden AI companies are teetering on the edge," writes ancient Slashdot reader Gilmoure. Tom's Hardware reports: In an interview over beers with the YouTube channel Cheeky Pint, Altman said that local inference, and, by extension, people having DRAM in computers at home, is "quite terrible." When discussing local inference on home computers with open-source models, Altman discussed the assumption that people will be able to run local AI on their own computers, the ones they may already own or want to build. Then he explained why that's a bad idea (at the 29-minute mark): "From a global allocation perspective, actually, it's quite terrible to have everybody have a bunch of the world's limited supply of DRAM sitting in their own computer and being used 5% of the time," Altman said. "You want maximum utilization in the cloud if you're trying to deal with this very supply-constrained environment. And somehow many people's intuition is the opposite of that." While OpenAI does have some open-source models, its primary business is in closed-source models that run in data centers, so he's really defending the company's business model. OpenAI isn't exactly threatened by some laptops and gaming rigs just yet, but local AI could mean people one day paying less for subscriptions to models like GPT-6 Luna if they have the power to run them at home. Altman's talk suggests that the company would prefer that more RAM go to OpenAI's data centers rather than home computers, which also sparks enthusiast fears of not being able to own your own hardware or software, but rather rent an agent and a computer in the cloud.

Scientists find microplastics falling with snow in the Sierra Nevada

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists measuring microplastic deposition across the Sierra Nevada during snowstorms found plastic particles at every single site they sampled. 

The sites included the most remote locations, accessible only by chairlift or on skis, far beyond any road. 

The findings matter well beyond the mountains. The Sierra Nevada snowpack supplies roughly 30% of California’s freshwater. 

What falls with the snow eventually makes its way into that supply.

Skiing to the sampling sites

Lead researcher Aleksandra Karapetrova, an environmental toxicologist at the University of California, Riverside, collected snow at seven locations across the Sierra Nevada during active storms. Getting there was difficult. 

“One site was accessed by chairlift. I had to ski to the rest of the remote sites. That’s how isolated they are,” she said. “But no matter how far out the site, I always found microplastics there.”

The expectation had been that at least some of the most isolated sites would show little or no contamination. That expectation did not survive contact with the data. 

Earlier research had already established that microplastics occur in Western US snow. What this study added was a storm-by-storm accounting. 

The scientists measured exactly how much plastic was being deposited during individual weather events. Such a method makes it possible to connect specific particle loads to specific atmospheric conditions.

How snow sweeps plastic from the sky

Microplastics enter the atmosphere through mundane routes.

Clothing sheds fibers during daily wear or through dryer vents, wind lifts small fragments from the ground, and plastic products break down into particles small enough to become airborne. 

Once suspended, they can travel considerable distances before anything brings them down.

Snow is particularly effective at collecting them. As snowflakes fall, they scavenge particles from the air around them. 

This process helps explain why the researchers measured substantially more plastic arriving with snowfall than during dry weather. 

The storm is not just bringing water. It is sweeping the atmosphere clean of whatever is suspended in it, plastic included.

What the analysis revealed

At remote sampling locations during the height of the storms, snow deposited between 270 and 3,200 plastic particles per square meter per day. 

At one site in the Mammoth Mountain ski area, where human activity is more concentrated and snowfall accumulation greater, the figure ran between 2,600 and 7,500 particles – roughly three times the remote-site range. 

“I didn’t think the plastics would stay suspended so much in the town,” Karapetrova said.

Even at that upper end, the actual mass of plastic is small. At the highest estimated deposition rate, snowfall over an acre would deliver about 1.5 grams of plastic per day. 

The particle counts are what make the numbers striking, not the weight.

The findings suggest that even the Sierra Nevada’s most isolated locations are not beyond the reach of airborne plastic pollution.

Where the plastic comes from

To trace the origins of the particles, the researchers followed the air masses that brought each sampled storm into the Sierra Nevada. 

Most had passed over Fresno and Madera counties in the Central Valley before arriving at the mountain sites.

That suggests the valley may be a significant source of the airborne plastic, though computer models could not pinpoint exact origins. 

The Central Valley is one of the most intensively farmed and heavily trafficked regions in the American West. Plastic is used in agriculture, packaging, and transport, generating abundant material for the wind to pick up and carry.

Identifying the particles required more than visual inspection under a microscope. 

The team used infrared spectroscopy to read each particle’s chemical signature. They then matched those signatures against a reference library to distinguish plastic from soil and plant material. 

The most common types found were polyethylene, polypropylene, and polyester, or PET. Cellulose derivatives, chemically modified plant-based materials used in consumer products, also turned up.

Beyond the mountains

The study didn’t assess what fraction of this plastic reaches drinking water, nor did it evaluate health effects directly. 

Although some plastic-associated chemicals have been linked to harmful biological effects, the study did not determine whether the particles found in Sierra Nevada snow pose risks to human health or aquatic ecosystems.

What the study makes clear is that atmospheric microplastic deposition is not confined to cities or coastlines or industrial zones.

It is happening continuously, across an entire mountain range, carried by weather systems that have no regard for how remote a location is.

“We are at a point where microplastics are in our atmosphere, not just the ocean, not just rivers. It comes down with the snowfall, making its way to places humans typically don’t go,” Karapetrova said. 

“We’ve reached a point where plastic pollution is falling out of the sky every day, across this entire region, even when it isn’t snowing.”

The research is published in the journal Environmental Science & Technology.
