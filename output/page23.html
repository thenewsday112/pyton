West Ham’s attacking prowess has been a standout of their campaign so far. WHUFC have scored the most goals in the league, with their tally of 32 being five more than league leaders Middlesbrough.

As was anticipated, Jarrod Bowen is leading the goalscoring for the Hammers, having scored six goals and laid on a further five assists in the Championship. Konstantinos Mavropanos has scored five goals too, proving to be a surprise threat in front of goal, mainly from set-piece situations.

However, the Irons could have scored more goals if they had more of a presence up front.

Joel Piroe has been a prolific marksman in the Championship over previous years, but has slightly struggled this season with three goals scored. Pablo Felipe draws praise for his work rate and hold-up play, but he’s yet to prove he can be a reliable goalscorer with one netted in eight league outings. However, the player whose form which has been the most surprising is Taty Castellanos, who was expected to star in the Championship.

The numbers behind Taty Castellanos’ struggles with West Ham

Castellanos has only scored one league goal this season – surprising, considering he netted six goals in 18 Premier League games last term, and has previously succeeded in Serie A (16 goals in 75 games) and La Liga (13 goals in 35 games).

The underlying numbers are where the concern arises though. The West Ham man has tallied up 3.06 xG, so his actual goal tally equates to an xG underperformance of 2.06. His 28 shots total puts him in the top 8% of Championship strikers, so he’s getting opportunities and failing to take them.

However, there is a current level of inconsistency with West Ham’s strikers, and that could prove to be an issue in the long run if they return to Premier League football. None are truly proven at the level, so they could look to reinvest at striker in the summer if promotion is secured.

In the short-term though, Nuno needs to find a solution by working with what he’s got.

Digital office communication affects stress, creativity, and perceived tone

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Office messages used to move on paper.

People used to type, copy, and file them by hand, but now they arrive in seconds via email, Slack, Microsoft Teams, text, and more. Trading these messages can feel like a conversation, but that feeling misleads people because the writer hears a tone of voice the reader often does not.

Nicholas Epley, a behavioral scientist at the University of Chicago, described the problem when email was a new, fast tool.

“But what’s different in this medium is…the ease with which we can fire things back and forth. It makes text-based communication seem more informal and more like face-to-face communication than it really is,” said Epley.

Researchers in psychology, management, and communication at universities in the United States have since tested the changes new tools bring to work.

Most of their studies are small. Together, they cover how well tone comes through on a screen, how stressed workers feel, and how teams come up with ideas when they aren’t in the same room.

Memos were built for filing

JoAnne Yates, a management professor emerita at MIT Sloan School of Management, studied how American companies built their first internal communication systems.

Her book Control Through Communication follows typewriters, carbon copies, and filing systems as they changed business writing in the late 1800s and early 1900s.

Cabinets that sorted papers by subject worked best for documents covering one subject each. The internal memo grew up alongside them, a one-topic document a company could copy, file, and find again.

Speed came much later. Yates worked with Wanda Orlikowski of MIT and Melissa Mazmanian of the University of California, Irvine, on a study of professionals who carried mobile email devices. At first, the devices gave those workers flexibility and peace of mind.

Mobile email also raised everyone’s expectations for how quickly someone should respond. The team wrote that professionals chose to work “anywhere, anytime,” but in practice they ended up working “everywhere, all the time.”

Tone gets lost in text

Epley and psychologist Justin Kruger tested how well tone travels in an experiment with 30 pairs of college students. Each student received a list of 20 short statements and gave each one a sarcastic or a serious tone.

One student in each pair read the statements into a tape recorder, and the other emailed them. Both groups expected their partners to read the tone right about 78% of the time.

Listeners got it right about 75% of the time. Email readers managed 56%, not much better than a coin flip.

The voice messages came close to what the senders hoped, and the emails fell well short. Still, readers and listeners alike guessed they judged the tone correctly 90% of the time.

Kruger and Epley blamed egocentrism, the difficulty people have seeing things from someone else’s point of view. A writer hears the intended tone, but the reader sees only words.

“E-mail is fine if you just want to communicate content, but not any emotional material,” said Epley.

Short replies now carry signals of their own.  In a Binghamton University study led by psychologist Celia Klin, 126 undergraduates rated one-word replies such as “Okay” and “Sure.” A period made those replies seem less sincere in a text message, but not in a handwritten note.

Email turned into a stress signal

Stephen Barley and Debra Meyerson of Stanford University and Stine Grodal of Boston University studied workers at high-tech companies.

Engineers, technical writers and marketers logged every phone call, email and face-to-face conversation over a series of workdays. The team reported in Organization Science that people who spent more time handling email felt more overloaded.

In later interviews, Grodal noted that participants spent about 90% of their discussion on email. Questions about phone calls and meetings drew little emotional response.

“You can actually see it on the screen; it has material properties. And so email becomes your to-do list of the day,” said Grodal.

Gloria Mark, an informatics professor at the University of California, Irvine, tested the opposite. Her team cut off email for 13 civilian employees at an Army research center outside Boston for five workdays. Heart rate monitors and computer software tracked the workers the whole time.

With email, the workers switched computer windows about 37 times an hour. Without it, they switched about 18 times, and Mark’s team used that switching to measure multitasking. Their heart rates also varied more during the email break, which the researchers read as a sign of lower stress.

Video narrows people’s focus

As more people started working from home, many meetings moved onto video. Melanie Brucks of Columbia Business School and Jonathan Levav of Stanford University tested what that does to brainstorming.

The researchers split 602 people into pairs and gave each pair five minutes to list creative uses for a Frisbee or bubble wrap. Some pairs sat together in a rooms, and others worked over video. The video pairs came up with fewer ideas, and fewer creative ones.

Eye tracking pointed to a reason. People on video spent more time looking at their partner and less time looking around the room. They also remembered fewer of the props the researchers had set out, such as a large houseplant.

The same pattern appeared among 1,490 engineers at company sites in five countries, although video didn’t hurt the next step: picking the best idea.

“When you’re visually narrowing yourself… you’re putting blinders on, basically,” said Brucks.

Video can also wear people out. In a Stanford survey of more than 10,000 people, about 13.8% of women and 5.5% of men said video calls left them very to extremely tired. The team found that the subjects seeing their own faces on screen for hours helped explain that gap.

Off-hours email raises anxiety

The pressure doesn’t end when the workday ends. William Becker of Virginia Tech, Liuba Belkin of Lehigh University and Samantha Conroy of Colorado State University surveyed full-time employees and their partners about checking email outside work hours.

Workers felt more anxious when employers expected them to monitor email at night, even if the workers spent little time actually working, and their partners reported worse health and less relationship satisfaction. After-hours email is a source of job stress that permeates the whole household.

“If the nature of a job requires email availability, such expectations should be stated formally as a part of job responsibilities,” said Becker.

Most of this research predates Slack and Teams becoming everyday tools. The tone experiment used email between college students, and the punctuation study used text messages, so these studies didn’t test workplace chat apps directly.

Grodal has said her team needs to return to workplaces and test changes such as letting workers skip after-hours replies. For now, those changes remain untested in the field.

The full study was published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.

How to get involved

Read the Center for Plain Language’s free guides if you write messages to coworkers.  After the General Services Administration redirected the government’s plain language website in 2025, the nonprofit reposted the Federal Plain Language Guidelines

Explore the NIOSH Total Worker Health program if you help shape workplace policy. The federal program works to make jobs safe and to support workers’ health and well-being.

Follow the Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology for research behind workplace practices. Its members study how people work, and the society shares that research with policymakers.

Try the National Day of Unplugging each March. This day calls for a 24-hour break from phones and computers that runs from sundown to sundown.

‘Very worrying’: Gary Rowett reacts to Derby County’s season so far

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Derby County’s recent poor run of form has been ‘very worrying’, says their former manager Gary Rowett.

Derby County have won only one league game so far this season. DCFC are in the Championship drop zone along with Preston North End and Burnley after their first 12 fixtures.

The Rams are sat in 23rd position in the table and they are four points from safety behind Watford. John Eustace’s side drew 1-1 away at Sheffield United last time out with Bobby Clark getting their goal at Bramall Lane.

Photo by Lewis Clarke, via Wikimedia Commons, licensed under CC BY-SA 2.0.

Their ex-boss Rowett has delivered his verdict on the situation at Pride Park on talkSPORT: “They’ve struggled since the takeover collapsed. It felt like they were preparing everything for the takeover then everything got put on hold. I do believe that does have an impact. All of pre-season, the start of the season (you’re preparing for the takeover), then all of a sudden you know where you are but you’ve got to rebuild essentially around it. I think they’ll come good Derby but certainly the form has been very worrying at the moment.”

Derby County are back in action this weekend as they look to turn their fortunes around. They face an away trip to Millwall.

Derby County endure tough start to the new Championship season

Derby County have been plagued with injuries in this campaign to date. DCFC have a whole host of defenders sidelined including Matt Clarke, Sondre Langas and Eiran Cashin.

As a result, they have been leaky at the back and haven’t been as solid as they are used to being under Eustace. The Rams have recently delved into the free agent market to snap up experienced pair Jack Robinson and Shane Duffy in an attempt to tighten things up at the back.

Further up the pitch, they haven’t been ruthless enough in front of goal. In fact, they are the joint-lowest scorers in the league with Watford and Preston North End having found the net on only 10 occasions.

Derby County will get the chance to bolster their ranks when the January transfer window opens up for business with some new faces. They will also have the opportunity to let some players head out the exit door this winter to help free up space and funds to pave the way for reinforcements.
