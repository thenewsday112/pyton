The 12 strangest science stories we can’t stop thinking about

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
A male Amazon river dolphin (Inia geoffrensis) urinating into the air

There are some stories that stay with you forever, the big scientific moments that change the course of history. These are not those moments – but we can’t stop thinking about them anyway.

Push my button

Surgeon Stuart Meloy was performing a pain-relief operation on a woman’s spine when she started exclaiming emphatically. “You’re going to have to teach my husband to do that,” the woman said. This inspired Meloy to invent an implant to help women who are unable to achieve orgasms in the usual way. New Scientist broke the news in 2001, sparking a frenzy of worldwide media excitement. But 13 years later, Meloy was still struggling to raise funds for a full clinical trial, and the “orgasmatron” never reached a climax.

Monkey pleasure

A long-tailed macaque Shutterstock/fiz_zero

We aren’t the only primates capable of coming up with creative ways to pleasure ourselves. In a study of long-tailed macaques (Macaca fascicularis) in Bali, Indonesia, Camilla Cenni at the University of Lethbridge in Alberta, Canada, and her colleagues filmed hundreds of examples of male and female monkeys using stone tools as sex toys, rubbing or tapping them on their genitals. According to Cyril Grueter at the University of Western Australia, the Balinese monkeys get plenty of food handouts from humans, and this frees up time for sexual experimentation. But some monkeys in other places have unusual kinks, too: another study reported a wild macaque in Japan rubbing its genitals on the back of a sika deer.

Head transplants

In 2015, surgeon Sergio Canavero announced plans to carry out the first human head transplant, intended to extend the lives of people with muscle and nerve degeneration or widespread cancer. He claimed his technique could overcome technical hurdles, including how to fuse the spinal cord and prevent the body’s immune system from rejecting the head. In the following two years, Canavero published animal experiments supposedly backing up his methods, but other surgeons remained highly sceptical. A human head transplant never did happen, but Canavero has reportedly moved on to an even bolder ambition: transplanting a brain into a non-sentient clone.

Rats driving

Have you ever wondered if a rat can drive a car? Kelly Lambert at the University of Richmond in Virginia has. She and her colleagues built a tiny car with three copper bars that would steer it left, forward or right. Sure enough, 18 rats learned to drive towards food rewards. They even found it relaxing, as measurements of their stress hormones indicated. Lambert proposed that driving challenges could replace traditional maze tests when using rat models to study neuropsychiatric conditions.

The sniff test

Humans are more similar to rats than we like to think. In a psychology experiment, Noam Sobel and his colleagues at the Weizmann Institute of Science in Rehovot, Israel, surreptitiously filmed 153 volunteers. After shaking hands with someone of the same sex, people smelled their shaking hand more than twice as much as they did before the handshake. Puzzlingly, after shaking hands with someone of the opposite sex, people were more likely to sniff their non-shaking hand. The team suspected that humans use chemical signals all the time without being aware of it.

Urine fountains

Communication through scent is, of course, widespread in mammals, but some never-before-seen behaviour in dolphins took biologists by surprise in 2025. While studying Amazon river dolphins (Inia geoffrensis), or botos, in Brazil, Claryana Araújo-Wang at CetAsia Research Group in Ontario, Canada, saw a male “flip his belly up out of the water, expose his penis and then proceed to urinate into the air”, she told New Scientist. They saw dolphins do this a total of 36 times, and most of the time another male was nearby and would pursue the stream of urine. The purpose of this behaviour hasn’t yet been established, but hormones in urine might signal a male’s social position or physical condition, said Araújo-Wang.

Giving the finger

As for human modes of communication, many of us struggle to put our smartphones down – so the last thing we’d want is for them to crawl across the table towards us. Nevertheless, Marc Teyssier at the University of Paris-Saclay in France and his colleagues gave phones this ability in 2018, with a robotic appendage dubbed the MobiLimb. It plugs into a device’s USB port and, for some reason, looks eerily like a human finger, giving it a spooky quality reminiscent of Thing from The Addams Family. The MobiLimb can stroke your wrist, wave, wag like a tail or, most disconcertingly, drag the phone around. Thankfully, the idea hasn’t taken off.

Horror frog

A taxidermied male hairy frog Gustavocarra/CC BY-SA 4.0

If you find the thought of a phone with a finger horrifying, wait until you hear about the nightmare-inducing hairy frog Trichobatrachus robustus. In 2008, researchers reported that these frogs can break their own bones to produce claws that burst through their skin. David Blackburn at Harvard University’s Museum of Comparative Zoology thought these weapons were for defence, but two years later, another team argued that they were used in fights between males – and that the frog’s unusual hairs were a kind of armour to protect its vulnerable parts from sharp claws.

Corpse shrinking

In 1957, when New Scientist was 1 year old, we reported on a new method for shrinking an adult human body to the size of a baby. At an exhibition of inventors in Winnipeg, Canada, the unnamed inventor of this process claimed it would make disposing of dead bodies simpler, as they could be slid into underground tubes and take up less space. The method involved applying high pressure to the corpse and squeezing out fluids so its flesh could be compressed into a mould. “All this is, of course, highly ingenious but it is uncertain why the inventor thinks that his newly proposed form of burial is in any way better than the current and very popular method of cremation,” the writer mused.

The brain has its own microbiome. Here’s what it means for your health

Transient anus

The warty comb jelly has an anus that comes and goes Andrey Nekrasov/imageBROKER.com​/Alamy

We may take it for granted, but the anus, which allows food to pass through the body, was an evolutionary breakthrough, both figuratively and literally. The earliest animals, like jellyfish today, had only one gut opening, where food went in and waste came out. In 2019, scientists discovered that the warty comb jelly (Mnemiopsis leidyi, pictured top) forms an anal opening when it needs to defecate, but it vanishes straight afterwards. This, they suggested, could represent how the anus evolved in the first place.

Fart science

Flatus – gas expelled from the anus – has been a topic of endless fascination for many readers (and journalists). In 2001, we featured the work of Michael Levitt at the Veterans Administration Medical Center in Minneapolis, a leading authority on the subject. He developed a “Toot Trapper” that reduces odours by trapping certain gases using activated charcoal. We also reported on a device for monitoring gas emissions from dogs, used to test dog snacks that were supposed to reduce flatulence. In 2016, researchers in Australia worked out which foods cause the smelliest farts by mixing faeces with different foods. They recommended eating less protein and more slow-release carbohydrates to minimise hydrogen sulphide production. In 2022, Nagarajan Kannan at the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, and his colleagues worked out that some poos float because of gas from gut microbes, confirming a hypothesis first put forward by Levitt 50 years earlier.

Brew your own

Even stranger things go on inside some people’s intestines, as we found out in 2019. A 46-year-old man started experiencing unexplained mental fogginess, dizziness and memory loss, and had to give up his job. Later, doctors found high levels of alcohol in his blood when he hadn’t been drinking. Eventually, they realised his gut contained high levels of Saccharomyces cerevisiae – brewer’s yeast – which got him drunk any time he ate a lot of carbohydrates. This rare condition, known as auto brewery syndrome, had been triggered when the man took antibiotics for a thumb injury, which disrupted his gut microbiome. This year, a study reported that another man with this condition fully recovered after getting a transplant of faeces from a healthy donor. Both articles were written by Alice Klein, who we also have to thank for the monkey sex toys, car-driving rats, smelly farts and floating poos. Thanks, Alice!

Dowman ban upheld, football’s rules need to change

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Morning.

Let’s start again with Max Dowman whose three match ban was upheld after Arsenal’s appeal against the red card failed. The club had made the bid on the basis of wrongful dismissal and excessive punishment, but the claim was dismissed by an independent regulatory commission who will publish their ‘written reasons’ in due course. Thanks for that.

You will already know I think this is too severe. I thought the sending off was harsh, especially with the video evidence that backed the appeal, and for a player to miss three games for what was such a minor reaction to a heavy challenge is really unfair. And I would say that if this were an Arsenal player or not. It’s another example of how we hear about the concept of player protection all the time, but the reality is there have been countless incidents which could have been used to demonstrate there’s a seriousness about that but it never, ever happens.

How absurd is it that Max Dowman will serve the same ban as Ryan Shawcross got for snapping Aaron Ramsey’s leg in two? Remember when Dan Smith launched himself at Abou Diaby’s ankle, putting him out of the game for 9 months and causing an injury so severe it plagued the rest of the Frenchman’s career? He didn’t even get a red card that day, so the next time you see Dermot Gallagher pontificating on Sky about how a referee got an obviously wrong decision right, apply that context (he was the ref at the Stadium of Light that day).

Football has a problem with actual violent conduct, but chooses to deal with it by trying to make us think performative infractions like Dowman’s are worthy of the same punishment as the acts of real violence. The dangerous tackles, the two-footed leaps (which seem to creeping back into the game a bit, probably because they’re not being punished), pushing an opposition player when they’re in the air, these things are not on the same scale. If what Dowman did is worthy of a three game ban, the next time someone goes studs first over the ball into the shins of their opponent, they should get ten games at least.

But we know that’s not going to happen. Almost as long as this blog has been going (coming up on 25 years now!), I’ve made the case for there to be a sliding scale of punishment for acts that meet the definition of violent conduct. Some things are just more violent than others, but as long as this blanket punishment of three games regardless of the seriousness of the incident is in place, the more those in charge do the game and the players a disservice.

Still, it is what it is now, and Arsenal will have to live without Dowman for the games against Liverpool, Hull City, and the away trip to Newcastle on the far side of the upcoming November Interlull. He could well have been involved at some point this weekend against Liverpool, but now we’re sweating on the fitness of Noni Madueke, with Eberechi Eze the only other option for the left hand side. There are some whispers that Christos Tzolis could be back in contention for Sunday, which would be a very welcome boost, but the forward line looks a bit light for a game of this significance.

Tuesday’s game against Fleetwood will live in the memory because of the giant killing aspect, but for two young Academy players, it’s a night that will hopefully be formative. For Dowman, whether you think what he did was worthy of a red card or not, there’s a lesson to learn about how you react. To be quite honest, I think there’s something to like about the way he was prepared to stick up for himself against a grown man who wanted to ‘leave a bit on him’, as they say. The talented players are the ones who get kicked most though, and it’s something he’s going to have to contend with as his career develops. Just ask Bukayo Saka. The two have plenty of time on their hands right now, and I’m sure it will be part of his footballing education.

The other player is Marli Salmon who probably should have done better on their goal, but for me is less culpable overall than some of the senior players who didn’t perform at the right level. Arsene Wenger once said:

You pay for the education of young players with points. If I play a 20-year old centre back, I know he’ll cost me points during the season and I have to stand up for that. A less talented 28 year old would cost me less points. However, by 23/24 you have a player.

Marli Salmon is 17. He didn’t cost us any points. There’s a long way still go in his development, but I’m sure with the coaching staff they’ll go over what happened against Fleetwood and he will learn from it. It is more difficult for young defenders than young attackers too. A mistake at the top end of the pitch, like Dowman scuffing a shot wide from a position he probably ought to score from, doesn’t feel quite as costly as a forward running off a young defender to score the opening goal of the game. But again, it’s all part of the process, every young player makes mistakes, it’s how you then use them to improve that counts, and I’m sure he’ll do that.

Haunting the Cepheus Flare

Explanation: Spooky shapes seem to haunt this dusty expanse, drifting through the night in the royal constellation Cepheus. Of course, the shapes are cosmic dust clouds visible in dimly reflected starlight. Far from your own neighborhood, they lurk above the plane of the Milky Way at the edge of the Cepheus Flare molecular cloud complex some 1,200 light-years away. Over 2 light-years across and brighter than most of the other ghostly apparitions, vdB 141 or Sh2-136 is also known as the Ghost Nebula. It lies at lower right in the starry field of view. Inside the Ghost Nebula are reddish telltale signs of dense cores collapsing in the early stages of star formation. With the eerie hue of interstellar dust reflecting bluish light from hot young stars of NGC 7023, the Iris Nebula stands out against the dark just left of center. In the broad telescopic frame, these fertile interstellar dust fields stretch almost six full moons across the sky.

Shrouded in ice in the lonely Weddell Sea, Vega Island is an eerie sight to behold. On this speck of land east of the Antarctic Peninsula, ice, rock outcrops, and glacial moraines combine to create the appearance of a smirking skull in profile looking toward Devil Island.

These images show Vega Island’s creepy countenance at the start of austral autumn (left) and in spring (right). They were acquired with the OLI (Operational Land Imager) on Landsat 9 on March 22, 2025, and October 10, 2026, respectively. (Clouds obscured the island in March 2026.) A nunatak jutting out of the ice has the appearance of an eye socket, a lateral moraine forms a mouth, and Bahía del Diablo (Devil’s Bay) Glacier constitutes a jawbone. In the March 2025 image, a layer of snow coats the ice, which is typical of the region’s wettest month. That contrasts with the October 2026 image, when snow is absent from large areas and darker ice surfaces are visible.

Above-average temperatures in winter 2026—particularly in June—were partly responsible for so much bare ice in October, said glaciologist Mauri Pelto of Nichols College. The ice exposed on Vega and nearby islands indicates limited new snow accumulation over the winter, further reduced by sublimation and wind scouring, he said. “The winter season was no treat for these glaciers.”

The melt season ahead may also be more trick than treat. Bare ice is darker than snow and absorbs more solar energy, meaning that melting is poised to speed up as the days grow longer and warmer. “Even a thin cover of new snow from a spring storm would be helpful in the short run,” Pelto said. “When entombed in snow and ice, the skull is preserved. But when exposed to melt, it weathers away, which will lead to a changing jawline and expanding eye socket.”

October 10, 2026 NASA Earth Observatory/Lauren Dauphin

The first comprehensive mapping of Vega Island’s ice was aided by Landsat imagery. In a study published in 1982, scientists described the island’s four ice caps with protruding rocky cliffs and outlet glaciers. They leveraged early Landsat observations along with aerial photos and topographic maps to catalog 29 glaciers on Vega.

Of those glaciers, Devil’s Bay Glacier has been closely monitored since the austral summer of 1998-1999, with researchers measuring an overall decline in its mass. Satellite images acquired in recent decades show ice loss from other glaciers on the island as well, with increased areas of exposed bedrock.

Long before scientists began tracking its glaciers, Vega was a bright spot in a tale of terror born out of early exploration of the area. The Swedish Antarctic Expedition of 1901-1903 set out to study the geography, geology, climate, and wildlife on and around the eastern Antarctic Peninsula. The party became split into three groups during their explorations, isolated for a winter in different locations. Adding to the horror, they lost their ship to the pack ice. Cape Well-met, as it would come to be known, off the “forehead” of the Vega Island skull, was the site of a happy reunion of two of the groups in spring 1903. Soon thereafter, an Argentinean expedition rescued the survivors of the ordeal.
