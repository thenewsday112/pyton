IEEE Xplore Digital Library Adds AI Assistant

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


IEEE recently released the IEEE Xplore AI Research Suite, built on the full text of millions of peer-reviewed publications in the IEEE Xplore Digital Library. The library offers full-text access to IEEE standards, journals, magazines, conference proceedings, and more.

Researchers who use the AI suite to search the digital library receive answers grounded in verified scientific results, ensuring a high level of accuracy and trust.

The suite is available as an optional subscription upgrade to all IEEE Xplore Digital Library institutional subscribers.

Powered by a proprietary AI system optimized for STEM literature, the suite introduces powerful, intelligent features within the digital library. The suite enables users to seamlessly search and discover articles to accelerate their comprehension of complex technical papers.

The system surfaces deep insights, replacing the typical keyword queries with natural, conversational research workflows.

As AI tools become more common in conducting research, IEEE collaborated to build the suite around a simple principle: Answers should be trustworthy, not just fast, says W. Clem Karl, vice president, IEEE Publication Services and Products.

“AI can be a highly effective and beneficial tool that can help researchers accelerate discovery, enhance decision-making, and surface new connections,” Karl says. “However, many AI engines present a great risk of hallucinating answers or introducing bias into otherwise credible research. With this in mind, IEEE has taken a responsible approach in our AI strategy by developing concrete safeguards to ensure our solution is rigorously grounded in scientific evidence that users can trust.

“As stewards of the scholarly record, we held the new AI engine to the same standards as the peer-reviewed research we publish: A researcher shouldn’t have to trust an answer; they should be able to verify the work behind it.”

Collaboration with the scientific community

The IEEE Xplore AI Research Suite was developed in close collaboration with global scientific and library communities.

The development process began with research into user needs and the challenges associated with existing AI research assistants. More than 3,000 testers—including engineers, scientists, and library information professionals—provided feedback to refine the AI engine and support research workflows.

Capabilities include:

  • Semantic AI search. Users can input free-form, natural-language queries or paste long blocks of text to locate similar research and identify novel solutions.
  • Time-saving summaries. The platform generates immediate, accurate AI overviews of search results and provides in-context definitions of technical terms so that users can grasp new concepts faster and accelerate their research.
  • Built on trusted content. The suite is the only AI research assistant that uses both the full text and metadata of IEEE’s peer-reviewed journal articles and conference papers to optimize the accuracy and relevance of the results.
  • Comprehensive coverage. A variety of STEM metadata is available from journal articles and conference papers that have been curated to include scholarly articles without the typical noise found in other tools.
  • Source links and verifiable results. The AI-generated overviews include reference links to source documents, allowing users to verify information and cross-reference findings.

Reducing hallucinations

To mitigate the risk of AI hallucinations, the search-retrieval process is limited to querying the millions of IEEE peer-reviewed full-text documents, alongside an expertly curated dataset of STEM metadata. If the AI tool is unable to find relevant scientific content within the defined dataset, it is designed to inform the user rather than generate fabricated answers.

“IEEE is committed to providing the technical community with a highly precise and reliable research experience,” says Steven Heffner, IEEE’s chief publication officer and managing director of IEEE Publications. “The IEEE Xplore AI Research Suite offers something unique in that it is the only AI research assistant that leverages both the full-text and metadata of IEEE’s peer-reviewed content, rather than abstracts alone.

“The difference is significant: Our internal testing showed a threefold increase in result quality and accuracy across every key metric. These results underscore how critical full-text grounding is for technical research.”

For more information about features and coverage, or to request a demo for your organization, visit the IEEE Xplore AI Research Suite website.

Turn subsurface uncertainty into decision-ready insight

Drilling campaigns are among the most expensive and operationally complex activities in the energy industry. Rigs, crews, equipment, and logistics are scheduled months in advance, leaving little room for delay. Every hour of downtime can carry significant costs. Geoscientists and engineers rely on reservoir models to understand the subsurface and guide drilling decisions, often under intense time pressure. Yet the current modeling infrastructure that many companies rely on for these critical decisions was designed for smaller datasets, simpler workflows, and siloed operations. As subsurface data grows in scale and complexity, teams need a faster, more connected way to transform uncertainty into actionable decisions.

Now generally available, agile reservoir modeling (ARM) from SLB, integrated with Azure Data Manager for Energy (ADME), is co-engineered for cloud scale to close that gap. The combined solution connects ensemble-centric reservoir modeling and simulation with a governed data foundation, elastic Azure high-performance computing, and scalable storage designed for the throughput and concurrency of large ensemble studies.

Learn more about Azure Data Manager for Energy

Scale ensemble modeling with ARM on Azure

A single reservoir model represents one plausible interpretation of the subsurface. Ensemble modeling evaluates hundreds of model realizations together, giving asset teams a broader view of potential outcomes and a stronger basis for understanding uncertainty and risk.

The industry has long recognized the value of ensemble modeling for risk-aware decision-making, but adoption has been constrained by infrastructure and software designed for smaller, sequential studies. Moving from a limited model set to hundreds of realizations multiplies the compute, storage, data movement, and results that must be managed. ARM addresses this barrier by distributing realization generation and simulation across elastic Azure high-performance computing, while scalable storage provides the capacity, throughput, and parallel access required by ensembles of different sizes.

Connect reservoir workflows to a governed data foundation

Building and updating reservoir models draws on many systems: seismic, wellbore, interpretation, and simulation data, plus the applications and compute that process them. When these pieces are fragmented, customers must connect and orchestrate them on their own, and large-scale simulation becomes a disconnected, one-off exercise.

SLB and Microsoft combined their respective technologies, ARM engineered by SLB and ADME engineered by Microsoft, to remove that burden. Rather than a set of loosely connected tools, customers get one integrated workflow in which each component has a clear role:

  • Azure Data Manager for Energy is the governed data foundation. OSDU® Technical Standard-aligned services and OSDU Domain Data Management Services (DDMS) manage seismic, wellbore, interpretation, and simulation data.
  • Petrel™ subsurface software is where engineers define interpretation and uncertainty workflows, connected directly to data in ADME.
  • Agile reservoir modeling is the ensemble workflow engine. It orchestrates the generation of reservoir realizations and simulation runs and supports discovery and analysis at enterprise scale.

As a result, teams build ensembles from trusted corporate data and publish results back for discovery and reuse, which reduces data movement and improves collaboration across asset teams.

Put ensemble modeling into practice with a connected workflow

Beyond integration and scale, ARM distinguishes itself by orchestrating all stages of an ensemble study across modeling, simulation, data management, and analysis. The workflow tightly connects the tools reservoir teams already use with Azure-scale infrastructure and the governed data services provided by Azure Data Manager for Energy.

Agile Reservoir Modeling workflow using cloud HPC to generate and simulate multiple reservoir-model realizations in parallel delivered to web-based interface for analysis.

A walkthrough of how this works today:

Reservoir engineers define uncertainty workflows in Petrel software and prepare simulation models for supported SLB simulators, including the Intersect™ reservoir simulator and Eclipse™ reservoir simulator. ARM generates the realizations, prepares the simulation cases, and coordinates their execution across the Azure environment.

Azure high-performance computing executes multiple realizations in parallel. A shared, high-performance Azure Files volume holds model inputs, grids, simulation decks, temporary files, and results so the simulation fleet can read and write concurrently. Because compute capacity, storage capacity, throughput, and IOPS can be scaled independently, the environment can be sized for the concurrency of the ensemble rather than the constraints of fixed infrastructure.

As simulations complete, SLB reservoir engineering services manage the resulting ensembles and use OSDU search capabilities on ADME to support discovery, filtering, and sorting across large studies. Teams can locate relevant realizations and supported, published results without manually navigating disconnected project stores.

Engineers can analyze ensembles in the reservoir engineering workspace or load them back into Petrel software for further interpretation, history matching, and reservoir engineering studies. This allows teams to work in the environment and application best suited to each task while maintaining continuity, without having to step outside their workflow to manage orchestration.

ADME provides entitlement-based access control integrated with Microsoft Entra ID and supports compliance controls such as OSDU legal tags. Connecting ARM workflows to this governed data foundation helps maintain consistent access and compliance as corporate subsurface data is used across modeling and simulation. Because ARM workflows are integrated through Petrel software in ADME, reservoir engineers can perform ensemble-scale studies leveraging their corporate-wide data stores and publish results for discovery, eliminating friction and driving collaboration. Microsoft and SLB engineering collaborated closely to build the integration between Petrel software and ARM for enterprise-scale performance.

For reservoir engineers and asset teams, ARM turns ensemble modeling into a repeatable workflow rather than a bespoke infrastructure project. Teams can stress-test development concepts against calibrated ensembles that honor observed production history, not simply run a larger number of models. As new wells are drilled and new data becomes available, they can update their reservoir understanding without waiting for a major model rebuild. For energy IT and data leaders, the workflow connects simulation to an OSDU standard-aligned architecture with consistent identity, governance, search, and interoperability, reducing isolated data copies and disconnected applications.

The future of the reservoir lifecycle

Building on this partnership, Microsoft and SLB are working within the OSDU community to advance a more cloud-native standard for reservoir data. This work will help models, simulation results, and engineering workflows operate more consistently across applications, improving interoperability while reducing dependence on proprietary formats and silos. A shared, governed data foundation also paves the way for AI and agentic experiences that can discover data, coordinate workflows, and surface insights across the reservoir lifecycle. Combined with Azure-scale compute and storage, this creates a path toward more automated and continuous reservoir modeling.

AWS Weekly Roundup: Amazon Polly Generative engine, TorchNeuron for Trainium, and more (October 26, 2026)

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A few weeks ago I presented to a small group of commercial and technology leaders from Brazilian agribusiness at the AWS office in São Paulo. My talk ran through where generative AI fits across the different stages of an agribusiness operation, and the point I kept returning to is that the markets we call traditional are exactly the ones where keeping up with this technology decides who ends up ahead of the curve. Nobody there was arguing about whether AI belongs in agriculture. The questions were about delivery: who receives the output, in which language, on which device, often while driving between farms. That is why a launch I might otherwise have filed under routine leads my list this week. The Amazon Polly Generative engine upgrade added 14 new voices, two of them in Brazilian Portuguese. When your user has both hands on a steering wheel, the interface is a voice.

Presenting “GenAI no Agronegócio Brasileiro” at the AWS office in São Paulo.

The rest of the week had a sharper edge. The biggest launch is a security service built on the premise that the gap between a vulnerability being disclosed and a vulnerability being exploited has effectively closed, and AWS published the honeypot evidence for exactly that in the same week. Now, let’s get into this week’s AWS news…

AWS Continuum for code vulnerabilities is now generally available
AWS Continuum reframes vulnerability management as an end-to-end job rather than a scanner that hands you a list. It builds a context graph from your repositories and your AWS environment, assigns each finding an evidence level, and reproduces exploitable findings in an isolated sandbox with proof-of-concept code so you see the issue rather than a severity label. It ranks issues by how close the attack path runs to business-critical systems, then opens the fix as a pull request. The control boundary matters: every remediation currently requires human approval, so you review and merge the pull request, with autonomous classes of fix gated behind confidence thresholds for the future.

The “why now” came from AWS’s own honeypots in the same week. Amazon Threat Intelligence found a PHP2Shell exploitation attempt in MadPot telemetry dated October 17, four days before CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 were publicly disclosed, and on disclosure day proof-of-concept exploits were circulating around 02:00 UTC, roughly twelve hours before the embargo was due to lift at 14:00 UTC. The practical reading is direct: a patch window measured in days assumes a delay that no longer exists, and an investigation should start from the earliest known exploitation rather than from the disclosure date. AWS also published the AWS WAF mitigations for the same CVEs. Continuum is available in US East (N. Virginia) and US West (Oregon).

Introducing Amazon ECS Gateway
Amazon ECS Gateway splits the work of exposing a service between the people who own the network and the people who own the application. Platform teams define one gateway with the organization’s subnets, security groups, TLS certificates, listener settings, and AWS WAF web ACLs. Application teams opt in by adding a gateway connector to the service definition, and ECS gives them an ACM certificate, a unique DNS hostname, listener rules, a target group, and a security group that admits traffic only from the Application Load Balancer.

The numbers make the economics clear: up to 25 services share a single ALB, ECS provisions the first ALB on first connect and removes ALBs when they are no longer needed, and a gateway can associate with up to 10,000 clusters, with five gateways per cluster. You can adopt it incrementally with UpdateService while your existing load balancer stays in place. Amazon ECS Gateway is available in all commercial Regions where Amazon ECS is available, at no additional charge.

Last week’s launches
Here are some launches that caught my attention this week.

Amazon Polly delivers a major upgrade to its Generative engine – The Generative engine now runs a larger, more expressive catalog: 57 generative voices across 11 languages and 25 locales, with 14 new voices across 9 locales and Hindi and Finnish generally available for the first time. Two of the new voices are Brazilian Portuguese, Leo and Carolina. It is a drop-in upgrade, so the voice names, the same SynthesizeSpeech call, and the price all stay the same while the output gets more natural.

AWS announces TorchNeuron: native PyTorch support for AWS Trainium (public beta) – TorchNeuron is an open source PyTorch backend that lets you run your model on Trainium by switching to .to(‘neuron’), a configuration change instead of a rewrite, with eager execution and torch.compile intact. It integrates with the Neuron Kernel Interface for custom operators, and it works on Trainium2 and Trainium3 instances. If you want the bigger picture on why hardware choice should not lock in your software, the AWS Insights piece on that theme is a good companion read, and the release lands alongside AWS Neuron 2.33.

New ways to customize models with Amazon SageMaker AI, without managing GPUs – Training sessions are now generally available, giving you control of the post-training loop, including custom loss functions and reward signals, while SageMaker AI handles GPU allocation, distributed execution, fault recovery, and checkpoint storage. You pay for the tokens SageMaker AI processes, and the meter stops while your reward function scores a batch. There is a full technical walkthrough, the training session APIs, and new support for Muse Glimmer, Qwen 3.8, and Ministral 3.

Structured outputs for Claude models available on Amazon Bedrock – You can now get schema-compliant JSON back from any Anthropic Claude model on Amazon Bedrock, across both the bedrock-runtime and bedrock-mantle endpoints, without custom parsing, retry logic, or post-processing. Amazon ElastiCache Serverless for Valkey now supports online migration with zero downtime – You can migrate self-managed Valkey or Redis OSS on Amazon EC2 into ElastiCache Serverless with zero downtime, meaning zero errors returned to the client during the move, measured the way the ElastiCache SLA measures availability. You prepare the migration, start the data sync, then choose a manual or automatic cutover, with no third-party replication tools. It is available in all commercial Regions, the China Regions, and the AWS GovCloud (US) Regions.

Build a Hidden Lock With a Magnetic Imager

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


Like many a nerd, I have a certain fascination with locks. This originates in reading the memoir Surely You’re Joking, Mr. Feynman as a teenager, in which Richard Feynman recounts picking locks and cracking combinations while at his day job working on the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos. I currently have a couple of locksport pick sets and training locks that I purchased at hacker conferences over the years (although I’m still terrible at it), and I delight in the YouTube exploits of folks like the LockPickingLawyer.

All of which left me wondering if I could try building an interesting lock. One dodge that lockmakers have long used to challenge would-be plunderers is to conceal the existence of the lock itself. A modern variant of this is the magnetic lock, where a magnet is held or slid against an innocuous portion of a door or box, releasing a catch. The problem with this is that, once you guess the location of the lock, any strong magnet will open it.

So I started thinking about embedding an RFID reader under the surface and resigning myself to keeping track of yet another access card. But then I stumbled across something much more interesting: SparkFun’s US $100 magnetic imaging tile. I realized I could use this to build a magnetic lock that opens with a programmable secret gesture.

I used a few discrete components to drive the servo motor and put the lock into program mode [top], controlled by a Teensy 4.1 microcontroller [middle]. An array of Hall-effect sensors detect magnetic fields [bottom].James Provost

The magnetic imaging tile is a small-run SparkFun board based on the work of Peter Jansen. It features an 8-by-8 grid of Hall-effect sensors, and uses an SPI interface to talk to the microcontroller of your choice. These sensors can detect both the magnitude and polarity of a magnetic field, and the board can report its measurements hundreds to thousands of times per second, depending on how much processing power your microcontroller has available. This is easily fast enough to capture the motion of a magnet being moved by hand across the board’s sensor area—my secret gesture.

An interesting thing about using magnetic fields is that if I was in extreme paranoia mode, I could shield the entire lock behind copper sheeting, something I couldn’t do with an RFID reader. The copper sheet would act as a Faraday cage, blocking any RFID signal, along with any other electromagnetic fields an attacker with an EMF meter might use to locate the lock.

Choosing the Brains of the Box

I chose a Teensy 4.1 as my microcontroller. I like the Teensy because it offers loads of input/output, a 600-megahertz processor, a megabyte of RAM, and a built-in SD card, all in a slim package with an equally slim $32 price tag.

Wiring the Teensy to the magnetic-imaging tile was trivial, only complicated by the fact that one side of the board has a silk-screen error that misidentifies the SPI connections (SparkFun says this will be fixed in future runs of the board).

In extreme paranoia mode, I could shield the entire lock behind copper sheeting.

To create the gesture-recognition software, I once again turned to vibe coding. If you read my Hands On piece in the April 2026 issue of IEEE Spectrum on creating a teletext suite for ham radio, you’ll know I’ve been grappling with how vibe coding should be used in DIY projects, if at all. So this time, instead of letting Claude write all the software, I confined it to writing just two support libraries, which you can find in our online repository at https://github.com/IEEESpectrumHandsOn.

One library is in charge of recording a lock gesture and saving it to the Teensy’s nonvolatile storage. The other library monitors the tile and reports whether or not a newly detected gesture passes a selectable threshold to be considered a match.

I wrote the top-level code myself to keep it as DIY as possible. This code handles things like a button that puts the system in gesture-recording mode after a brief delay, the indicator LED for recording mode, and the code that operates the physical locking mechanism.

Hall-effect sensors rely on a current passing through a p-type slab of silicon. A magnetic field passing through the silicon deflects charge carriers toward the edges of the slab, creating a voltage proportional to the field [left]. By looking at the intensities of the voltages across an 8-by-8 grid of sensors, the microcontroller can reconstruct a gesture [right]. James Provost

Of course, I needed something to lock to demonstrate my proof of concept. I bought a wooden craft box and removed the existing lid latch. I glued a notched beam inside the lid and built a mechanism that uses a servo motor to rotate another beam in and out of this notch. I will be the first to admit this mechanism goes beyond “simple” and into “simplistic,” but it’s sufficiently robust for my demo. The box has ample room for the magnetic imaging tile to be mounted against one wall, along with the Teensy and other supporting electronics. These include a battery pack, the recording button, and a small additional board with two NPN transistors. These convert the Teensy’s 3.3-volt control signals to a 5-V signal needed to drive the servo.

Once it was all put together, I soon discovered that the small size of the magnetic tile’s sensing area—just 3 centimeters square—made it easy to fit the tile inside the box but hard to do the unlock gesture on the outside so that it is read correctly. Marking the outline of the sensor area on the outside defeated the point of having a hidden lock, so instead I once again turned to mining my Irish heritage. I created a Celtic knotwork pattern that runs in a band across the front of the lockbox (I used an online knotwork generator and then projected and traced the pattern onto the box). Using the pattern as a camouflaged reference grid I can make a repeatable gesture within the confines of the sensor area. (To make the gestures I’m using a magnetic stylus borrowed from a ferrite core-memory kit we featured in 2022.)

There’s obvious room for improvement in any more serious installation. The biggest drawback currently is that if the box is locked when the battery runs out, you can’t open it again without breaking something. This could be solved by adding a hidden inductive charging coil. Nonetheless, the demo lockbox works well—now I just have to think what I should store in it!

Plus500 now expects its 2026 revenue and EBITDA to beat what analysts had penciled in, after a third quarter in which its US futures and prediction markets arm kept growing. The London-listed trading group said so in its Q3 2026 trading update on Monday morning. Just over three weeks earlier, in a brief statement on October 2, the company had only said it was trading in line with expectations.

Revenue for the three months to September 30 came in at $203.2 million, an 11% rise on the $182.7 million booked a year ago. EBITDA grew 7% to $88.4 million, a 44% margin. Across the first nine months, revenue reached $666.1 million and EBITDA $275.9 million. Plus500 did not put a new number on its full-year outlook.

Prediction markets move into the core US offer

The part of the update most relevant to the event contract debate is the US business. Plus500 groups futures, options on futures and its prediction market products under what it calls non-OTC, as opposed to its traditional CFD business. That segment produced roughly 16% of group revenue and about 31% of new customers so far this year.

During the quarter, the company added CFTC-regulated prediction market contracts covering both sports and non-sports events, as well as single stock futures. It also signed two international B2B partners, Wealthsimple and Nelogica, which plug into its execution and clearing infrastructure. Funds held for customers in segregated accounts passed $1 billion at the end of September.

The timing matters. Federally regulated exchanges have become a crowded field this year, with sportsbook operators such as DraftKings now running their own platforms. DraftKings CEO Jason Robins recently claimed DKeX had become the third-largest US prediction exchange. Plus500 is taking a different route, acting as broker and clearing firm rather than building a consumer sportsbook brand.

A US super-app planned for early 2027

Plus500 also confirmed plans for a single US app in the first quarter of 2027. The company says the app will bring together futures, options on futures, prediction markets, stocks, options and ETFs. The stock and ETF side depends on Hedge, a US broker-dealer Plus500 has agreed to buy, subject to conditions. Hedge would give the group access to SEC-regulated securities for the first time. A second deal, for Guru in Brazil, would give Plus500 its first local operation in the country and a base for growth elsewhere in the region.

“In the US, Hedge will unlock access to SEC-regulated markets, supporting the planned launch of a new, multi-asset ‘super-app’ in Q1 2027,” chief executive David Zruia said in the statement.

Customer numbers and cash

New customers in the quarter rose 27% to 28,747, while the average cost of acquiring each one fell 14% to $1,150. Active customers totaled 119,528 for Q3. Customer deposits hit a quarterly record of $1.9 billion.

The group finished September with more than $775 million in cash and no debt, after paying out $87.4 million in dividends and spending around $63.5 million on buybacks during the quarter. Both acquisitions will be paid for from existing cash.

For anyone tracking how event contracts are spreading beyond Kalshi and Polymarket, the numbers show a traditional CFD broker now leaning on US prediction markets as a growth line. Real Madrid came away from Camp Nou on Sunday night with three points and a new fitness worry. José Mourinho revealed after the 2-1 win over Barcelona that Fede Valverde had spent a large part of the game carrying an injury, and the timing is far from ideal with three away fixtures to come in the space of seven days.

The coach went out of his way to single out the Uruguayan in his post-match press conference, saying Valverde “played injured for more than 40 minutes”. Mourinho added: “He got injured, wanted to continue, pushed himself to his limits, and gave everything while he was on the field.”

No details yet on Valverde’s problem

Mourinho did not say what kind of injury Valverde had suffered or at what point in the match it happened. At the time of writing, Real Madrid had not published a medical report on the midfielder, who was named in the starting XI alongside Aurélien Tchouaméni, Bernardo Silva and Jude Bellingham.

Lunin answers the call as Courtois misses out

Valverde was not the only fitness story of the weekend. Thibaut Courtois dropped out on matchday because of what the club called a flu-like illness, with Illia Voloshyn called up as cover. Andriy Lunin started in goal and, according to Real Madrid’s own match report, made a fine stop from a Gerard Martín header in the 66th minute.

Courtois, whose future has been discussed in terms of a possible one-year contract extension, now has a few days to recover before the next game.

Three trips in a week

The calendar offers Madrid little breathing space. Mourinho pointed out that “on Sunday we play away; then we play away on Wednesday, and then we have another away match on Saturday”. That run is where Mourinho’s faith in his squad will be tested. He insisted after the Clásico that “I have a rich bench, with players who can be starters”, and he backed it up at Camp Nou by leaving Eduardo Camavinga among the substitutes days after his strong display in the 4-1 win over RB Leipzig. Camavinga came on and set up the late winner for Kylian Mbappé.

The victory was Madrid’s fifth in a row and, as Mourinho noted, leaves them three points behind Barcelona rather than six or nine. Keeping that momentum through three away games will be far easier with Valverde available, so the club’s next medical update on the midfielder will be closely watched before the trip to Santander.
