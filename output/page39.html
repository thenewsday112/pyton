Ancient pollen reveals a surprising twist in the evolution of flowers

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Flowering plants dominate landscapes around the world, and scientists have long debated how they became so successful. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">One familiar explanation points to the asteroid that wiped out the dinosaurs.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Fossil pollen tells it differently. The two moments that remade plant life came tens of millions of years before and after that impact, in the two hottest stretches of the past 120 million years. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">In both of them, plants were dying out and appearing at the same time. In between, when the planet was cooler, very little changed.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Kale Sniderman, an honorary research fellow at the <a href="https://www.unimelb.edu.au/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">University of Melbourne</a>, led the work with colleagues in Australia and Austria. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">They read it all from pollen and spores, the only plant fossils common enough to count this way. Every tally in the study is a tally of pollen types rather than whole plants.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;Each of these extremely warm &#8216;turnover intervals&#8217; lasted a few million years,&#8221; said Greg Jordan, a professor of botany at the <a href="https://www.utas.edu.au/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">University of Tasmania</a> and a co-author. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;Thus, the warmest greenhouse climates simultaneously destroyed and promoted plant diversity, but only when considered over million-year timescales.&#8221;</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-reading-the-fossil-record">Reading the fossil record</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Unlike larger plant remains, pollen can accumulate in enormous quantities in lake sediments, preserving a detailed record of ancient vegetation.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The catch is that paleontologists have not sampled every stretch of time equally. Dig harder in one period and it looks richer than the one next to it.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;The number of available fossils varies over geological time and between regions. Because of this, simple counts of fossilized species can give a misleading sense of diversity over evolutionary time,&#8221; Sniderman said.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">So the team built its own compilation and held the sampling effort steady across every slice of time before comparing one slice with the next. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The southeastern Australian dataset runs to 14,254 records from 335 fossil assemblages at 167 sites. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Much of it came out of decades of drilling reports archived by the <a href="https://gsv.vic.gov.au/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Geological Survey of Victoria</a> and its Tasmanian counterpart.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For most of the past 125 million years that corner of the world sat near 65 degrees south, close to <a href="https://www.earth.com/plants/ancient-amber-discovery-reveals-antarctica-once-had-forests/">Antarctica</a>.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-two-bursts-of-change">Two bursts of change</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Across the record, two short intervals carry almost all the action. One falls in the middle of the Cretaceous, about 95 to 90 million years ago. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The other falls in the late Paleocene and early Eocene, about 58 to 54 million years ago. Both are among the warmest greenhouse episodes of the past 200 million years.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The losses in those intervals were heavy. Of the ferns, mosses and their relatives first recorded before about 89 million years ago, roughly 75% were gone by the second warm episode. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Conifers and their relatives lost roughly 60% of their recorded pollen types by about 84 million years ago.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Even the first flowering plants took about 80% losses by the early Eocene.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">New types arrived in the same intervals. The land plant flora inherited from the Early Cretaceous roughly tripled in variety between about 120 and 55 million years ago, and the two bursts are where that growth happened. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Flowering plants finally overtook the other groups during the second one.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The flora reorganized twice, in other words, rather than drifting. One statistical break in the record falls at 83.5 million years ago and another at 54.3 million.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-both-latitudes-turned-over">Both latitudes turned over</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">A warm world isn&#8217;t warm everywhere in the same way. The Cretaceous tropics have often been read as hot and dry enough to be lethal. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">If so, hothouse episodes might have helped plants only in cool, wet places like the one the Australian record came from.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">So the team ran the same analysis on fossil pollen from northeastern Africa, which sat on the equator through the Cretaceous. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Those records, about 48,000 pollen grains from 19 wells and sites, show the same mid-Cretaceous burst of arrivals and losses in all three plant groups.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For the younger half of the story the team compared its record with a published analysis of pollen from the American tropics. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">There, too, the rate at which new types appeared peaked during the late Paleocene and early Eocene, and tracked global temperature.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-climate-beat-geography">Climate beat geography</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Warmth isn&#8217;t the only candidate. Australia was drifting north and breaking away from Antarctica over this period, and the plant groups were also competing with each other.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team tested all of it. Temperature, the continent&#8217;s isolation, its latitude and the plant groups&#8217; effects on each other went into models that let them compete to explain the record. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Of the 20 relationships those models supported, 13 involved climate.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The same held when whole classes of explanation were compared. Allowing climate terms improved the fit in 89% of cases, while isolation, latitude and plant-on-plant effects did so in fewer than half.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The asteroid comes out looking minor. Among the plants, the <a href="https://www.earth.com/climate/climate-states-mass-extinctions/">extinctions</a> ran well ahead of the impact. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The equatorial record shows ferns and conifers collapsing long before it, and in southeastern Australia the cone-bearing plants were as varied as ever just after the <a href="https://www.earth.com/earth-science/asteroid-dust-may-explain-what-really-killed-the-dinosaurs/">dinosaurs went</a>.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The authors wrote that some global crises, the end-Cretaceous extinction among them, are secondary to warming phases in shaping life on land. A long <a href="https://www.earth.com/earth-science/when-pangeas-tropical-forests-died-252-million-years-ago-they-locked-earth-in-a-five-million-year-hothouse/">hothouse</a> reaches the flora more deeply than a single catastrophe does.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-a-warm-miocene-stalled">A warm Miocene stalled</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">One stretch of time argues with the rest. Australia had drifted far enough north by the early Miocene, roughly 20 million years ago, that local sea surface temperatures nearly matched the early Eocene. Plants there barely responded.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The difference is that the planet as a whole didn&#8217;t match it. Peak Miocene temperatures ran about 18°F (10°C) above today&#8217;s, where the earlier hothouse ran about 36°F (20°C) above. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Global temperature predicted the Australian record better than the local water did, and the American tropics show the same muted Miocene.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Something global, then, rather than a warm patch. The authors propose a chain: extreme warmth, high carbon dioxide and a small gap between the poles and the equator speed up the water cycle. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That spreads warm, wet, <a href="https://www.earth.com/climate/earths-ancient-forests-carry-a-warning-for-forests-today/">forested</a> habitat, which is where new lineages take hold.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">But ancient warming offers little reassurance for today&#8217;s plants. Human-driven climate change is unfolding far faster than either prehistoric episode, leaving species much less time to adapt.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Chris Mays is a paleontologist at the <a href="https://www.nhm.at/en" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Natural History Museum Vienna</a> and a co-author of the study.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;While extinct species will eventually be replaced by new species, not many of us will be satisfied having to wait the few million years for them to evolve, especially since we have the power to prevent the loss of species today, if we stop emitting CO₂ from burning fossil fuels,&#8221; said Mays.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-mystery-behind-plant-diversity">The mystery behind plant diversity</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Why extreme warmth produces new plant species at all is still unsettled. Killing them is the easy half, since enough heat pushes a plant past what it can stand.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The team lists candidates for the other half: a faster water cycle, steadier climates over the long haul, more carbon dioxide for photosynthesis. None of them is tested against the others here.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The African record stops at the end of the Cretaceous. It also holds only seven time intervals, too few to run the model comparison the Australian data supported.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The Australian analysis itself rests on 18 time bins, each a slice of geological time rather than a repeated experiment. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">And a count of <a href="https://www.earth.com/plants/ancient-pollen-reveals-how-grasslands-changed-after-the-last-ice-age/">ancient pollen</a> isn&#8217;t quite a count of plants, because plants that look identical in a pollen grain get counted once.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">More fossil records could help explain why extreme warmth transformed ancient ecosystems. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For now, the findings reveal how profoundly climate can reshape plant life – and why today&#8217;s rapid warming poses a very different threat.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The full study was published in the journal <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41477-026-02429-y" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank"><em>Nature Plants</em></a>.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8212;&#8211;</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Like what you read? <a href="https://www.earth.com/subscribe/">Subscribe to our newsletter</a> for engaging articles, exclusive content, and the latest updates.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Check us out on <a href="https://www.earth.com/earthsnap/">EarthSnap</a>, a free app brought to you by <a href="https://www.earth.com/author/eralls/">Eric Ralls</a> and Earth.com.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8212;&#8211;</p>
