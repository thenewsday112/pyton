Birds can get jet lag on long, fast flights and then fly off course

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

<figure class="wp-block-image size-full"><img alt="Manx Shearwater in flight low over the sea" class="wp-image-2596420" height="900" src="https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/SEI_318506601.jpg" width="1350" /><figcaption class="wp-element-caption"><div class="wp-image__caption--icon"><svg height="15" viewBox="0 0 15 15" width="15" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"><use></use></svg></div><div class="wp-image__caption--text">A Manx shearwater in flight low over the sea, near Wales</div><div class="wp-image__caption--credit">Nature Picture Library / Alamy Stock Photo</div></figcaption></figure> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">You don’t need a jet plane to get jet lag – even feathers will do. At least one species of bird gets jet lag, in the sense that its <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2489064-how-to-harness-your-body-clock-for-a-longer-healthier-life/">body clock</a> can diverge from local time by more than an hour on fast flights across the Atlantic, as revealed by navigational errors.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">“We’ve slightly shied away from using the term jet lag itself, because people associate that with the health impacts of that clock shift, rather than the clock shift itself. But it’s exactly the same process that causes jet lag in people,” says <a href="https://www.biology.ox.ac.uk/people/patrick-lewin">Patrick Lewin</a> at the University of Oxford. “It’s having these physiological and behavioural effects, where the birds are mistiming their behaviours.”</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">It is likely that other long-distance fliers, possibly including some insects and bats as well as birds, also get jet lag in the clock-shift sense, says Lewin.</p> <div class="advert-wrapper advert__mid-article"> <div class="advert"> <div class="advert__inner"> <p class="advert__copy"> Advertisement </p> <div class="advert__slot" id="mid-article-slot"> </div> </div> </div> </div> <div class="read-more__standard wp-block-newscientist-read-more"> <a class="read-more__standard--wrapper no-animation" href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2330907-birds-wearing-tiny-trackers-reveal-migration-routes-in-amazing-detail/"> <strong>Read more</strong> <p>Birds wearing tiny trackers reveal migration routes in amazing detail</p> </a> </div> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Manx shearwaters are small seabirds that fly vast distances, using a technique called <a href="https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abo0200">dynamic soaring</a> to <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/1941172-albatross-inspires-ocean-skimming-drones/">exploit wind gradients</a> near the sea surface. “They’ve evolved this niche of being really amazing long-distance travellers,” says Lewin.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">They breed on <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2579731-an-island-has-been-waging-a-war-on-rats-they-think-theyve-finally-won/">islands around the UK and Ireland</a>. While parents are <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/1821117-mg12817414-200/">raising a chick</a>, they may fly more than 1000 kilometres westwards to reach rich fishing grounds in the mid-Atlantic. Once they have food, they fly back in a fairly straight line, diverting only to skirt around landmasses such as Ireland.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">How do they achieve <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2200976-we-still-dont-know-how-some-animals-find-their-way-on-huge-migrations/">these navigational feats</a>? While some birds can sense Earth’s magnetic field, Manx shearwaters don’t appear to. Lewin’s team has confirmed this by attaching small magnets to the birds that should disrupt geomagnetic navigation. They <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2026.08.037">had no effect</a>.</p> <div class="wp-block-newscientist-newsletter-promotion"> <div class="newsletter-promotion__content"> <p class="newsletter-promotion__tagline">Free newsletter</p> <h4 class="newsletter-promotion__title"> Sign up to The Daily </h4> <p class="newsletter-promotion__excerpt"> </p> <div class="newsletter-promotion__actions"> <a class="button button--secondary button--has-icon" href="https://www.newscientist.com/sign-up/daily/"> <span>Sign up to newsletter</span> <svg height="18" viewBox="0 0 24 24" width="18" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"> <use></use> </svg> </a> </div> </div> <div class="newsletter-promotion__background"> <span class="newsletter-promotion__gradient"></span> <div class="newsletter-promotion__image__wrapper"> <div class="image__wrapper"> <img alt="" class="attachment-4x3-sm size-4x3-sm wp-post-image" height="450" src="https://www.newscientist.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/16102059/the_daily_2025_ed_newsletter_landingtiles_2400px5.jpg?w=600&amp;h=450&amp;crop=1" width="600" /> </div> </div> </div> </div> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Instead, it appears that shearwaters rely on a sun compass – if you know what time it is, you can maintain a course by adjusting your orientation <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/1999803-sun-traces-giant-figures-of-eight-in-the-sky/">relative to the sun</a>. Many animals, from insects and fish to turtles and birds, have been shown to do this. If their internal clocks are reset using artificial light and darkness, they head in the wrong direction.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">“We realised that there might be a kind of natural experiment where the birds were manipulating their own clocks,” says Lewin.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For instance, at 6am, a Manx shearwater wanting to <a href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2338722-is-jet-lag-worse-if-you-travel-east-rather-than-west/">fly eastwards</a> back to its nest needs to head sunwards. At noon, it needs to fly at right angles to the sun, which will be due south. But if a shearwater travels 15° of longitude eastwards, the local time will be 1pm, while its internal clock will still register the time as noon, so the sun will no longer be due south. This means birds flying rapidly east should veer slightly off course in a clockwise direction over the course of day, in the absence of other cues.</p> <div class="read-more__standard wp-block-newscientist-read-more"> <a class="read-more__standard--wrapper no-animation" href="https://www.newscientist.com/article/2484724-australian-moths-use-the-stars-as-a-compass-on-1000-km-migrations/"> <strong>Read more</strong> <p>Australian moths use the stars as a compass on 1000-km migrations</p> </a> </div> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Other researchers have been attaching GPS trackers to Manx shearwaters for decades, so the paths taken on nearly 4000 foraging trips are known.
