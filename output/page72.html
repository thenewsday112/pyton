I Used AI to Fake a Scientific Paper. Here’s What Happened

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


Science has a spam problem. And the spam filter is getting clogged up.

According to all reports, academic publishing is drowning in what has come to be called AI slop: low-quality, mass-produced content generated with AI. By 2025, an estimated 57 percent of all published academic articles exhibited detectable evidence of large language model (LLM) influence, a massive jump from 12 percent in 2023. What’s more, over 12 percent of Nature Communications reviews were found to be completely AI-generated in 2025.

Given that for every 40 papers showing statistical evidence of AI usage in 2025, only one formally disclosed it, these figures likely grossly underestimate LLM involvement today. Of course, evidence of AI use doesn’t necessarily mean a paper is slop. But some estimates suggest that hundreds of thousands of fake, often AI-generated, publications are now produced annually.

“It’s not just the [scientific] record under threat, it’s science as a whole,” warns Amanda Sulicz, IEEE’s research-integrity manager. “AI slop is completely eroding trust in science and every scientific process that we’ve worked for centuries to create.”

Yet statistics and statements claiming that the problem is big—perhaps even existential for science—don’t really get the message across. Just how easy is it to use AI to create a convincing but completely fake paper? And without adding even more to the burden on stretched peer reviewers, who do this work on a volunteer basis, do publishers have the tools to keep AI slop out of the scientific record?




Borrowing from the idea of “vibe coding,” in which programmers use natural-language prompts to generate code, I decided to “vibe-research” a made-up paper by prompting LLMs to generate hypotheses, code, and text with minimal human input. Then I asked Jenny Mahoney, managing editor of the multidisciplinary and open-access journal IEEE Access, if we could war-game an attempt to sneak my AI-generated monstrosity past her journal’s defenses. She and IEEE kindly agreed.

The experiment would become a two-part test. First, whether someone with no special AI expertise could use off-the-shelf AI tools to generate a convincing paper describing research that had never been performed, and do it well enough to fool a publisher’s defenses. Then I would raise the stakes: What happens when more advanced AI systems don’t just write the paper but conduct underlying research?

Along the way, the experiment became a window into how AI automation is transforming scientific discovery and forcing stakeholders to rethink the entire academic publishing system.

Round 1: Creating the Forgery

To give me a chance at creating a convincing fake, I gave myself permission to prompt multiple times (within reason). But to make the fight fair, I couldn’t access the most advanced paid versions of LLMs—only free ChatGPT (at the time, GPT-5.3/5.2 Mini) and Gemini (3 Flash). Moreover, I could not directly edit any output, nor ask for outside help from expert AI-prompt engineers. This needed to be an amateur effort, with the paper generated as much as possible by the LLMs alone.



I assumed that using fake author names and affiliations would immediately raise a red flag, and I didn’t want to drag the names of established academics into the experiment, so the best bet was to name myself as the sole author. That choice limited me to subjects in which I had some academic experience. So I chose my Ph.D. topic from nearly two decades ago: optical solitons, which are individual light pulses that propagate without any change to their shape.

My first attempt—“Can you vibe-research me an original paper in optical solitons?”—left a lot to be desired. Though Gemini was happy to do the task (unlike ChatGPT, which was more forceful in not generating synthetic data or studies), the result was underwhelming. And it highlighted a recurring problem during the process: Without any real research to work with, the output was laughably thin, with the LLMs often struggling to fill four pages. However, as I became more adept at prompting the models, they loosened up and became more characteristically loquacious.

I broke the paper down into its constituent parts—abstract, introduction, methods, and so on—and tackled them one by one. If one LLM refused to go any further, switching to the other and asking what the next step would look like “hypothetically” would often do the trick. After a dozen or so prompts, I had a realistic-looking paper. A dozen or so more prompts generated and tidied up the figures and captions, produced references and citations, and converted the paper into IEEE Access’s required style.

At this point, I read the full paper carefully. To me, it looked pretty good. The science sounded convincing. I even had visions of returning to academia to conduct in real life the experiments that the LLMs had proposed.

What’s more, I couldn’t see any obvious telltale “AI-isms” that would immediately be flagged. For example, the LLMs didn’t produce any tortured phrases—unusual paraphrases of standard technical terms, like “profound neural organization” replacing “deep neural network.”

Other AI-isms were arguably present, involving stylistic tics like hedging language (“potentially, typically”), grouping things in threes (“The study provides a clear, concise, and actionable framework”), and inserting formulaic transitions (“In summary,…”). But as the previous sentence demonstrates, these tics are hard to spot because they are often used by unimaginative humans, too.

Round 1: Attempt to Publish

Two Ways to Make a Paper With AI


I took two very different routes to creating a scientific paper with AI. First, I coaxed free, general-purpose chatbots through the process step by step; then I turned to FARS, an autonomous research system designed to do much of the scientific work itself.

ROUND 1: CHATGPT + GEMINI

1. Choose a research topic
I ask the free chatbots to create an original paper on optical solitons, my former Ph.D. field.

2. Break the paper into pieces
The initial results are thin, so I prompt separately for the abstract, introduction, methods, and other sections.

3. Keep steering the LLMs
When one model refuses or stalls, I switch models, rephrase prompts, and ask what the next step would look like “hypothetically.”

4. Build and polish the paper
After about two dozen prompts, the LLMs have generated the text, figures, captions, references, and citations and formatted the paper for IEEE Access.

5. Put it to the test
I send the fake paper to IEEE Access, where automated integrity checks flag fabricated and unverifiable references.

→ CAUGHT
IEEE’s integrity checks flag fabricated and unverifiable references.

ROUND 2: FARS

1. Give FARS a research topic
I enter an abbreviated version of the same optical-soliton topic. FARS generates a research proposal in 21 minutes.

2. Authorize the experiment
With a click, I tell FARS to carry out the experiment described in its proposal.

3. Let FARS do the research
FARS conducts the computational experiment and emails me the next day to say it’s complete.

4. Tell FARS to write the paper
Without checking the results, I click “Write paper.” Two hours later, FARS produces a submission-ready article.

5. Put it to the test
I remove FARS’s disclosure, put my name on the paper, and send it to IEEE Access.

→ CAUGHT
The references are real, but IEEE flags the paper’s heavy reliance on non-peer-reviewed preprints.


Handing the fake paper over to IEEE Access left me torn. After putting in a fair amount of effort into making the AI-generated forgery, would I be happy if it passed through the checkers, or saddened that the only remaining line of defense for publishers is the evermore-burdened peer-review community? And if it was spotted, would I be angry that I didn’t think of a red flag or be glad the checkers did their job?

As it turned out, I was filled with relief when the paper got picked up quickly for having hallucinated references, otherwise known as ghost citations. When I ran the paper through Gemini afterward, without qualm it happily reported that seven “modern LLM-related research references have been fabricated to support the paper’s specific narrative.” Thanks for the heads up, Gemini!

In fact, Gemini underestimated how many ghost citations there were. When Mahoney ran the paper through a research-integrity checker called STM Integrity Hub, she picked up 12, and another checker from MolecularConnections could not validate 24 references, including one that had the classic tell of “John Smith” as a single author (no offense to all the academically inclined John Smiths out there). “I am an experimental physicist by training, so this was not at all my area, but nothing seemed majorly scientifically wrong” about the paper, says Mahoney. “It was just that so many hallucinated references immediately cast doubt on the entire work.”

Feedback on the fake paper from Gaetano Assanto, an expert in optical solitons and a former collaborator of mine from Roma Tre University, in Italy, was similarly mixed. Concentrating on the paper itself, he thought it looked professionally written. But he noted that the mathematics was very dense (“too much math for me in a single shot!”) and would require specialized knowledge to evaluate. And, although the text looked legitimate, Assanto noted an excessive use of acronyms in the abstract, and he was suspicious of a couple of confidently proclaimed physics statements that were at best debatable.

The AI Arms Race

On one level, this outcome was positive: A complete novice using free tools couldn’t get an AI-faked paper past the publishers’ automated defenses or a potential peer reviewer’s eagle eye. But it was hardly a resounding victory for the scientific publishing system. With general-purpose LLMs and no special prompting expertise, I had managed to conjure a superficially plausible scientific paper out of research that had never happened. And more advanced LLMs are no longer making the kind of mistakes their free, dumber relatives are making.


These tools aim to address a serious bottleneck to scientific discovery: us.


It’s common knowledge in the academic publishing world that, in the cat-and-mouse game between bad actors and publishers, AI slop papers continue to slip through publishers’ safeguards. “It feels like building a detection tool today may not be effective with the AI-created paper of tomorrow,” says Luigi Longobardi, IEEE’s director of publishing ethics and conduct. This leaves the humble peer reviewer as a publisher’s last line of defense. Yet peer review is simply not fit to handle the volume of papers—both legitimate and slop—passing through.

Even before AI tools became ubiquitous, the peer-review system was being pushed to its breaking point, processing well over 3 million papers in 2022. Fundamentally, this problem stems from science’s publish-or-perish culture, which pressures academics to constantly publish papers in prestigious journals to secure funding and advance their careers. This culture not only forces many academics to regularly churn out incremental research, but has also long driven some to enlist the help of “paper mills”—unethical organizations that produce low-quality or fabricated papers—just to keep pace. AI only amplifies these existing dynamics.

Burden on Academics

Mahoney says IEEE Access alone receives about 60,000 submissions per year, accepting around 20 percent, or about 12,000 papers. Every accepted paper is reviewed by at least two independent reviewers. But despite the journal’s roster of tens of thousands of peer reviewers, it’s getting harder and harder to secure their services, with the average number of reviewer invitations being sent out for each article increasing by 50 percent since 2018. For these volunteers, peer review is a significant commitment on top of teaching and conducting their own research. Now they’re also being asked to spot AI slop.

Of course, peer review is not the end of scrutiny for any paper. Once it has entered the scientific record, it must then pass muster with the scientific community. This is where thousands of papers with telltale signs of inappropriate AI use (and other forms of research misconduct) have been exposed.

There are two high-profile homes for this important and completely volunteer-led post-publication review work. PubPeer is a place where researchers, often called scientific sleuths, can anonymously identify flaws in the literature. And Retraction Watch documents and publicizes corrections to the scientific record.

Even the people who track problems in the scientific literature can find themselves caught up in AI fabrications. Ivan Oransky, cofounder of Retraction Watch and a member of the PubPeer Foundation’s board of directors, has encountered papers containing fabricated references attributed to him. In one case, a journal article criticizing post-publication peer review included a fabricated reference supposedly written by Oransky himself—“which was sort of funny,” he chuckles.

Oransky argues that AI has exposed long-standing weaknesses in the scientific publishing system rather than creating an entirely new problem: “What I liken it to is a fire, where publish-or-perish has always been there as kindling, paper mills are the gasoline, and AI is the accelerant.” But though concerning, the crisis could create an opportunity, he says. “If I’m trying to be optimistic, maybe AI slop and the volume of submissions could make more people aware of those problems and try and solve them at a fundamental first-principles level.”

Blurred Lines

My homemade forgery had failed for reasons that today’s publishers know how to spot. But that raised a more unsettling question: What happens when AI isn’t simply fabricating a paper but conducting research that blurs the line between legitimate science and AI fabrication?

Making my vibe-researched paper look like a cave drawing, new automated research systems are handing increasingly large parts of the scientific process over to AI. Tools including FARS (Fully Automated Research System), AutoResearchClaw, The AIScientist-v2, Zochi, AI Researcher, and DeepScientist all aim to address a serious bottleneck to scientific discovery: us.


What happens when AI isn’t simply fabricating a paper but conducting research that blurs the line between legitimate science and AI fabrication?


Often with just a single prompt, these AI engines can conduct a comprehensive literature search, from which they can generate a completely reasonable research proposal. They can design and conduct experiments using digital datasets and simulations and then write a full research paper—all with no human input after the initial prompt. Very recent systems are even beginning to go beyond the digital realm. LabClaw, for example, now links to autonomous labs that can generate real-world data for tasks such as drug discovery.

The output is, to most intents and purposes, actual research, and therefore potentially worthy of publication. But with humans in the loop in only the most nominal sense, these tools bring into question the meaning of the term “legitimate science.”

FARS, which caused a stir this year when it created 166 papers in 417 hours, was developed by the Singapore-based startup Analemma. “We built FARS because research is still constrained by human attention, fragmented tools, scarce technical labor, and slow experimental iteration,” explains Xiwen Su, Analemma’s manager of product operations and partnerships. “FARS can pursue many research threads simultaneously, 24/7, without fatigue, and at scale.” (The online service that I used to access FARS was discontinued after Analemma was acquired by Baidu while this article was being reported.)

Su and the team behind Analemma kindly agreed to let me loose on FARS and gave me enough credits to create a paper. Which gave me another opportunity to put Mahoney’s research-integrity checkers to the test.

Round 2: Using an AI Science Engine

FARS is not an LLM in and of itself, but rather an agentic AI-orchestration system connected to a computing cluster that has access to essentially any open- or closed-source LLM it needs. The system uses four specialized agents for ideation, planning, conducting the experiment, and writing, all designed to perform the main tasks of science autonomously.

Though FARS initially focused on AI research, Su told me it should be reasonably effective across other domains. As I wanted a topic similar to that of my first fake paper, I pasted in an abridged version of that paper’s title—“LLM-aided Optical Soliton Dynamics Modelling for Next-Gen Communications”—into the prompt box and hit “Send.” Just 21 minutes later, FARS generated a research proposal based on an extremely detailed literature review—far more than I or any other human could have achieved in that time.

With a click, I tasked FARS with conducting the experiment it detailed in its research proposal. The next day, I received an email saying my experiment was complete. Without checking the results, I pressed a button marked “Write paper,” and the final submission-ready article was produced in 2 hours.

At this point, I finally had a look at some of the output. At first glance, the paper looked convincingly human-written. The only giveaway was a line just below the abstract reading: “WARNING: This paper was generated by an automated research system.” I deleted that, put my name in its place, and sent it to Mahoney.

Round 2: Attempt to Publish

Because FARS is designed to spot gaps in the scientific literature and conduct real research to close them, I was more confident it would breeze through the publisher’s checks. But just as my fake paper fell down in the references, so too did the AI-generated real one.

“Your fake article had lots of hallucinated references,” says Mahoney. “While this AI-generated one had no fake references, our team would have rejected this paper because it did have a huge percentage of references to preprints, which likely has something to do with the dataset the AI has access to.” Because preprints haven’t yet undergone peer review, IEEE Access considers heavy reliance on them a problem.

In addition, although the research-integrity checking tools didn’t spot any issues with figures, Mahoney did. One figure included a red line labeled with the nonsense term “lhewl,” and an AI “brain” in another figure had some random dots in it, which ironically were a tell that it was AI generated.

Automation Changing the Landscape

Automated research systems are very much a work in progress, but they are already starting to make waves in the scientific community. For instance, an AI Scientist academic paper and papers from the Autoscience Institute were accepted through double-blind peer review in 2025 by the International Conference on Learning Representations. As these systems improve and start to produce research outputs at volume, the waves are likely to build to a tsunami that will threaten today’s scientific and publishing process.

“FARS doesn’t kill the paper, but it does challenge the paper as the unit of knowledge,” argues Su. “We believe the future looks more like a living knowledge graph—where individual, well-scoped contributions (some made by humans, some by machines) connect and build on each other dynamically.”



In this future, humans will continue to have roles in the scientific and publication processes—just very different and limited ones. For example, instead of conducting experiments themselves, humans would sense-check and review automated output at key points. And traditional line-by-line peer-reviewing will be a thing of the past, though Su emphasizes that this change will not eliminate human judgment. “Automated systems can handle reproducibility checks, statistical validation, and novelty screening,” she says. “Humans can then focus on the harder question: Does this [research] matter?”

A Human-Centric or AI-Driven Future?

Though from a very different world than Su, Mahoney agrees that humans need to be freed to focus on the science, and that technology holds the key. “It’s promising to see the progress that has been made to automate some of the triage tasks, such as author checks, tortured phrases, duplicate submission, reference validity, et cetera,” she says. “During peer review, we’re also using AI semantic search tools to help editors match manuscripts to the most relevant reviewers, who are best placed to stop the slop before entering the scientific record.”

In addition, Mahoney thinks that AI cannot be banned or overly restricted, as this will stymie progress at a time when human knowledge could and should be growing at an unprecedented rate. “Researchers are increasingly using AI in their work, from literature discovery, to code generation, to data analysis, and this is not necessarily a bad thing,” she argues. But she disagrees that peer reviewers and humans more broadly will play a smaller part in scientific progress; Mahoney believes the future still requires humans at the wheel. The question is not just whether the research matters, she says. “The question should be: Can this work be trusted and reproduced?”

To this end, Longobardi argues that AI may ultimately require scientists to prove not just what they discovered but how they discovered it. One possibility, he says, is using technology to verify research at the source. “The system doesn’t really help because it’s not designed to not trust the paper,” he explains. “Maybe institutions can think about developing their own trust markers, automatically integrating the equipment researchers use with blockchain technology to say ‘Yes, this person was in the lab on this day and got this data.’”

Elements of this approach are already emerging. For instance, two departments within the Max Planck Institute of Colloids and Interfaces have implemented a sample database, assigning each and every sample created under their roofs with a unique ID and QR code. And the University of Southampton’s School of Chemistry and Chemical Engineering has rolled out an electronic lab notebook system. These innovative safeguards represent early efforts toward keeping humans front and center in scientific progress, and making the scientific process itself more transparent.

Whether this human-centric vision, Su’s AI-driven aspiration, or some other picture of the future wins out remains to be seen. The one thing my vibe-research adventure has clarified is that business-as-usual science is not an option. Scientists and publishers must adapt to a world in which it’s becoming increasingly difficult to spot the difference between real research and AI forgeries, and where deciding what counts as legitimate scientific work may become even harder than detecting outright fraud.

The problem with thinking you’re part Neanderthal

There’s a theory that many of us have an “inner Neanderthal.” The idea is that Homo sapiens and its cousin species once bred, leaving some people today with a trace of Neanderthal DNA.

Industrial Intelligence: The next growth opportunity for manufacturing

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every manufacturer has its own industrial intelligence. Some is documented, and some resides in the knowledge of experienced workers.

Consider an experienced operator preparing to restart a line after a changeover. The machine settings matter, but so does knowing how a particular material behaves, what happened during the previous shift, and when an apparently minor variation deserves a closer look. A procedure can describe the sequence, but successful execution depends on the judgment that comes from experience and the organization’s accumulated know-how.

That combination is industrial intelligence: a manufacturer’s accumulated know-how, operating practices, production data, and understanding of its own business.

The opportunity now is to make more of that intelligence available wherever decisions are made, then connect it to the physical world of manufacturing. The strategic destination is not more agents, but rather to turn the existing business intelligence into an engine for profitable growth.

Individual knowledge can be translated into organizational advantage

Expertise has limited reach when it stays with one person, inside one system, or within one plant. Leaders now must determine how to extend that reach without stripping away the context that makes the knowledge useful.

For example, an engineer evaluating a process change needs more than a production record. They need to understand the circumstances behind it: the equipment configuration, the quality requirements, and the trade-offs an operator made. Connecting those elements can help people apply experience beyond the place where it originated.

When individual knowledge becomes widely accessible, innovation can spread to the maintenance workshop, the planning office, the engineering team, and the production floor. More people can contribute because more people can work with what the organization knows.

But access to knowledge is only part of the opportunity. Manufacturing ultimately is physical, and it depends on materials, machines, and products.

When intelligence participates in action

Physical AI describes systems that can perceive conditions, reason about them, and take action within the physical world. By connecting machines and processes, manufacturers get a clearer view of what is happening on the factory floor. Agents can then reason on top of that information to identify patterns, predict problems, and determine the best course of action. Physical AI brings business intelligence into the physical process, allowing machines and systems to respond to changing conditions.

Consider a robotic-handling operation encountering variation in the parts arriving at its station. Vision can help the system assess a part’s position. A model informed by the material and task can help determine an appropriate movement. Connected controls can then execute an adjustment within validated limits. The value is not merely seeing the variation; it is responding appropriately.

The same principle applies to quality operations. When Toyota Industries connected previously fragmented paint shop data, engineering and operations teams were given the context needed to understand defect drivers. The company reports that analysis cycles fell from five days to under four hours, while its pilot reduced relevant paint defects by about 25%. The breakthrough was not simply identifying another signal; it was helping people understand why defects were occurring and respond before the outcome was locked in.

This is the type of situation where industrial intelligence has become essential. Data may reveal what is happening, but industrial intelligence provides the context for what should happen, which constraints cannot be breached, and when uncertainty requires human judgment.

Simulation offers a way to examine proposed actions before applying them to equipment. Manufacturers can explore how a change might affect a process in a digital environment. That does not remove the need for physical validation, but it creates room to learn without treating the production line as the first experiment.

Responsible deployment, therefore, begins with operational discipline. Leaders need to establish clear authority or what systems may change, reliable connections to equipment, defined safety boundaries, and a record of decisions and actions. People must be able to recognize uncertainty, intervene, and bring equipment to a safe state. Trust rests on those practical arrangements, not on confidence in a model alone.

More capable machines, more capable people

Manufacturing has long combined human ingenuity with increasingly capable tools. Physical AI extends that relationship. Intelligent equipment can take on suitable repetitive or physically demanding tasks, while agent-assisted operations can help people navigate information-intensive decisions and changing conditions.

The aim is not to make machines smarter; it is to see what people can build when knowledge can travel, intelligence can act, and ambition has more room to become reality.

Rockwell Automation illustrates the point. At its Singapore plant, technicians use a maintenance copilot that combines manufacturing data, machine manuals, and knowledge captured from experienced workers. This system has reduced the time required for new workers to learn how to troubleshoot hundreds of machines from nine months to three. An engineer could explore more process alternatives before committing to a change. An operator could help improve the system’s response rather than repeatedly managing the same exception.

This type of progress requires investment in equipment but also in people: training, time to learn, and playing a meaningful role in shaping how systems work. Machines become more capable so people can become more capable.

Industrial Intelligence is the answer to sustainable growth

Industrial intelligence connects what a manufacturer knows: the experience of its people, the signals from its operations, and the lessons accumulated over time. Physical AI gives that intelligence a way to act in the physical world, turning knowledge into decisions and decisions into action.

The next challenge for industry is not simply to produce more, but to grow without adding complexity at the same rate. Intelligence can help manufacturers turn experience into scalable capability—improving productivity, accelerating innovation, expanding capacity, and enabling people to solve problems that were previously difficult to tackle.

The next frontier of industry is not smarter machines or more automated factories; it is businesses that can continuously learn, adapt, and act. The opportunity is to turn the intelligence embedded in every operation into a compounding advantage for growth.

IEEE Xplore Digital Library Adds AI Assistant

IEEE recently released the IEEE Xplore AI Research Suite, built on the full text of millions of peer-reviewed publications in the IEEE Xplore Digital Library. The library offers full-text access to IEEE standards, journals, magazines, conference proceedings, and more.

Researchers who use the AI suite to search the digital library receive answers grounded in verified scientific results, ensuring a high level of accuracy and trust.

The suite is available as an optional subscription upgrade to all IEEE Xplore Digital Library institutional subscribers.

Powered by a proprietary AI system optimized for STEM literature, the suite introduces powerful, intelligent features within the digital library. The suite enables users to seamlessly search and discover articles to accelerate their comprehension of complex technical papers.

The system surfaces deep insights, replacing the typical keyword queries with natural, conversational research workflows.

As AI tools become more common in conducting research, IEEE collaborated to build the suite around a simple principle: Answers should be trustworthy, not just fast, says W. Clem Karl, vice president, IEEE Publication Services and Products.

“AI can be a highly effective and beneficial tool that can help researchers accelerate discovery, enhance decision-making, and surface new connections,” Karl says. “However, many AI engines present a great risk of hallucinating answers or introducing bias into otherwise credible research. With this in mind, IEEE has taken a responsible approach in our AI strategy by developing concrete safeguards to ensure our solution is rigorously grounded in scientific evidence that users can trust.

“As stewards of the scholarly record, we held the new AI engine to the same standards as the peer-reviewed research we publish: A researcher shouldn’t have to trust an answer; they should be able to verify the work behind it.”

Collaboration with the scientific community

The IEEE Xplore AI Research Suite was developed in close collaboration with global scientific and library communities.

The development process began with research into user needs and the challenges associated with existing AI research assistants. More than 3,000 testers—including engineers, scientists, and library information professionals—provided feedback to refine the AI engine and support research workflows.

Capabilities include:

  • Semantic AI search. Users can input free-form, natural-language queries or paste long blocks of text to locate similar research and identify novel solutions.
  • Time-saving summaries. The platform generates immediate, accurate AI overviews of search results and provides in-context definitions of technical terms so that users can grasp new concepts faster and accelerate their research.
  • Built on trusted content. The suite is the only AI research assistant that uses both the full text and metadata of IEEE’s peer-reviewed journal articles and conference papers to optimize the accuracy and relevance of the results.
  • Comprehensive coverage. A variety of STEM metadata is available from journal articles and conference papers that have been curated to include scholarly articles without the typical noise found in other tools.
  • Source links and verifiable results. The AI-generated overviews include reference links to source documents, allowing users to verify information and cross-reference findings.

Reducing hallucinations

To mitigate the risk of AI hallucinations, the search-retrieval process is limited to querying the millions of IEEE peer-reviewed full-text documents, alongside an expertly curated dataset of STEM metadata. If the AI tool is unable to find relevant scientific content within the defined dataset, it is designed to inform the user rather than generate fabricated answers.

“IEEE is committed to providing the technical community with a highly precise and reliable research experience,” says Steven Heffner, IEEE’s chief publication officer and managing director of IEEE Publications. “The IEEE Xplore AI Research Suite offers something unique in that it is the only AI research assistant that leverages both the full-text and metadata of IEEE’s peer-reviewed content, rather than abstracts alone.

“The difference is significant: Our internal testing showed a threefold increase in result quality and accuracy across every key metric. These results underscore how critical full-text grounding is for technical research.”

For more information about features and coverage, or to request a demo for your organization, visit the IEEE Xplore AI Research Suite website.

Turn subsurface uncertainty into decision-ready insight

Drilling campaigns are among the most expensive and operationally complex activities in the energy industry. Rigs, crews, equipment, and logistics are scheduled months in advance, leaving little room for delay. Every hour of downtime can carry significant costs. Geoscientists and engineers rely on reservoir models to understand the subsurface and guide drilling decisions, often under intense time pressure. Yet the current modeling infrastructure that many companies rely on for these critical decisions was designed for smaller datasets, simpler workflows, and siloed operations. As subsurface data grows in scale and complexity, teams need a faster, more connected way to transform uncertainty into actionable decisions.

Now generally available, agile reservoir modeling (ARM) from SLB, integrated with Azure Data Manager for Energy (ADME), is co-engineered for cloud scale to close that gap. The combined solution connects ensemble-centric reservoir modeling and simulation with a governed data foundation, elastic Azure high-performance computing, and scalable storage designed for the throughput and concurrency of large ensemble studies.

Learn more about Azure Data Manager for Energy

Scale ensemble modeling with ARM on Azure

A single reservoir model represents one plausible interpretation of the subsurface. Ensemble modeling evaluates hundreds of model realizations together, giving asset teams a broader view of potential outcomes and a stronger basis for understanding uncertainty and risk.

The industry has long recognized the value of ensemble modeling for risk-aware decision-making, but adoption has been constrained by infrastructure and software designed for smaller, sequential studies. Moving from a limited model set to hundreds of realizations multiplies the compute, storage, data movement, and results that must be managed. ARM addresses this barrier by distributing realization generation and simulation across elastic Azure high-performance computing, while scalable storage provides the capacity, throughput, and parallel access required by ensembles of different sizes.

Connect reservoir workflows to a governed data foundation

Building and updating reservoir models draws on many systems: seismic, wellbore, interpretation, and simulation data, plus the applications and compute that process them. When these pieces are fragmented, customers must connect and orchestrate them on their own, and large-scale simulation becomes a disconnected, one-off exercise.

SLB and Microsoft combined their respective technologies, ARM engineered by SLB and ADME engineered by Microsoft, to remove that burden. Rather than a set of loosely connected tools, customers get one integrated workflow in which each component has a clear role:

  • Azure Data Manager for Energy is the governed data foundation. OSDU® Technical Standard-aligned services and OSDU Domain Data Management Services (DDMS) manage seismic, wellbore, interpretation, and simulation data.
  • Petrel™ subsurface software is where engineers define interpretation and uncertainty workflows, connected directly to data in ADME.
  • Agile reservoir modeling is the ensemble workflow engine. It orchestrates the generation of reservoir realizations and simulation runs and supports discovery and analysis at enterprise scale.

As a result, teams build ensembles from trusted corporate data and publish results back for discovery and reuse, which reduces data movement and improves collaboration across asset teams.

Put ensemble modeling into practice with a connected workflow

Beyond integration and scale, ARM distinguishes itself by orchestrating all stages of an ensemble study across modeling, simulation, data management, and analysis. The workflow tightly connects the tools reservoir teams already use with Azure-scale infrastructure and the governed data services provided by Azure Data Manager for Energy.

Agile Reservoir Modeling workflow using cloud HPC to generate and simulate multiple reservoir-model realizations in parallel delivered to web-based interface for analysis.

A walkthrough of how this works today:

Reservoir engineers define uncertainty workflows in Petrel software and prepare simulation models for supported SLB simulators, including the Intersect™ reservoir simulator and Eclipse™ reservoir simulator. ARM generates the realizations, prepares the simulation cases, and coordinates their execution across the Azure environment.

Azure high-performance computing executes multiple realizations in parallel. A shared, high-performance Azure Files volume holds model inputs, grids, simulation decks, temporary files, and results so the simulation fleet can read and write concurrently. Because compute capacity, storage capacity, throughput, and IOPS can be scaled independently, the environment can be sized for the concurrency of the ensemble rather than the constraints of fixed infrastructure.

As simulations complete, SLB reservoir engineering services manage the resulting ensembles and use OSDU search capabilities on ADME to support discovery, filtering, and sorting across large studies. Teams can locate relevant realizations and supported, published results without manually navigating disconnected project stores.

Engineers can analyze ensembles in the reservoir engineering workspace or load them back into Petrel software for further interpretation, history matching, and reservoir engineering studies. This allows teams to work in the environment and application best suited to each task while maintaining continuity, without having to step outside their workflow to manage orchestration.

ADME provides entitlement-based access control integrated with Microsoft Entra ID and supports compliance controls such as OSDU legal tags. Connecting ARM workflows to this governed data foundation helps maintain consistent access and compliance as corporate subsurface data is used across modeling and simulation. Because ARM workflows are integrated through Petrel software in ADME, reservoir engineers can perform ensemble-scale studies leveraging their corporate-wide data stores and publish results for discovery, eliminating friction and driving collaboration. Microsoft and SLB engineering collaborated closely to build the integration between Petrel software and ARM for enterprise-scale performance.

For reservoir engineers and asset teams, ARM turns ensemble modeling into a repeatable workflow rather than a bespoke infrastructure project. Teams can stress-test development concepts against calibrated ensembles that honor observed production history, not simply run a larger number of models. As new wells are drilled and new data becomes available, they can update their reservoir understanding without waiting for a major model rebuild. For energy IT and data leaders, the workflow connects simulation to an OSDU standard-aligned architecture with consistent identity, governance, search, and interoperability, reducing isolated data copies and disconnected applications.

The future of the reservoir lifecycle

Building on this partnership, Microsoft and SLB are working within the OSDU community to advance a more cloud-native standard for reservoir data. A shared, governed data foundation also paves the way for AI and agentic experiences that can discover data, coordinate workflows, and surface insights across the reservoir lifecycle. Combined with Azure-scale compute and storage, this creates a path toward more automated and continuous reservoir modeling.
