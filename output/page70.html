Managing open-weight model risk in enterprise deployments

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The growing AI landscape is giving enterprises more choice in how they build, customize, and deploy increasingly capable models. Open-weight models are an important part of that landscape. They give enterprises access to model weights and greater flexibility to inspect, evaluate, modify, and run models within their own environments.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">While that flexibility can be valuable, it also affects how enterprises evaluate models, where they implement safeguards, and who is responsible for maintaining those safeguards over time.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">As enterprises choose which models to deploy, the relevant question is broader than whether a model is open-weight or closed-weight. Enterprises need to understand what they are using the model for, what could go wrong in that context, and what evidence and controls they need to manage risks.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Today, Microsoft and NVIDIA are publishing Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise, the first in a planned series of joint resources intended to help enterprises address those questions. The paper introduces a shared risk taxonomy and an approach to managing model risks grounded in established standards and frameworks, including International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standards and the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The paper reflects a core idea: weight openness alone does not determine an AI system’s risk. Risk depends on the model, use case, deployment, and safeguards.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Read the whitepaper: Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise<br />Understanding risk in the deployment context<br />Open- and closed-weight models can share many of the same underlying risks, but how those risks materialize depends on the deployment. A model used to help an employee draft a document presents different considerations than the same model used in a customer-facing workflow, just as a standalone inference endpoint differs from a system connected to external data or tools.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The architecture matters too. A risk may originate in the model, while the most effective control may sit in the inference environment, application, or surrounding operational processes. The joint guidance looks beyond the model label to factors including provenance, intended use, users, deployment conditions, and surrounding controls. This first paper focuses primarily on model-layer risks. Evaluating a model alone does not establish the safety of an agent or application: teams must also assess its permissions, data access, tools, runtime, and operational controls.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Establishing a common language<br />Managing these risks consistently also requires a common vocabulary across engineering, security, legal, safety, and business teams.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The initial Microsoft and NVIDIA taxonomy organizes risks into five broad categories: Cybersecurity, Data Protection, Content, Human-AI Interaction, and Bias. It is not a policy or deployment checklist, but instead it should be used by enterprises to adapt it to their own systems, use cases, and risk criteria, then connect relevant risks to evaluations, mitigations, and accountable owners.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Using evaluations as risk evidence<br />Evaluations help turn the taxonomy into something enterprises can use. The paper maps evaluations to a selection of risks across the five categories, giving teams a way to start with a risk that matters for their deployment and identify evaluations that can produce relevant evidence about it.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Microsoft Foundry helps enterprises assess quality, groundedness, and safety with built-in and custom evaluations and automated red teaming. Its configurable content-safety controls help detect and mitigate harmful inputs and outputs and prompt attacks. Teams should validate control availability for their model and deployment, including preview status, and combine these capabilities with application-specific testing, monitoring, and human oversight.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Evaluations still need to be interpreted in the context of a particular deployment and the goal is to make it easier for teams to connect the risks they identify with useful evidence, while building a broader set of evaluation resources that can evolve alongside models, deployment patterns, and emerging risks. Microsoft and NVIDIA plan to use the joint repository to share additional evaluations and point to existing resources that can help enterprises assess specific risks.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Accounting for changes in control and responsibility<br />Open-weight access gives enterprises greater control over how a model is evaluated, modified, and deployed. Model access and deployment are separate choices: open-weight models can also be used through managed platforms. Responsibility for safeguards depends on the service and deployment configuration; enterprises that operate their own model deployments may take on controls otherwise provided by a managed service.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Those safeguards can operate at different layers, and (depending on the risk) enterprises might use model changes, access controls, input and output filtering, monitoring, or human review. The important step is connecting each identified risk to controls that can effectively address it within the deployed system. Those decisions need to be revisited as models and deployments change.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Building on this work<br />Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise focuses primarily on the model layer. Future work from Microsoft and NVIDIA is intended to extend this approach across the runtime, harness, and application layers, with additional technical guidance on evaluation and mitigation.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">We also expect the resources supporting this work to evolve as new models, evaluations, and deployment patterns emerge. The joint repository will provide a place to share additional evaluation resources, including contributions from the broader community.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">For enterprises deploying open-weight models today, the paper provides guidance they can begin applying to their own use cases. We encourage developers, security practitioners, researchers, and others working in this space to read the paper, explore the evaluations, and contribute evaluations that can help strengthen this shared body of work.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Read the joint Microsoft-NVIDIA paper, Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Explore the joint repository and technical resources.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise was developed jointly by Microsoft and NVIDIA, with Andrew Berkley and April Chen of Microsoft and Michael Boone, Eileen Long, and Arun Rao of NVIDIA leading the development and editing of the paper.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Explore Microsoft Foundry evaluations and content-safety controls for implementation guidance.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">To learn more about Microsoft Security solutions, visit our website. Bookmark the Security blog to keep up with our expert coverage on security matters. We’ve spent a ton of time talking about the difference between sandboxes and containers, and helping our customers and the industry as a whole understand when to use which. In fact, Docker president Mark Cavage dedicated his <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G1tT3pwbsMM" id="dkr_opening-keynote-94981" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">opening keynote</a> at WeAreDevelopers World Congress North America to this exact topic, using live demos to show why sandboxes (and not containers) provide the level of isolation required to contain autonomous agents. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">To be clear: none of that means we suddenly think less of containers or the vital role they will continue to play in the software development process and ecosystem. We are Docker, after all. And lest anyone forget, as the just-released <a href="https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2026/technology/data" id="dkr_2026-stack-overflow-developer-survey-94981" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">2026 Stack Overflow Developer Survey</a> confirmed yet again, when it comes to containers, there’s Docker, and there’s everyone else. Vendors, analysts, research firms, everyone’s got a groundbreaking new survey. When it comes to the developer community, it’s arguably <strong>the</strong> survey, the one developers themselves pay attention to. Over the course of 16 years, it has become something of a de facto standard for those of us interested in truly understanding how developers think and act. Released earlier this month, the 2026 survey reached more than 30,000 respondents across 169 countries.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Of course, this year’s survey did more than just remind us of Docker’s continued container dominance. It painted a picture of an industry in transition as developers grapple with one of the biggest questions in technology right now: how to bridge the gap between what they want to do with agents and what they can securely and confidently run in production. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Consider:</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li>Only 7% of developers trust AI output for important work decisions. Nearly half (48%) trust it only when they can verify it themselves.</li> </ul> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li>Security and privacy ranked as the second biggest factor in whether teams adopt or expand AI tools (behind only accuracy). </li> </ul> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li>Nearly one in four developers said AI-related security or privacy issues cost their team valuable time in the past year.</li> </ul> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Put it all together, and the missing ingredient is clear: trust. Developers want to tap into everything agents make possible without crossing their fingers and hoping “everything” doesn’t include a catastrophic security failure.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">I didn’t point out those earlier Docker numbers just to brag (though, hey, I’m a marketer, so it was at least part of it). I mentioned them because they’re a reflection of trust — trust earned across more than a decade helping developers make software portable, open, reproducible, and secure. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Today, we’re building on that foundation and extending that trust into the agentic era. At WeAreDevelopers, you saw the first steps. We launched <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/introducing-cloud-sandboxes-start-on-your-laptop-finish-in-the-cloud/" id="dkr_docker-cloud-sandboxes-94981">Docker Cloud Sandboxes</a>, published the next-generation <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/docker-sandbox-kit-spec-cncf/" id="dkr_sandbox-kit-specification-94981">Sandbox Kit specification</a> (which we’ve committed to submitting to the Cloud Native Computing Foundation), and built nearly everything we showed around one idea: helping developers <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/manufacturing-trust-for-ai-agents-keynote/" id="dkr_manufacture-trust-for-ai-agents-94981">manufacture trust for AI agents</a>. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">If it all looked and felt like a slightly different Docker, that was intentional. We’re at the start of a transformative era for Docker, and you’ll see a lot more from us in the coming weeks and months.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">But no matter how much the industry changes, and no matter how much we evolve along with it, one thing won’t change. Docker will continue to run on trust.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">If you’d like to see what that looks like in practice, <a href="https://www.docker.com/products/docker-sandboxes/" id="dkr_try-docker-sandboxes-94981">try Docker Sandboxes</a> (and get a $250 credit if you sign up before Oct. 31). Is Your Organization Messing Up AI Adoption?

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p>The way I see most organizations roll out AI feels more like a farce than a strategy. If it were turned into a movie, reviewers would say it was unbelievable. Nobody could be that stupid.</p> <p>Yet here we are. And what makes it all the more frustrating is that <strong>we have been here before</strong>.</p> <p>Back in 2014, I wrote a book for Smashing Magazine called <a href="https://shop.smashingmagazine.com/printed-books/digital-adaptation/"><em>Digital Adaptation</em></a>, looking at the mistakes being made in how organizations were approaching digital transformation and what they should be doing instead. The similarities between then and now are striking.</p> <p>So in this post, I want to look back at <em>Digital Adaptation</em> and see how the lessons in that book apply to the AI era and what we can learn from the past to help us navigate the challenges of AI today.</p> <p>The biggest mistake, both then and now, is approaching each as a technological challenge.</p> It’s About People, Not Technology <p>Sure, both digital transformation and AI are technological advances, and yes, some level of technological implementation is required. But that is the tip of a very large iceberg.</p> <p>Back in the midst of the digital transformation era, IT departments were rolling out CMSs, CRM systems, intranets, and so on. Meanwhile, on the customer-facing side, organizations were rolling out websites, apps, and personalization. But they were putting almost no emphasis on <em>how</em> people were going to use these systems.</p> <p>Internal systems were hard to use, and customers either didn’t care what was being built or wanted something else entirely.</p> <p>The same is happening with AI. Senior management are buying into the hype, and IT teams are rolling out Copilot Studio licenses company-wide and considering the job done. Sure, there is a bit of organization-wide generic training, but that’s about it.</p> <p>Just like before, staff are left wondering what to do with these new systems and feeling under pressure to use them and deliver results.</p> <blockquote>Organizations need to recognize that AI is a <strong>cultural shift as much as a technology</strong>, and that shift has enormous implications for their staff.</blockquote> <p>After all, this time around, it is even worse, because this time, staff fear for their jobs. They have read the same headlines that you have, and no amount of promises from senior management is going to make them feel any better.</p> Why Would Staff Want To Adopt AI? <p>I mean, think about it from a staff member’s point of view. AI offers them very little personally, even setting aside the fact that management may be intending to use it to replace them.</p> <p>The main reason senior management are excited about AI is that it provides additional <strong>productivity</strong>. People can do more in less time. But that does not benefit staff. They are still required to work 9 to 5, but now they are expected to do more in the same time. They don’t get any time back, and they don’t get paid more, so why would they embrace AI?</p> <blockquote>So before asking staff to embrace AI, organizations need to ask themselves how they intend to <strong>motivate</strong> them to do so.</blockquote> <p>One strategy is to help staff identify the parts of their job they hate and how AI can help them do those tasks more quickly and less painfully. People tend to be a lot more receptive to change when it is presented as an <strong>opportunity to improve their working lives</strong>.</p> <p>But, that should just be the beginning. Organizations may need to consider providing incentives for staff to adopt AI in terms of career progression, compensation, or shorter working hours.</p> Staff Need Time And Support To Adopt AI <p>Also, adopting AI is not a simple task. It involves challenging every working practice they have built up over (in some cases) decades. All of that <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_knowledge">procedural knowledge</a> has to be rebuilt. That is a lot to ask of anybody in even the best of circumstances, and most employees do not find themselves in the best of circumstances.</p> <p>Most are overworked and struggling to stay on top of their to-do lists. They don’t have time to sit down, learn a new tool, and figure out how it fits into their workflow.</p> <p>That doesn’t just take time. It also takes a lot of <strong>consideration</strong> and <strong>experimentation</strong>. It is not enough to just send a member of staff on a training course and expect them to be able to use AI to its full potential.</p> <p>At a minimum, staff need training specific to their roles, such as the <a href="https://better-ux-with-ai.boagworld.com/">AI training I provide to UX teams</a>. But organizations should be going even further.</p> <p>They need to develop sophisticated training programs that help staff from different departments understand how to use AI in their specific roles. They need ongoing support and the time to experiment with AI to find the best ways to use it.</p> <p>But formal training will only take you so far. In my experience, the most effective support comes from <strong>colleagues</strong>, which is why I would start with the enthusiasts rather than the resisters. Every organization has a handful of people who are already experimenting with AI, often without anybody asking them to. Bring those people together, give them the time and space to share what is working for them, and let their enthusiasm spread to everyone else.</p> <p>I saw this work during the digital transformation era too. In <em>Digital Adaptation</em>, I described an internal conference where staff showed their colleagues what they had built, and it did more for adoption than any memo from the top ever could. People are far more likely to try something when they see a colleague in a similar role using it successfully than when they are told to by senior management.</p> <blockquote>Rather than trying to win over the most resistant staff first, organizations should find the people already experimenting with AI, give them time to share what they have learned, and let social proof do the heavy lifting.</blockquote> <p>Unfortunately, experimentation comes with a big downside… mistakes will be made.</p> It Needs To Be Safe To Fail <p>Many organizations have, to be frank, a culture of fear where mistakes are seen as a sign of incompetence. However, by its very nature, learning a new tool like AI will involve making mistakes, and this can be a massive barrier to adoption.</p> <p>I saw this during the era of digital transformation. Senior management would demand digital transformation and were confused when it didn’t happen.</p> <p>What they didn’t realize was that they were sending mixed messages to staff. Sure, they were asking for digital transformation, but they were still demanding that the same key performance indicators (KPIs) be met, and they still pointed out any mistakes in their annual reviews.</p> <p>There was <strong>no space to fail</strong>, as the pressure to deliver was relentless.</p> <p>This time around, it seems to be even worse. I hear horror stories of staff being reprimanded for using too many tokens just weeks after being told to adopt AI. Or staff being lectured so much about what they should or should not do with AI that they are afraid to try anything. It is safer to just do what they have always done.</p> <blockquote>If management want staff to adopt AI, then they need to make staff <strong>feel safe to experiment</strong>. They need to adjust KPIs, change how staff are reviewed, and relax some of the governance while staff are learning.</blockquote> Handle Governance With Care <p>Look, I get it. AI cannot be rolled out without some governance in place, but again, this is an area that organizations have typically handled poorly.</p> <p>In the midst of digital transformation, governance would either be non-existent, or it would be so strict that it would stifle innovation, and unfortunately, I see the same pattern emerging with AI adoption.</p> <p>Staff need some governance to help them know where they stand. It helps them understand what is expected of them and what they can and cannot do.</p> <p>But too often organizations have been so strict that it is easier not to do anything at all. Pages of instructions about what people should and should not share with AI create that sense of fear that I mentioned earlier.</p> <blockquote>Organizations need a short set of principles that would fit on a handful of posters, similar to <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles">gov.uk’s design principles</a>. Beyond that, they need to demonstrate a degree of trust in their staff to use common sense.</blockquote> <p><img src="https://files.smashing.media/articles/is-your-organization-messing-up-ai-adoption/design-principle-tumblr.jpg" /></p> <p>Of course, mistakes will get made, but that is the cost of change, and so organizations need to accept that and do what they can to mitigate that in how they roll out the technology.</p> Adaptive Technological Rollout <p>That said, organizations will need to be careful in how they roll out AI from an IT perspective. Once again, it is easy to lock everything down and stifle the adoption of AI.</p> <p>Again, this is something I highlighted back in 2014 with digital transformation. Overstretched IT teams would pick a set of technologies and roll them out organization-wide, often with little or no consultation with the individuals who would be using them. Anything outside of that set of technologies had to go through a complex approval process, normally involving a steering committee. The result was a glacial pace of change that was one of the major contributing factors to the failure rate of digital transformation projects.</p> <p>Today we are seeing the same thing. IT teams are opting into a tool like Copilot and calling it a day, but that often falls massively short.</p> <p>Take, for example, a UX team I am working with at the moment. Management is pushing hard for AI adoption, and the team is excited about the potential of creating an AI-powered research repository and even AI personas. It sounds like a wonderful case study in AI adoption, with enthusiastic management, an excited team, and innovative ideas.</p> <p>Unfortunately, the reality is that nothing is happening because Copilot cannot deliver on the team’s vision, and so they are currently bogged down in bureaucracy as they try to persuade the AI steering group that they need to use a different toolset. Understandably, the team’s enthusiasm is waning. Why bother?</p> <p>And that team is one of the more patient ones. In many organizations, staff are not waiting for the steering group at all. Just as we saw shadow IT during the digital transformation era, with people signing up for Dropbox and Google Docs because the official tools could not do what they needed, we are now seeing shadow AI. When the in-house AI tool will not let people do their jobs, they open a personal ChatGPT or Claude account on their phone or home laptop and get on with it.</p> <p>That is far more dangerous than the problem the restrictions were meant to prevent. Company data ends up in tools the organization has no control over, with no oversight and no record of what has been shared. In trying to lock everything down, organizations end up pushing the risk somewhere they cannot see it.</p> <p>The instinctive reaction is to clamp down with stricter policies, blocked websites, and warnings about disciplinary action. But that rarely works, and it drives the behavior further underground. <strong>Shadow AI is a symptom</strong>, and a useful one, because it shows exactly where the official tools are falling short.</p> <blockquote>Instead of trying to stamp out shadow AI, organizations should learn from it. Talk to staff about what they are using personal accounts for and what they feel they cannot do with the AI they have been given, then use that to shape what gets rolled out next.</blockquote> <p>Look, I understand the cost of a messy, out-of-control tech stack. It is not ideal. However, in the early days of adoption, we may have to live with a bit of chaos while people explore the edges of what is possible and work out common use cases.</p> <p>Organizations need to allow staff to explore different uses of AI and even different AI apps to find what suits their specific needs in their specific roles. They can always start locking things down later as the real organizational needs become clearer.</p> A Dangerous Time <p>I admit all of this feels dangerous to some organizations. They want to control the AI adoption process centrally and make sure nothing goes wrong. I understand that. But change comes with a degree of risk, and the risk of moving too slowly is greater than the risk of moving too fast.</p> <p>However, a bigger danger still is when organizations see this as a technological challenge and overlook the human side of things. AI adoption is not simply about rolling out new technology. Just like digital transformation, it is about <strong>people</strong>, <strong>culture</strong>, and <strong>change management</strong>. The sooner organizations realize the scope of what they are dealing with, the better.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For most of my career, building software started with the same setup: keyboard, mouse, editor, terminal, browser, and an impressive number of tabs I was definitely going to look at later. <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Working with outside collaborators can add more work for a repository owner. You need to confirm who still needs access, change permissions as projects evolve, and archive repositories once they&rsquo;ve served their purpose.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">To keep up, repository owners need to track collaborators, deadlines, and follow-ups for each repository. As a project grows, that manual housekeeping becomes harder to keep up with and easier to defer.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">We faced this problem in a shared organization where GitHub employees (&ldquo;Hubbers&rdquo;) collaborate with customers and partners on product trials and professional services engagements. We revoke it. The safe outcome is the <em>default</em> outcome.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Never destroy anything.</strong> Evergreen will <strong>never</strong> delete a repository or its contents. Archived repos are recoverable, and every access change is written into the repo&rsquo;s tracking issue, so reverting a mistake is as easy as a copy-paste to Copilot.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-how-it-works-one-brain-many-hands">How it works: One brain, many hands</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Under the hood, Evergreen is a small Go webhook server running in a container. If someone who <em>isn&rsquo;t</em> a Hubber closes an issue, the app quietly reopens it and posts a note explaining why. Decisions only count when they come from the people authorized to make them.</p> <h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-informing-the-right-people">Informing the right people</h3> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here&rsquo;s a subtle problem: we know the people who need to respond to a review are Hubbers, but we didn&rsquo;t always know <em>which</em> Hubber(s) owned a given repository.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Evergreen resolves the right people to notify through a fallback chain:</p> <ol class="wp-block-list"> <li>If a repo sets an <code>evergreen_repo_owner</code> custom property (a <code>@user</code> or <code>@org/team</code>), use it.</li> <li>Otherwise, look at recent issue and commit authors and cc the ones who are on the access review team, i.e., the Hubbers actually working in that repo.</li> </ol> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The result: the right owners get pinged automatically, without anyone maintaining a spreadsheet of &ldquo;who owns what.&rdquo;</p> <h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-fail-secure-and-fail-closed">Fail secure, and fail <em>closed</em></h3> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">When a review deadline passes with no response, cleanup revokes every collaborator in the snapshot. We were careful about a subtle edge case: what if some removals succeed while others hit a transient API error? Marking the review &ldquo;done&rdquo; while a removal is still outstanding would be a quiet inconsistency, and we didn&rsquo;t want that.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">So Evergreen fails <strong>closed</strong>. If any removal fails, the review stays pending and the issue stays open, and the next run retries the stragglers. It only records &ldquo;revoked&rdquo; once every removal actually succeeded. And whatever gets removed is written into the issue, so restoring access is a matter of re&#8209;adding the handles it listed.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-user-experience-automation-that-feels-considerate">The user experience: automation that feels considerate</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Automated housekeeping has a reputation for being the office smoke alarm: loud, easy to ignore, and usually going off while you&rsquo;re cooking something perfectly fine. We worked hard to make Evergreen feel like the opposite:</p> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li><strong>It comes to you.</strong> The work happens as an issue in <em>your</em> repo.</li> <li><strong>It tags the right people.</strong> No &ldquo;who owns this?&rdquo; archaeology.</li> <li><strong>It&rsquo;s crystal clear about the ask.</strong> Every issue states what to do, the exact deadline, and precisely what happens if you ignore it.</li> <li><strong>It nudges before it acts.</strong> A reminder comment lands before the deadline: no surprise revocations.</li> <li><strong>It has an escape hatch.</strong>&nbsp;Org owners (Hubbers) can opt out by setting a custom property (<code>evergreen_exempt_from_access_review</code> or <code>evergreen_exempt_from_archive</code>).</li> </ul> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-rolling-it-out-carefully">Rolling it out carefully</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Automation that manages access and archives repositories deserves a careful rollout, especially into a large, active org (ours has 1,400+ Hubbers, and many more customers and partners). We wanted the very first run to be a complete non&#8209;event. A few practices got us there:</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Dry&#8209;run first.</strong> Evergreen ships with a <code>DRY_RUN</code> mode that logs every write it <em>would</em> make without touching a single collaborator or repo. We ran it against the real org and read the logs like tea leaves until we trusted the decisions it wanted to make.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Overcommunicate.</strong> Before flipping the switch, we broadcast what was coming, when, and what people needed to do, so the first issue anyone saw wasn&rsquo;t a surprise. We found that when you&rsquo;re telling a large group the same thing for the seventh time, it&rsquo;s often when many are still hearing it for the first time.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Watch everything with Splunk.</strong> Evergreen emits structured <code>logfmt</code> logs to a dedicated Splunk index. That observability is how we caught edge cases in the wild (empty collaborator lists, unusual permissions, repos with issues disabled) and tightened the logic before they mattered.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Respect the platform&rsquo;s speed limits.</strong> When you open issues and post comments across <em>every</em> repo in a big org, you slam into GitHub&rsquo;s <strong>secondary rate limits for content creation</strong>: 80 requests/minute and 500/hour. While this is a thoughtful preventative control to avoid system abuse, it requires a bit of intention when automating IssueOps at large scale.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">So Evergreen paces itself <em>by construction</em>. It processes repos in batches, spaces out each content&#8209;creating call by a couple of seconds, and sleeps between batches to let the rolling window clear, and it only counts the repos that actually create content, so a quiet quarter where nothing&rsquo;s eligible still runs fast. We opted for proactive avoidance over reactive back&#8209;off.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-lessons-we-re-taking-with-us">Lessons we&rsquo;re taking with us</h2> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li><strong>Make the safe path the default path.</strong> Fail secure, then make responding easy enough that failing secure rarely triggers.</li> <li><strong>Use the primitives you already have.</strong> Issues, webhooks, custom properties, and git history did the work of a bespoke UI, a job queue, and an audit database.</li> <li><strong>Meet people where they work.</strong> An issue in your repo beats a dashboard you&rsquo;ll never open.</li> <li><strong>Logs are a revert button.</strong> Writing every change into the issue turned &ldquo;oops&rdquo; into &ldquo;paste these three handles into a Copilot session.&rdquo;</li> <li><strong>Respect the limits.</strong> At org scale, rate limits aren&rsquo;t an edge case: they&rsquo;re the design constraint.</li> </ul> <h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-keep-your-org-evergreen">Keep your org evergreen</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Routine hygiene is the kind of work that&rsquo;s easy to defer and perfect to automate. The trick isn&rsquo;t heroic cleanup sprints: it&rsquo;s making the boring, safe thing happen on a schedule, with a friendly face and an undo button.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">That&rsquo;s Evergreen. And because it&rsquo;s built entirely from native GitHub features, you can stand up the same pattern yourself.</p> <div class="wp-block-group post-content-cta has-global-padding is-layout-constrained wp-block-group-is-layout-constrained"> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://docs.github.com/apps/creating-github-apps">Ready to make repository hygiene routine?</a> Start with one recurring access or archival task, then use GitHub Apps, actions, issues, and custom properties to automate the problem away.</p> </div> <p>The post <a href="https://github.blog/engineering/platform-security/staying-evergreen-how-we-automated-secure-access-and-repo-hygiene-in-a-shared-org/">Staying Evergreen: How we automated secure access and repo hygiene in a shared org</a> appeared first on <a href="https://github.blog">The GitHub Blog</a>.</p>

AI Agent Security Risks: What Can Go Wrong When Agents Act on Their Own

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The thing that makes an <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/what-are-ai-agents/" id="dkr_ai-agent-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">AI agent</a> useful, its freedom to act, is also what can introduce risk. An agent doesn&#8217;t just answer a question, it browses, runs code, and calls tools on its own, so a single bad or manipulated instruction can turn into real damage instead of just a wrong answer. That’s the shift traditional application security wasn’t built for, and it’s why <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/manufacturing-trust-for-ai-agents-keynote/" id="dkr_trust-has-to-be-built-into-the-system-around-the-agent-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">trust has to be built into the system around the agent</a>, not assumed.</p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">An agent decides its own path, picking tools at runtime, chaining actions together, and usually running with the credentials of whoever launched it. That autonomy is the source of most agent-specific risk, and it points to the two-part answer that runs through this post: contain what an agent can reach, and oversee what it does.</p> <div class="wp-block-ponyo-zeta organism toc-exclude"> <blockquote class="container"> <h2 class="wp-block-ponyo-heading text-lg"> Key takeaways </h2> <ul class="wp-block-list"> <li>AI agents introduce risks traditional app security wasn&#8217;t built for: they choose tools, chain actions, and often run with your credentials.</li> <li>The main risks are prompt injection, tool misuse and poisoning, excessive autonomy, credential exposure, and data exfiltration.</li> <li>Most trace back to one thing: the agent can act, so containment and oversight matter more than the model.</li> <li>The two-part answer is isolation to limit blast radius and governance to oversee behavior.</li> </ul> </blockquote> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why agents break traditional security models</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">If you&#8217;ve secured web services, the model is familiar: requests arrive at known endpoints, deterministic logic handles them, and you design controls around that predictability. Agents break that assumption. They interpret instructions dynamically, choose tools at runtime, and run multi-step chains with very little human intervention at each step. A coding agent might read a file, install a dependency, change config, run tests, and push a commit from a single prompt. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Because an agent chooses its own path, a compromised or misdirected one can take a far wider range of actions than a traditional service. Every risk below has already produced <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/ai-coding-agent-horror-stories-security-risks/" id="dkr_documented-real-world-incidents-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">documented real-world incidents</a>, and they all follow from that shift.</p> <div class="wp-block-ponyo-image"> <img alt="Diagram of the top AI agent security risks including prompt injection, data exfiltration, tool misuse and poisoning, excessive autonomy, and credential and secrets exposure." class="fade-in" height="1218" src="https://www.docker.com/app/uploads/2026/10/docker_AI-Agent-Security-Risks-2320x1218.jpg" title="- docker AI Agent Security Risks" width="2320" /> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">1. Prompt injection</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Prompt injection is the risk that defines agent security. Because an agent acts on whatever text it reads, an attacker doesn&#8217;t need access to your systems. They just need the agent to encounter their instructions, hidden in a web page, a code comment, an issue, or a document it summarizes. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">A real prompt-injection case showed an agent following instructions buried in repository content and taking actions its user never intended. </p> <div class="wp-block-ponyo-table"> <table class="responsive-table"> <tbody class="wp-block-ponyo-table-body"> <tr class="wp-block-ponyo-table-row"> <td class="wp-block-ponyo-cell"> <span class="responsive-table-label"></span> <span class="responsive-table-value"> <span class="responsive-table-value-content"> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Incident highlight:</strong> A coding agent followed instructions <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/mcp-horror-stories-github-prompt-injection/" id="dkr_buried-in-a-repositorys-own-content-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">buried in a repository&#8217;s own content</a> and took actions its user never intended. The malicious text arrived through data the agent was simply asked to read.</p> </span> </span> </td> </tr> </tbody> </table> </div> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The agent has no reliable way to tell a legitimate instruction from an injected one, so the defense is to limit what a hijacked agent can reach.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">2. Tool misuse and tool poisoning</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">Agents act through tools: API connectors, database queries, file operations, and code execution. Every tool is an access vector, and two things could go wrong. </p> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">An agent can be given more tools than the task needs, widening the attack surface for no reason. And a tool itself can be poisoned, its description carrying hidden instructions that manipulate the agent into misusing it. The mitigations are to scope tools to the task at runtime, source them from <a href="https://www.docker.com/products/mcp-catalog-and-toolkit/" id="dkr_registries-with-verified-provenance-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">registries with verified provenance</a>, and review tool descriptions rather than trusting them by default.</p> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">3. Excessive autonomy</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">The more an agent can do without review, the more a single bad decision costs. Excessive autonomy is the risk of granting an agent broad, standing permission to act, most visibly when developers run <a href="https://www.docker.com/blog/what-is-yolo-mode/" id="dkr_yolo-mode-94888" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">YOLO mode</a>, where every action is auto-approved. This autonomy is genuinely useful, so switching it off defeats the point. The better move is to run the agent where that autonomy can&#8217;t reach anything it shouldn&#8217;t, so speed doesn’t necessarily mean increased blast radius.</p> <div class="wp-block-ponyo-table"> <table class="responsive-table"> <tbody class="wp-block-ponyo-table-body"> <tr class="wp-block-ponyo-table-row"> <td class="wp-block-ponyo-cell"> <span class="responsive-table-label"></span> <span class="responsive-table-value"> <span class="responsive-table-value-content"> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Incident highlight:</strong> A developer asked a coding agent to clean up an old repository. Nothing in the setup gave a human a chance to catch it before it executed.</p> </span> </span> </td> </tr> </tbody> </table> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">4. Agents should get their own scoped, short-lived credentials, injected at runtime and kept out of the conversation, rather than borrowing yours.</p> <div class="wp-block-ponyo-table"> <table class="responsive-table"> <tbody class="wp-block-ponyo-table-body"> <tr class="wp-block-ponyo-table-row"> <td class="wp-block-ponyo-cell"> <span class="responsive-table-label"></span> <span class="responsive-table-value"> <span class="responsive-table-value-content"> <p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Incident highlight:</strong> An AWS engineer handed a small Cost Explorer bug to a coding agent running with their own operator-level access. The result was a thirteen-hour regional outage.</p> </span> </span> </td> </tr> </tbody> </table> </div> <h2 class="wp-block-heading">5. Data exfiltration and untrusted handoffs</h2> <p class="wp-block-paragraph">An agent with network access and sensitive information in its context can send that data somewhere it shouldn&#8217;t, whether through a compromised tool or an injected instruction.
