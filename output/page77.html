The Commodity Futures Trading Commission has told prediction-market operators Novig and ProphetX not to list a set of sports event contracts the two companies had already self-certified, according to Front Office Sports. The agency also asked both firms to explain how the markets would comply with federal commodities law.

A CFTC spokesperson confirmed that letters had been sent to both companies. “Any derivatives contract offered by a CFTC-regulated exchange must be compliant with the Commodity Exchange Act,” the spokesperson said.

Micro contracts and scorigami

The two letters target different products. Novig was told that its micro contracts, such as a market on which player opens the scoring in a basketball game, are open to manipulation. ProphetX was told that its scorigami contracts, which settle on whether a game finishes with a scoreline that has never occurred in that sport, do not meet the Commodity Exchange Act’s definition of a swap.

Sources told Front Office Sports that the letters describe the filings as false self-certifications, the term used when an exchange certifies a contract that may not be permitted under the statute. Neither set of contracts had been listed, so no customer has traded on them.

ProphetX framed the request more narrowly. A company spokesperson said CFTC staff “requested that we voluntarily defer listing the Scorigami contracts pending further review, not that we withdraw them.” The company said it complied, has since supplied further analysis on the contracts’ compliance and economic utility, and remains in contact with agency staff. Novig did not immediately respond to a request for comment, the outlet reported.

A signal to the wider market

Under the current framework, exchanges can self-certify and list new contracts without prior approval, and the CFTC can step in afterward. The agency would not say if it has ever before told an operator to hold back a self-certified sports contract. One source described the letters as “kind of a warning shot” for the sector.

The move does not appear to be limited to two companies. Senior CFTC officials have also spoken with DraftKings and FanDuel and cautioned that listing micro contracts could bring the same response, according to the report. Other exchanges have voiced frustration with the agency’s stance, pointing out that many state regulators allow comparable in-game wagers at licensed sportsbooks.

The letters follow a separate round of CFTC inquiries last month into incentive programs at Kalshi, Polymarket, Rothera and Novig. They also come ahead of a final rule the agency is expected to issue after its June proposal, a 267-page notice that would restrict in-game props and limit markets on high school sports. The proposal sits inside a wider dispute over how sports contracts are treated under federal rules, with several states arguing those products should fall under gambling law.

Leagues have pushed for limits

One source said the Novig and ProphetX markets were exactly the type of event contracts professional leagues have objected to most. On October 19, the NBA tightened the rules on player props and micro bets for its sportsbook partners, among them markets on who scores a game’s first basket, Yogonet reported. Those league rules cover traditional sportsbooks, not prediction markets.

It is not clear what the CFTC would do if either company went ahead and listed the contracts. Both firms have the chance to make their case to the agency, and the outcome is likely to shape how far other exchanges push into single-play sports markets before the final rule lands. Sports already account for most of the activity on the largest platforms, as shown in recent data on NFL trading volume across prediction markets.

From Chip Lab to Center Stage

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


Many engineers are more comfortable working behind the scenes developing technology than speaking to consumers about products. But Preethi Ashwath has learned to take center stage, talking about the “hearable” and wearable devices she has had a hand in developing as an analog integrated circuit designer in the consumer electronics division at Analog Devices of Wilmington, Mass.

The IEEE member is one of the few women who work in the specialized field, where about 11 percent are female, according to career platform Zippia.

Ashwath stepped out from behind the scenes last year to meet with attendees at the Consumer Electronics Show (CES) in Las Vegas. She demonstrated Analog’s new earbuds, which track people’s heart and lung health through vibrations in their ear canals.

After she demonstrated her ability to explain the technology to consumers in simple terms, Analog selected her to represent the company at other events. She says she has “come to see speaking as part of contributing to the field, rather than something separate from technical work.”

She also gives technical talks on various aspects of electrostatic discharge (ESD) and other topics, as well as participates in panel discussions, she says, because they “reach engineers well beyond my immediate team.”

Ashwath is an active volunteer with the IEEE Circuits and Systems Society.

“Whether through designing semiconductor products, publishing technical articles, reviewing conference papers, or speaking at industry events, I try to contribute to the growth of the engineering community and the advancement of the field,” she says.

Discovering circuit design at Intel

Ashwath, who grew up in Bengaluru, India, learned about electronics engineering from her high school physics tutor.

That introduction led her to take electronics as one of her core courses in her final years there. At the time, Ashwath’s older sister was pursuing an engineering degree.

“Between my interest in electronics and seeing her already on that path, it became clear to me that engineering was the way to go,” Ashwath says.

After earning a bachelor’s degree in electronics and communications engineering at the SJB Institute of Technology, in Bengaluru, she wanted to pursue a career in very-large-scale integration. But that changed once she moved to the United States and began her master’s program in electrical engineering at the University of California, Irvine.

She discovered her passion for circuits and devices during an internship at Intel in Santa Clara, Calif. She learned about circuit design, package design, and reliability verification tools such as Ansys Totem.

“Seeing every layer of the chip development process gave me a more complete picture of what goes into building a semiconductor product,” she says. “Through that experience, I realized I felt most connected to design.

“It was the hard work and creativity that drew me in. You start with a specification, build a circuit, simulate it, then wait for the chip to come back from fabrication. When your design works, it’s a real feeling of satisfaction. It teaches you patience and perseverance.”

After attaining her master’s degree, she was hired by Intel as an analog design engineer. She gained experience in mixed-signal circuit design before joining Analog. In her current role, she works on consumer hearables such as earbuds and wearables including fitness trackers and health monitors.

Her main focus is designing ESD pad rings, the borders of specialized circuits built around a microchip’s outermost edge to protect it from the discharge, which can damage circuit paths or introduce latent defects that lead to failures later in the chip’s life cycle.

“On a chip with multiple voltage domains, you have to plan how the protection structures are placed and connected across the whole pad ring—and get it right,” Ashwath says. “A mistake there could cause a chip to fail in the field in ways that are very hard to debug afterward.

“It’s detailed work that requires understanding both the device physics and how the whole chip fits together.”

Part of the job is designing and creating an integrated circuit’s building blocks, including ESD protection structures and other mixed-signal blocks. Ashwath conducts rigorous checks to verify they work as intended before being integrated into a chip.

She collaborates with ESD advisors, layout designers, and the digital team that designs the chip’s digital logic.

“I work with them to make sure my analog circuits and their digital blocks work together correctly,” she says. “No two days look exactly the same.”

In 2024 she was recognized with the company’s Impact Award for her “exceptional work on a critical project with significant cross-functional business impact.”

Presenting the final product

Ashwath was selected to represent Analog at events after a senior leader encouraged her to ask for high-visibility opportunities, she says.

“Most of my time is spent on designing circuits and simulations,” she says. “At CES, I stood in front of customers, engineers, and members of the press, explaining our technology and answering questions on the spot.”

The earbuds she demonstrated monitor minute vibrations from a user’s heart and lungs, according to the Analog website. The vibrations can detect arrhythmia, a leaky heart valve, or fluid in the lungs.

“Hearables are evolving from playing back audio to becoming health-monitoring platforms,” she says. “The ear turns out to be a good location for picking up physiological signals, so these devices can capture vital signs while someone is simply wearing them normally.

“The hard part is achieving high signal quality. The signals you’re trying to detect are very small and buried in the noise from the person’s movements and the surrounding environment.

“That’s where analog front-end design becomes critical. Extracting a meaningful signal from a noisy real-world environment is fundamentally an analog problem.”

Being face-to-face with customers at the conference was new to her.

“What struck me most was seeing how people react to the technology,” she says. “When you’re designing a block, you’re focused on specifications and margins. At CES, after explaining what the product does, I had a better understanding of why it matters to them.

“That connection between the circuit work and the final product is something you don’t always see from the design bench.”

Since the CES, Ashwath has presented other technologies she’s worked on. At the annual National Association of Music Merchants show in Anaheim, Calif., she demonstrated the A2B Automotive Audio Bus to the audio industry. The bus uses a low-cost unshielded twisted-pair cable to carry multichannel digital audio, control data, synchronized clock signals, and power in fewer than 50 microseconds, according to Analog.

“IEEE connects you with academia, which you don’t get much exposure to once you’re in industry. That connection between industry and academic work is valuable in both directions.”

At the company’s Global Technology Conference—an internal event held in Boston that draws engineers from the company’s more than 50 sites around the world—Ashwath led a demonstration of its solutions for augmented reality smart glasses.

“Each of these events has a different audience,” she says. “Learning to explain my technical work to different people has become a skill I’ve acquired.”

The most rewarding part of her job, she says, is seeing the end result of her work.

“You spend months on a design, and there’s a long wait between finishing it and finding out whether your instincts were right,” she says. “When the measurements match what you simulated, that’s the most satisfying moment in this job.

“The other part is knowing my work ends up in a real product. The circuits I design go into devices people actually use every day.”

Giving back to the community

Like many engineers, Ashwath joined IEEE to access research published in the IEEE Xplore Digital Library. It keeps her current in a field that keeps moving, she says.

During her first year of membership, in 2021, she discovered other perks including networking and educational opportunities.

“IEEE connects you to a community outside your own company,” she says. “Attending lectures and technical talks exposes you to approaches and problems you wouldn’t encounter in your own work.”

She eventually began seeking out volunteer opportunities, specifically with IEEE conferences and journals, because they are “how you stay current with what’s happening in circuits and systems beyond your own day-to-day work,” she says.

“As an analog designer,” she says, “being connected to that broader community matters. The field moves, and you need a way to keep learning from it.”

She serves as a peer reviewer of papers for several IEEE conferences including the IEEE International Symposium on Circuits and Systems and the IEEE International Midwest Symposium on Circuits and Systems. ISCAS is the flagship conference of the IEEE Circuits and Systems Society. MWSCAS is the oldest U.S. conference on the technologies.

“Someone has to assess whether submitted work is technically sound and worth presenting,” she says, “and that only happens if practicing engineers are willing to put in the time.”

The combination of learning from the community and contributing to it is what makes her membership worthwhile, she says.

“Analog design is a field where you’re always learning. Just when you feel you understand one architecture, there’s another one to learn,” she says. “Being an active learner isn’t optional in this field; it’s the requirement, and being part of the IEEE community helps with that.

“IEEE also connects you with academia, which you don’t get much exposure to once you’re in industry.

“Reviewing papers means reading what researchers are working on, which is often quite different from how the same problems get approached in a product environment. That connection between industry and academic work is valuable in both directions.”

PHP2Shell and eMudhra Patched

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Two security events landed side by side this week, PHP2Shell and the eMudhra certificate compromise. Both reached the same place, the foundation their applications are built on.

PHP2Shell is a pair of critical flaws in the PHP engine itself, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619. They let an unauthenticated request become code execution on the server, on any PHP application, however careful its own code is, and PHP runs about 69% of the websites whose server-side language is known.

The eMudhra compromise is a different kind of problem. Eight intermediate certificates from the certificate authority eMudhra turned out to have private keys that can be computed from the public certificates, which let anyone issue a certificate for any website and have it accepted. Browsers revoke certificates like these on their own, but a container image carries its own trust store and keeps trusting them until it is rebuilt.

Docker Hardened Images published a fixed PHP image within 24 hours

The dhi.io/php image was rebuilt on the patched PHP and published early on October 22, within 24 hours of the upstream release and after the DHI testing and verification steps. Pulling the current tag puts you on the fixed version. The few DHI images that bundle their own PHP application, such as composer and nextcloud, follow as their upstream projects release.

Rebuilt across the catalog, verifiable tag by tag

The certificate compromise touched the whole catalog, because every image carries a trust store. In the 48 hours after the PHP release, the DHI build system ran 16,567 package and image builds across the catalog. By 10pm UTC on October 22, almost all catalog tags were on the fixed CA store, with most of the rest in the build queue. That percentage comes from the SBOM that ships with every image, read for every published tag to check which CA store and which PHP version it carries, and you can run the same check on anything you pull.

How to make sure your environments are covered

If you run PHP on Docker Hardened Images, pull the current tag for your branch and redeploy, and rebuild anything you built on top of a DHI image so it picks up the new base docker pull dhi.io/php:8.4

Customizations built through the DHI Enterprise workflow are rebuilt automatically when the base layer is patched. For the certificate fix the same applies to every image you build from a DHI base, PHP or not. The Docker Security update from October 22 lists the affected PHP versions and the support path.

If you run PHP in any other container, check the PHP version running inside it rather than the tag on the outside, and rebuild it on a patched base.

Build from a trusted foundation

Weeks like this one will keep coming, but a trusted foundation will make all the difference.

If you are not building on hardened images yet, this week is the wake-up call and the week to start. Both events landed on the foundation, and that is where the fix has to happen, in the image itself, rebuilt from fixed sources before anyone pulls it.

This is exactly what Docker Hardened Images were built for. Docker builds, patches and maintains the system packages inside each image from source, and the build system is event-driven, so an upstream release or a changed package triggers rebuilds of everything that depends on it. Each image ships signed, with its SBOM, SLSA Build Level 3 provenance and VEX data attached, which is why a rebuild of this size can be measured as well as done.

The catalog is free and open source under Apache 2.0, with near-zero CVEs on every image. It stays compatible with the Alpine and Debian your teams already run, so adoption is a one-line change to the FROM line in your Dockerfile, with no migration project attached. Accordion Icons: Which Work Best?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

So what icon is the most reliable to show expansion and collapsing for accordions? Is there really any noticeable difference between carets, chevrons, arrows, and triangles?

Well, research shows that there is some difference indeed. But what’s more interesting is that split menus are harmful, why it's better to not have icons and text labels link to different actions, and why the caret is the safest icon choice. Let’s dive in!

No Icon = Opening The Page

The findings aren’t very surprising: without any icon in use, users expect that tapping on the menu item would take them directly to a new page. Users often struggle with custom icons as they don't clearly signal that they'll open an accordion. And people tend to tap fairly equally on the icon and on the label for most icons.

Many designs (like the one below) tend to slightly overcomplicate things by adding arrows and carets pointing everywhere, depending on the state of the accordion. But typically we don't need to use an arrow to indicate a link to the category.

The rule of thumb is very simple: if it’s a link, the easiest and most effective way is to simply underline it — without any arrow, caret, or other icon. Perhaps it’s not particularly exciting, but it works flawlessly every single time.

Confusing Behavior With Arrows ▶ and Split Buttons

Using a right-facing arrow icon ▶ isn’t associated with an expectation of going directly to a new page. And having the same icon pointing down ▼ doesn’t necessarily imply that an accordion is going to open on tap/click.

It’s a good idea to avoid split buttons for accordions — where the text label links directly to a page, but the icon opens the accordion. Instead, it’s much more reliable to allow users to tap on a main category and expose the menu; and to access the category page, we add a link to it inside the menu.

Plus +/− and Arrows ↓/↑ Are Understood, But Can Be Misunderstood

For accordions, we typically use +/− or arrows ↓/↑ to signal expanded and collapsed states, and typically it works well. However, both have many different interpretations. Plus + can mean anything from saving for later to adding to cart to zooming in, and can be particularly confusing for multi-level navigation, with many levels open at the same time.

Arrows ↓/↑ can mean scrolling or sliding or finding the next match, but also a jump to a particular portion of a page. Both plus and arrows are well-understood and work, but depending on the design, can be confusing — mostly because they are overloaded.

Wrapping Up

The safe choice is to use the caret/triangle pointing down ▼ by default, and change it to a caret pointing up or a caret pointing sideways ▶ on interaction. If you need an alternative, then +/- typically work well, too.

But we certainly don’t need any further icons or arrows to indicate where the links go. The same goes for split menus, which often produce more mistakes than help. Instead, we have one button or dropdown that does exactly one thing — shows all options and allows users to drill down.

And if you need a direct link, we link the title directly to the page and underline it. More often than not, simpler is better.

AWS Continuum for code vulnerabilities is now generally available

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Today, we’re announcing the general availability of AWS Continuum for code vulnerabilities. AWS Continuum is a security service that finds and validates code vulnerabilities, then delivers fixes as patches you can automate or as pull requests in your repositories. AWS Continuum for code vulnerabilities confirms which findings are exploitable in your environment, ranks the resulting issues by business impact, and proposes a fix for each one. Every remediation starts in a mode where Continuum proposes the fix and a person approves it before it ships. In the future, you will be able to decide which actions Continuum can take on its own, within the guardrails you define.

Using frontier artificial intelligence (AI) models, threat actors can turn a vulnerability disclosure into working exploit code in minutes, and the same tools that speed up development also increase the volume of findings your teams must process. If your organization runs a large application estate, your scanners likely produce thousands of findings a day, and many of them need no action. A finding can be unreachable, unexploitable, or already mitigated by a runtime control. Generic severity scores don’t tell you which resources a threat actor can actually reach, so a critical score on a dev-only utility looks the same as a medium score on a service one step away from a payments database. Previously, confirming whether a finding was exploitable took hours of manual investigation, and shipping a fix with confidence took days of coordination across security, infrastructure, and application teams, because the blast radius of a change is hard to assess without business context. To close that gap, Continuum proves which findings are exploitable and bounds the risk of each fix it proposes.

Continuum builds a context graph from two primary sources: your code repositories and your AWS environment. For AWS, you set up a collector in the account and Region where your code runs. The collector gathers resource details such as AWS Config resources and network configuration so Continuum can judge whether a vulnerability is reachable over the network and what a threat actor could reach from it. From this graph, Continuum learns which systems are business-critical, who owns them, how services depend on one another, and which data each one touches. It uses that picture to identify your highest-value systems, such as customer data stores, payment pipelines, and authentication services, and then ranks each resulting issue by how close its attack path runs to those systems.

Continuum analyzes each finding against your code and its environment context and assigns an evidence level, such as a verdict proven by static analysis of the vulnerable code path. For findings it can reproduce, Continuum runs the exploit in an isolated sandbox and attaches proof-of-concept code with the reproduction steps. It produces the attack path, the supporting source analysis, and a blast radius assessment instead of a risk score alone. It also evaluates whether your existing defenses would detect and stop the validated path.

For each confirmed issue, Continuum generates a fix (a code patch, a dependency upgrade, or a configuration change) and opens it as a pull request in your connected repository, ready to review. Every fix requires human approval: you review and merge the pull request, so the final decision stays with your team. In the future, you will be able to build confidence in specific remediation classes and let Continuum apply those fixes automatically once its confidence gates pass, such as test coverage, scope bounds, and historical success rate.

AWS Continuum for code vulnerabilities in action
To show how this works, I open AWS Continuum in the console and go to Code vulnerabilities. Before I can review issues, I set up an environment. I connect a code source, either a GitHub integration or an Amazon S3 bucket, and then create an environment that scans it on a schedule I choose. An Amazon S3 source has a couple of prerequisites: the bucket sits in the same account and AWS Region where I turn on AWS Continuum, and I grant access through an AWS Identity and Access Management (IAM) role that AWS Continuum assumes. I can optionally add AWS infrastructure context by setting up an AWS Continuum collector in the account and Region where my code runs and authorizing my environment to use it. For the full requirements and steps, see the AWS Continuum User Guide.

The Overview page brings these pieces together in one place, showing my Code integrations, the Environments built on them, and the status of any AWS collector that supplies AWS resource context.

I open one of my environments. The Issues tab lists validated, prioritized issues rather than raw scanner output, each with a severity, a status, and the time it was created. I choose one of the issues.

The Overview rates it High severity, with the status Completed and the remediation already Delivered to GitHub. The Risk assessment explains why. Continuum rates its Exploitability as Exploitable, with an evidence level of Static proven and a validation run status of Completed. Likelihood is High, since the service is reachable from an attacker, requires no authentication, and needs no user interaction. Impact is Moderate, which means an attacker gains the ability to modify business data, with a blast radius reaching other users in the same tenant. A Proof section includes the source analysis behind the verdict.

The Remediation section shows a fix already delivered as a pull request in GitHub, changing four files, with 50 lines added and 24 removed across the cart service. Continuum opens the fix as a pull request titled Continuum fix, and I choose View pull request to open it in GitHub. The console is explicit that a generated patch does not mean the issue is fixed. I still review and merge it, which keeps the final decision with my team. A Contributing findings section lists the specific scanner finding this issue was built from.

The pull request description carries the same risk assessment, remediation summary, and proof from the console, so whoever reviews it in GitHub sees the evidence without leaving their workflow.

The Findings tab lists the raw scanner findings across the environment, the evidence that issues are built from.

The Scans tab shows the history of each scan with its status, origin, and request and completion times, and I use Configure scan frequency to change the cadence or Queue scan to run one on demand. The Configuration tab is where I manage the environment: the code and context sources it reads from, its code integrations, environment settings such as whether Continuum creates remediation patches, and the AWS collectors it uses for AWS context.

In a single workflow, I work through the validated issues in priority order with each scan, reviewing the evidence and scope behind each one before I approve and merge the fixes I trust. Every fix waits for my approval, so the backlog keeps shrinking while my team keeps control of what ships.
