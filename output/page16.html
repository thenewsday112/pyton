Xisco Munoz’s short time as Sheffield Wednesday boss was a dismal failure, but he’s won four trophies and started the 2026/27 season unbeaten with Malaysia’s Johor DT.

Sheffield Wednesday have been through a lot since Xisco Munoz was sacked after just 12 games in October 2023. The highs of Danny Rohl‘s tenure followed, but SWFC would plunge into administration in the 2025/26 season and are now looking to recover after relegation to League One.

Munoz’s short time in charge of the Owls is not fondly looked back on. He arrived as the replacement for the hugely popular Darren Moore after winning the League One play-off final, and his only win would come in a penalty shoot-out triumph over Stockport County in the Carabao Cup.

Photo: Arne Müseler / arne-mueseler.com / CC-BY-SA-2.0 (cropped).

Munoz has managed far and wide across his time in the dugout, and that has continued since his Wednesday stint. Currently, he serves as the boss of Malaysian side Johor DT, and he’s found serious success.

Xisco Munoz continues Johor DT’s huge domestic success after failed Sheffield Wednesday stint

Just over a month after leaving Sheffield Wednesday, Munoz would join Slovakian side DAC Dunajska Streda. He was in charge for 40 games, averaging 1.63 points per match before being sacked after a year.

In the summer of 2025, Munoz took on a new challenge in Malaysia as Johor DT boss. The club are the country’s biggest footballing force, and the Spaniard has helped maintain the status quo in impressive fashion.

In his first season, Munoz led JDT to the domestic quadruple, winning the Malaysian Super League, Charity Shield, Malaysian Cup and FA Cup. It marked the fourth season on the bounce they have achieved such a feat, and they have started the 2026/27 campaign in similar fashion. JDT have played 15 games across all competitions this season, winning 14 and drawing one.

In a bid to take their success to the continental stage, they have taken seven points from three games in their AFC Champions League group. Munoz may well be judged heavily on his success in this competition, as it is where they play the strongest level of opposition.

JDT are the leading force in Malaysian football by some distance, with their strong financial backing providing them with high-level facilities and the ability to attract players from overseas, unlike the vast majority of their rivals. It means Munoz’s success in domestic competition is expected, but his work in getting them to the last eight of the AFC Champions League last season deserves praise, as does their strong start in the same competition this time around.

The Verdict: Munoz’s recovery after Sheffield Wednesday nightmare won’t leave fans wondering what could have been

Munoz’s Malaysian adventure is certainly an exciting one. He leads a club dominant on the domestic stage and growing on the continental one, making it an intriguing and unique project.

However, such is the dominance of Johor DT in Malaysia that this isn’t the average case of a manager going from strength to strength after a tough spell. Sheffield Wednesday will not be looking at his quadruple-winning season and wondering what could have been if they had stuck it out with him in the dugout.

SWFC are on the lookout for a new boss, and they will be hoping for a hit like Rohl and not a big miss like Munoz with League One promotion at stake.

When looking back at the career of Guti, Real Madrid’s ‘Eternal Promise’, it’s hard not to think what could have been.

A visionary playmaker and occasional forward, the academy product could delight and frustrate in equal measure, his moments of genius almost but not quite atoning for his unfulfilled potential.

At least, that’s roughly how you’re supposed to discuss a player like Guti. Or the not-Arsenal Denilson. Or Antonio Cassano.

His natural talent was unrivalled, you’re supposed to say, but laziness and the allure of a celebrity lifestyle got in the way.

He might have been Real Madrid’s answer to Xavi, you continue, had he kept his head down, given 100 per cent in every match and spent a little more time on the training pitch instead of in front of the mirror.

Those 13 caps for Spain? Oh Guti, naive Guti! Had you been a little more professional, that might have been 113!

This is the common way to speak about players with ‘unfulfilled potential’ — those with bucketloads of talent who didn’t quite become all-time greats. But not only is it patronising, it’s also wide of the mark.

The good

First off, it’s worth remembering that Guti, a player once lambasted by his own club president for still being a ‘promise’ at the age of 31, won five La Liga titles and three Champions League trophies across 15 seasons at Real.

His was a career glittering with silverware.

Guti was part of several iconic Madrid line-ups, playing with the likes of Clarence Seedorf and Davor Šuker in the 90s, with Zidane, Brazilian Ronaldo and the other Galacticos in the early 2000s, then with the likes of Kaká and Cristiano Ronaldo in his final season.

He also played various roles within those teams. A midfielder at youth level, Guti briefly became a forward, netting an impressive 18 goals in 2000-01, before eventually switching back to midfield — partly in order to accommodate the striking talents of Ronaldo, Raúl and others.

From that deeper position, the Spaniard delivered his best performances, mastering the art of the assist.

Guti can also lay claim to a strange honour: to have completed not only the best backheeled assist in football but the second best as well. (Though it’s hard to say which is which.)

The first, in a 4-2 win over Sevilla in 2006, was a pinpoint through-ball, delivered from the centre of the pitch around 25 yards from goal. The spectacular flick fooled the entire Sevilla defence, finding Zinedine Zidane’s feet and allowing the Frenchman to slam home.

On this day in 2006, Guti decided to really take the piss with his assist for Zinedine Zidane in Real Madrid's 4-2 win over Sevilla…pic.twitter.com/FFG9o5CEHv

— Planet Football (@planetfutebol) January 15, 2020

The second came near the end of Guti’s Madrid career, in a match against Deportivo in January 2010.

Clean through on goal, Guti elected not to shoot but to backheel the ball away from goal to a grateful Karim Benzema. It was a moment of utter madness and sheer brilliance.

Marca dedicated its front page to the pass, its headline reading ‘The backheel of God’.

The bad

Those are the good bits, but Madrid fans will also talk of a player who went missing in big games, who was petulant and picked up cards seemingly out of boredom — a footballer who ultimately preferred making himself pretty to making himself play better.

It’s hardly an unfamiliar tale in the sport.

One of the most widely circulated stories about Guti’s less-than-impeccable attitude took place during Fabio Capello’s second spell as Madrid boss.

Capello, so the story goes, had instructed his players to wait in the centre circle at the start of the training session. Wanting to see whether the squad would follow orders, Capello turned up 15 minutes late…on purpose.

When he arrived, he found one player, Guti, sheltering from the Spanish sun in a dugout.

That love of leisure didn’t abate with age, either. During his final Madrid season, Guti may have been pulling off outrageous backheels, but he was already thinking of retiring to a beach in Thailand — a fact he gleefully admitted during a television interview.

And when it wasn’t about putting his feet up, it was seemingly about getting his hair right.

Former Real Madrid players Steve McManaman and Michael Owen recently agreed that Guti was the ‘vainest’ player they had ever played with. “He had a mirror right next to his space in the dressing room,” Owen said.

Unprofessionalism is a stick constantly used to beat Guti with. But even if these stories are only the tip of the iceberg, even if Guti was one of the laziest players in Real Madrid’s history, is that any reason to criticise him?

The lazy

There’s a certain moralism in football analysis when it comes to a player’s work ethic. Those who enjoy themselves in their spare time are deemed selfish playboys; those who stick around for an hour after training are worshipped as gods.

You only have to look at the current fawning over players like Jesse Lingard to see how this weird mindset pervades people’s thinking.

While many have (wrongly) criticised Lingard for his social media japes and choreographed celebrations, others have (perhaps also wrongly) applauded the winger for his professionalism: he makes the most of his abilities by giving 100 per cent in every match, they say, and he deserves a huge amount of credit for that.

I can understand why you might adopt this attitude when trying to motivate a child. But as a judgment on an adult’s life, isn’t it a little weird?

Cristiano Ronaldo received praise from Alex Ferguson for practising his horrible free-kick technique after training. And that makes sense: managers should encourage hard work among their own players in order to get results.

But does it make Ronaldo praiseworthy in any objective sense? God no. Go and make friends, you weirdo. You’re young. Work’s over.

With Guti, those so-called negative habits — the grooming, the fondness for celebrity culture, the love of leisure — are no more or less praiseworthy than spending time in a gym or doing a few extra keep-ups.

In fact, if you turned down a holiday with your friends, telling them you’d rather stay at the office to fulfil your potential as an account manager, they’d think you’d lost the plot. You would have lost the plot.

And so with football: yes, footballers can always dig a little deeper, raise their level a notch. But at some stage, after so many titles, you have to wonder whether that digging is really the best use of your time.

When he secured his place in Madrid’s first team, Guti had made it. Completely, utterly made it. Fulfilled the childhood dreams of a million Spaniards.

To wonder what could have been — to say that Guti wasted his potential by balancing football with his personal life — is a glass-half-empty way to look at an occasionally breathtaking career.

Review: The new Kindle Colorsoft is... fine.

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The best Kindle e-readers are essential for book lovers, and Amazon just released a new Kindle Colorsoft (and the Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition with an aluminum body), along with a new basic Kindle, Kindle Paperwhite/Paperwhite Signature Edition, and the Kindle Click page-turner. Following last year's refresh of the Kindle Scribe lineup, that means Amazon has an all-new Kindle family, just in time for the holidays.

I've been reviewing the Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition for Mashable for the past few weeks. Except for the new aluminum body, fig colorway, and higher storage, it's functionally the same as the base Colorsoft model, so we won't be reviewing them separately.

If you've been curious about Amazon's color e-readers, then I'd recommend reading my Kindle Colorsoft review first. Unfortunately, while there's a lot that I do love about this device, it doesn't fix the biggest problem — the color is still very disappointing.

That cover is definitely yellow-ish. Credit: Timothy Werth / Mashable

Kindle Colorsoft review: Manage your expectations when it comes to color

Now, I want to be clear about one thing: Amazon uses E Ink technology for its e-readers. This distinctive, paper-like digital display is a product of the E Ink Corporation. And the truth is that E Ink just doesn't do color very well. So, the technology is what it is.

The E Ink color experience on the Kindle Colorsoft is disappointing. That was true with the original model from 2024, and it hasn't changed with the 2026 model. What has changed is that the Colorsoft now offers true edge-to-edge color, so there are no dimming or lighting issues around the edges.

But Amazon promises readers something more: "Paper-like color perfect for comics, graphic novels, and more." And that's not what I'm seeing. In fact, some of Amazon's official photography with the Kindle Colorsoft appears to exaggerate the color saturation, at least to my eye. I've never seen color quite that bright in practice.

I haven't seen colors quite this bright on my review unit, sadly. Credit: Amazon

I put the Kindle side-by-side with illustrated books, comic books, and graphic novels I have on my bookshelf, and the difference is very obvious, even when you crank up the vividness and brightness to the limit. There's just no comparison, really.

Even in comics with more neutral tones and softer colors like the classic Batman comic The Long Halloween, the difference stands out clearly. How important is the color problem? Well, the word "color" is right there in the name — Kindle Colorsoft. Though, I suppose the emphasis should be on "soft."

The color difference is obvious. Credit: Timothy Werth / Mashable What elder millennial nerd doesn't own a copy of 'Watchmen'? Credit: Timothy Werth / Mashable

I have seen more vibrant and colorful electronic paper technology. Samsung's ePaper does a better job with color, though there are other trade-offs. It takes time for the screen to change to a new image, which isn't an option in an e-reader that demands near-instant page turns.

In my photos of Samsung's ePaper, you'll notice brighter colors, but there's a reason Samsung reserves this tech for commercial use.

Samsung's ePaper commercial display technology on display. Credit: Timothy Werth / Mashable A closer look at Samsung's ePaper commercial display technology. Credit: Timothy Werth / Mashable

If you want to read comics, magazines, or illustrated books in digital form, a tablet like the new iPad Mini or Amazon Alexa tablets would be a better option. (Mashable is in the process of reviewing both of those devices.)

And you can always go to an actual bookstore.

Amazon Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition: What I liked

To be clear, there is a lot to love about the Kindle Colorsoft, particularly the Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition. I love the fig color, which is a close match for the new burgundy iPhone 18 Pro. (Fun fact: An Amazon spokesperson told Mashable the fig color was inspired by the same industry trend report that inspired the burgundy iPhone.)

The new aluminum body available in the Paperwhite SE and Colorsoft SE is worth the upgrade. They have a similar feel to the Apple iPad Air, which I also loved. It's also satisfying to see the cover screen artwork and your book covers in color.

Credit: Timothy Werth / Mashable Credit: Timothy Werth / Mashable

I also appreciated the ability to zoom in on comic panels and illustrations, which is a great way to appreciate fine details in the artwork.

Credit: Timothy Werth / Mashable ...enhance! Credit: Timothy Werth / Mashable

You can double-click to read comics panel-by-panel on the Colorsoft, rather than page-by-page. Often, this works really well, but for artists who use complicated panels of varying sizes and styles (like you'll find in The Long Halloween), some of the panels fit very awkwardly on the screen.

The new Kindle Colorsoft also has a lot of the features we loved in other Kindle devices — a waterproof design, a battery that lasts for weeks, auto-adjusting light. Amazon also moved the power button to the side, which is a smart update. Only the Signature Edition supports wireless charging, and it now has magnetic pogo pins on the back. That means if you're used to using Qi wireless charging accessories with your Kindle, they won't work here. For me, this isn't a problem.

To sum up — the Kindle Colorsoft isn't a bad color e-reader. It just suffers from the same problems all color e-readers do.

The Kindle Colorsoft: Who it's for

If you believe some color is better than no color, or if you're a note-taker who loves to highlight their books, then the Kindle Colorsoft would be a smart upgrade from the basic Kindle. (Pro tip: We named the Kindle Scribe Colorsoft a Mashable Choice product, and the ability to write on the screen lets you use color in much better ways.) However, the Kobo Libra Colour is retaining its top spot as our favorite color e-reader.

The Kindle Colorsoft would also be a smart option for kids in the process of graduating from illustrated kids' books to chapter books. The Colorsoft could help make this transition a bit more inviting, and parents can also get a Kindle Colorsoft Kids Edition for $299.99.

More 2026 Kindle updates

As of May 20, 2026, Amazon no longer lend software updates or provide web services to devices released in 2012 or earlier, essentially bricking them. Users will still be able to read currently downloaded books on the device, but to add new books, they will need to connect the device to a computer. This is a huge hit, especially to Libby users who need web service access to read borrowed books.

The affected Kindles include:

  • Kindle DX Graphite

  • Kindle Keyboard

Owners of these Kindle models are obviously upset, as it means they'll need to purchase a new device to maintain access to all features. Which Kindle is right for you?

If you're concerned about buying the newest tech, that's not an issue since, as of October 2026, there are nine new models to shop.

The latest Kindles all boast a maximum brightness of 94 nits and a 300 ppi, high-resolution display with zero glare, so you can enjoy sharp text and images at any viewing angle. How to get free e-books on Kindle

With just a click, transfer your Libby e-book to your Kindle device. Credit: Samantha Mangino / Mashable Your Libby ebook will automatically populate in your Kindle library. Credit: Samantha Mangino / Mashable

The best way to read free e-books on a Kindle is with Libby. Libby allows you to borrow e-books through libraries, and all you need is a library card. Through the Libby app, borrow books and then select the "Read with Kindle" option, which allows you to send these e-books to your Kindle library.

Another option for finding free e-books is through Stuff Your Kindle events. These are author-driven events that offer limited-time deals on e-books, often making them heavily discounted or totally free.
